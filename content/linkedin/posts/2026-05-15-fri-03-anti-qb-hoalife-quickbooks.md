HOALife relies on QuickBooks for accounting. The fund separation problem follows the integration.

HOALife is priced at about $45-$95/month. The platform uses QuickBooks for its accounting layer. That means the compliance problem of QuickBooks, specifically the absence of enforced fund separation, is baked into the HOALife workflow.

An integration with QuickBooks does not fix the architectural limitation. QuickBooks still uses a single general ledger. The integration passes data to and from that ledger. The data structure does not change because it is accessed through HOALife's interface.

For communities in Florida, California, Maryland, Washington, and New Jersey, the statutory fund separation requirement does not make exceptions for platforms that integrate with QuickBooks. The requirement is that operating and reserve funds be legally separated. An integration with a single-ledger tool does not satisfy that requirement.

The other concern with relying on a QuickBooks integration is data reconciliation. When the same financial data lives in two systems that sync periodically, discrepancies occur. Those discrepancies require manual investigation and can obscure the fund position that the board is relying on for compliance decisions.

Purpose-built HOA accounting with enforced fund separation is a different category of tool than a management platform that integrates with QuickBooks.
