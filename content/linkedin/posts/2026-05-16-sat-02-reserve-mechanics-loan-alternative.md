When reserves run out, boards have two options: special assessment or loan. Both are more expensive than funding correctly.

A special assessment is an immediate charge to every homeowner above and beyond regular dues. For a $200,000 roof replacement in a 100-unit community, that is $2,000 per unit, due when the board says so. Homeowners who cannot pay create collection problems. Homeowners who can pay are angry.

An HOA loan (line of credit or community loan) is available from some lenders to cover major capital expenditures. The board borrows the money, pays for the work, and then increases monthly dues to repay the loan over time. The cost of borrowing adds to the total cost of the project.

Either outcome costs more than funding the reserves adequately in the first place. The special assessment or the loan repayment schedule will typically exceed what the additional monthly reserve contribution would have been.

The reserve study calculates exactly what monthly contribution avoids both outcomes. That is the number the board should be using when it sets the annual budget.

Choosing a lower contribution to keep dues down is a decision to defer the cost, not eliminate it. The deferred cost earns no interest and often grows.
