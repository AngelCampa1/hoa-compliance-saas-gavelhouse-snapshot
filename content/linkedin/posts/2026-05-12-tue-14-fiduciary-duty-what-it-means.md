Fiduciary duty is not a concept. It is the legal standard courts use to evaluate board decisions.

When a homeowner sues a board member personally for mismanaging reserve funds, the court asks whether the board member acted with the care and prudence a reasonably careful person would use in similar circumstances.

Approving a budget that comingles reserve and operating funds, when state law explicitly prohibits commingling, fails that standard. A board member who knew the statute and approved the budget anyway has a harder time defending themselves than one who had no knowledge.

The D&O insurance question is: did the policy cover the specific conduct at issue? Many D&O policies exclude intentional violations of statute. Others have sub-limits for financial mismanagement claims. Boards that assume their D&O policy makes them immune from personal exposure in all circumstances are often wrong.

The practical protection is clean financials and a documented reserve program. A board that can show it commissioned a reserve study, maintained separate accounts, and followed the funding plan has a strong defense.
