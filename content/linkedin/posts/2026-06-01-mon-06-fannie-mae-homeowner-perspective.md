This is a scenario that will play out in a lot of communities after January 4, 2027.

A homeowner lists their unit. A buyer makes an offer. The buyer's lender runs the Full Review. The association is allocating 8% of its budget to reserves, not the 15% Fannie Mae now requires. The lender cannot sell the loan to Fannie Mae. The deal falls apart or the buyer pays a significantly higher rate on a non-conforming loan.

The homeowner had no vote in this outcome. The board made the reserve allocation decision years ago, maybe several boards ago. The goal at the time was to keep assessments low. That is a reasonable impulse. Volunteer boards serve homeowners, and lower assessments feel like a service.

But the reserve fund allocation is more than a financial management decision. It is a property liquidity decision for every homeowner who will ever want to sell.

A buyer who cannot get conventional financing either walks away or demands a price reduction that accounts for financing friction. Either way, the underfunded reserve calculation lands on the seller's balance sheet, not the board's.

The board members who voted to keep allocations low probably had no idea this was the consequence. Most of them were not informed that Fannie Mae eligibility depended on their budget line item.

This is the kind of downstream effect that compliance-first financial management is supposed to prevent.

What do you think the board's disclosure obligation to homeowners is in this situation?
