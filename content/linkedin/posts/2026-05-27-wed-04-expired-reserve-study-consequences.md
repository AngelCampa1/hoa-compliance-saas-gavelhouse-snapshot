A reserve study expires when the required update cycle passes without a new study.

Colorado: five years. Delaware: five years. Nevada: five years. Virginia: five years. Oregon: three to five years. California: three years. Washington: three years. Hawaii: required with 30-year horizon.

When the study expires, several things happen simultaneously.

The funding plan in the study is no longer current. Component remaining useful lives are all shorter by the number of years since the study. Replacement cost estimates are all lower than actual current costs. The percent-funded calculation from the expired study does not reflect the current funded status.

If your state requires an annual percent-funded disclosure, as California does, and you are disclosing from an expired study, you may be making a materially inaccurate disclosure.

The fiduciary duty obligation requires board members to act on adequate information. An expired reserve study is not adequate information for a major financial decision about contribution rates or capital projects.

Boards operating from expired studies should understand that the protection of the business judgment rule is harder to claim when the information the board relied on was known to be stale at the time of the decision.

Updating the study is an investment in defensibility, not just compliance.
