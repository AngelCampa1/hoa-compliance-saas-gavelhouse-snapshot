A special assessment is not a financial emergency. It is the predictable result of skipping reserve contributions for years.

The roof doesn't suddenly fail without warning. The pavement doesn't crack overnight. The pool equipment doesn't die unexpectedly. The reserve study estimated these replacements years in advance. The board chose not to fund them adequately, or wasn't told to, and now the bill is due.

The sequence is almost always the same:

1. Reserve contributions are set below the study's recommendation, often to avoid a dues increase.
2. The reserve fund grows, but not fast enough.
3. A major component reaches the end of its useful life.
4. The fund doesn't cover the replacement cost.
5. The board issues a special assessment.
6. Owners are angry. Some can't pay. The collection process becomes contentious.

The hard truth is that a $300 per-unit special assessment is almost always the deferred accumulation of $15 per month that wasn't collected over five years.

Reserve contributions are not optional savings. They're the mechanism for distributing the cost of long-term assets across the people who benefit from them over time.

When the mechanism is skipped, someone eventually pays the full price all at once. That someone is whoever happens to own a unit the year the component fails.
