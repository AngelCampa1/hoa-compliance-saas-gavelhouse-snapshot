If your HOA software requires a QuickBooks subscription to do accounting, you're paying for two tools that together still don't solve the compliance problem.

HOA platforms that rely on QuickBooks integrations pass the accounting responsibility to a tool that has no knowledge of HOA compliance requirements. QuickBooks doesn't know:

- That California requires annual percent-funded disclosures
- That Florida prohibits reserve waivers for structural components
- That New Jersey's reserve balance can never drop below zero
- That Fannie Mae's 2027 rule requires 15% allocation

QuickBooks is accounting software. It tracks money in and money out. It doesn't enforce HOA-specific fund separation requirements, doesn't generate reserve study reports, doesn't flag when a reserve balance drops below a statutory minimum.

An HOA platform with a QuickBooks integration is two subscriptions where you still have to manage compliance manually.

What does that cost? QuickBooks Simple Start is $35/month. Add a QuickBooks-dependent HOA platform on top of that and you're often paying more than Gavelhouse's flat rate while having less reserve compliance coverage.

This is not about QuickBooks being bad software. It's about using the right tool for the specific compliance requirements that HOA boards face.
