A handful of states have explicit statutory rules for reserve and operating funds beyond general fiduciary duty:

California: Civil Code §5510 requires reserve funds to be segregated; the Davis-Stirling framework imposes $100 to $500/day fines for non-compliance.

Florida: FL Stat. §718.111(14) requires separate accounting of condo reserves and operating funds (joint investment is permitted only with separate accounting). §720.303 imposes the same separate-accounting duty on HOAs. The DBPR Division of Condominiums can fine condo and co-op boards up to $5,000 per violation under §718.501(1)(d)(6) and Florida Admin Code Chapter 61B-21.

Maryland: HB 292 (2025) requires mandatory reserve funding per the most recent reserve study under Md. Real Prop. §11B-112.2. Enforced via the Maryland AG Consumer Protection Division.

New Jersey: N.J.S.A. 46:8B requires separate accounting; recent legislation (S2760 / S3992) added baseline funding rules (pending independent verification of specific provisions).

Washington: RCW 64.34.382 / 64.90 require reserve studies and separate reserve accounts for condos.

In every other state, general fiduciary duty principles prohibit a board from misappropriating reserve funds for operating expenses. The absence of a specific commingling statute does not create permission to mix accounts.

For boards using accounting software that does not enforce separation, the risk is not just accidental transfers. It is the absence of any system-level barrier to commingling over time through poor record-keeping.

Gavelhouse enforces the operating/reserve separation at the database layer. The accounts cannot be mingled because the system does not permit it, regardless of which state the association is in.
