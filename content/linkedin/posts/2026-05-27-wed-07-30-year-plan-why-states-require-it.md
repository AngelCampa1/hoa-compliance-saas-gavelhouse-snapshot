A 10-year reserve plan looks fully funded for components that will fail in year 12.

A board reviewing a 10-year plan that shows adequate funding for the next decade may not realize that the largest replacement obligations, a parking garage ramp or a building envelope or a central HVAC system, sit just outside the plan's window.

A 30-year horizon forces the funding plan to capture the second and sometimes third replacement cycle for mid-life components. It prevents boards from declaring a short-term surplus while building a long-term shortfall.

California and Washington require the 30-year plan by statute. The discipline is the same reason pension funds use long-range actuarial projections rather than 5-year snapshots.

The compounding math matters. A component with a $200,000 replacement cost in year 25, funded over 30 years, requires roughly $6,700 per year in contribution. Funded over 5 years (if the plan only showed the last 5 years of the funding window), it requires $40,000 per year. A 10-year plan that excludes year 25 leaves that obligation invisible until year 20, when the contribution requirement becomes severe.

Boards in states without a 30-year requirement should consider adopting one voluntarily. The planning discipline is the same regardless of what the statute requires.
