New communities have the most important reserve study problem: the developer's numbers may not be accurate.

When a developer hands over control of an HOA or condo association to the homeowner board, the transition documents typically include a reserve study commissioned by the developer. That study was prepared to satisfy state requirements at the time of handover.

Developer-commissioned reserve studies have a known limitation: they were paid for by the entity handing over control. The incentive structure does not always produce conservative, realistic component cost estimates.

An independent reserve study commissioned by the incoming homeowner-controlled board often produces different numbers than the developer's study, sometimes significantly different.

For new communities in mandate states like California, Florida, and Washington, the incoming board has a legal obligation to maintain an adequate reserve program. If the program inherited from the developer is underfunded, the obligation to correct it belongs to the current board.

Requesting an independent reserve study within the first year of homeowner board control is the most important financial governance action a new board can take. The study either confirms the developer's numbers or identifies the gap that needs to be addressed before the first major replacement event.

The earlier the gap is found, the more time the board has to close it through regular assessments rather than a special assessment.
