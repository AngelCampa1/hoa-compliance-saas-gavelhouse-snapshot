Commingling means mixing reserve and operating funds. It is prohibited by statute in most states. Here is what it looks like in practice.

The most common commingling pattern is a single bank account where all HOA dues go in, all expenses come out, and the board tracks reserves on a spreadsheet. The account is one. The bank does not distinguish between operating deposits and reserve contributions.

When state law says "separate reserve accounts," it means a separate bank account, not a separate spreadsheet column. Florida §720.303, California §5510, Maryland §11B-112.2, New Jersey §46:8B-19, and Washington RCW 64.34.364 all use the language of separate accounts.

The subtler commingling pattern happens in QuickBooks. The board opens separate bank accounts but uses QuickBooks to manage both, and a transaction gets coded to the wrong account. The bank account is technically separate but a QuickBooks error moves reserve money to cover an operating shortfall. That is also commingling.

The cleanest architecture: separate bank accounts for operating and reserve funds, and accounting software that enforces which transactions can touch which account.

The violation is the mixing, regardless of how it happened. "My bookkeeper made an error in QuickBooks" is an explanation, not a defense.
