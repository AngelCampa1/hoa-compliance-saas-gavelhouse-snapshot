Why does a reserve study need a 30-year horizon? Most board members serve three years. Nobody is thinking 30 years out.

That's exactly the problem the 30-year plan is designed to solve.

A roof lasts 20 to 30 years. Pool decking lasts 15. Pavement lasts 20 to 25. These are not expenses that show up predictably year to year. They show up once, at a large cost, and then disappear for another two decades.

If you only plan for the next five years, you see no major expenses coming. The reserve fund looks fine. The board that inherited the community 20 years from now will look back at your funding decisions and find a $400,000 shortfall.

The 30-year plan solves this by:

Spreading the cost over time. Every homeowner pays a small amount each month toward a future replacement, instead of a large special assessment when the roof finally fails.

Showing cumulative risk. A 5-year view might show $40,000 in upcoming costs. A 30-year view might show $800,000 across all components. That's the real liability the board is managing.

Washington state requires a 30-year funding plan in every reserve study under RCW 64.34.382. Other states don't mandate it yet, but the logic is sound regardless of where you are.

The purpose of reserves is to smooth out large irregular expenses across the people who benefit from the asset over its life. The 30-year plan is how you do that math.
