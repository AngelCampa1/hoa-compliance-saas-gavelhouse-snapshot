The investment return assumption in your reserve study affects how much you need to collect each month. Most boards use the default without reviewing it.

A reserve study that assumes a 2% annual return on reserve funds calculates a higher required monthly contribution than one that assumes a 4% return, because you need to save more to compensate for lower investment income.

When interest rates change significantly, reserve studies that were calibrated to a different rate environment can produce contributions that are either too high (and cause homeowner pushback) or too low (and create a funding shortfall).

The practical approach: when your board receives the reserve study, ask the preparer what investment return assumption was used and whether it reflects current conditions for the types of accounts your community typically uses (money market, CD ladders, etc.).

The same logic applies to inflation assumptions. A study prepared in a low-inflation environment will project lower replacement costs than today's actual cost structure. Both the return assumption and the inflation assumption should be reviewed when the study is updated.

California's three-year study cycle and Washington's three-year cycle exist partly to keep these assumptions current. A study that is more than three years old is almost certainly using outdated rate assumptions in addition to outdated replacement costs.
