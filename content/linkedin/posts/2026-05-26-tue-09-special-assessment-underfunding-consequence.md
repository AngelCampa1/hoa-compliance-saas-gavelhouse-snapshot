A special assessment is not a surprise. It is the end of a predictable chain.

The chain starts with a reserve study that shows an underfunded status. The board reviews the study and decides to defer the contribution increase. The next year, the same decision. The year after, the same decision.

The roof reaches the end of its useful life. The assessment is then divided by the number of units and billed to homeowners who may have bought into the community after the underfunding decision was made.

Special assessments are expensive in multiple ways. The direct cost can run thousands of dollars per unit for a single major component failure. Homeowners who cannot afford the assessment may default on it. The board faces the option of liening those homes and forcing outcomes no volunteer board signed up to manage.

The indirect cost is the effect on home values. Communities with a history of special assessments signal to buyers that the reserve fund management has been inadequate. That signal suppresses prices and reduces market activity.

Underfunding reserves is a board decision that transfers cost and risk to future homeowners. The volunteer board members who made that decision may have moved on. The homeowners who pay the assessment will not.
