There are two categories of state reserve law: mandate states and disclosure states. The obligations are completely different.

Mandate states require HOAs and condo associations to commission a formal reserve study, fund it according to the plan, and, in some cases, prohibit reducing funding below a threshold. California, Florida, Maryland, Washington, Colorado, and Nevada are mandate states.

Disclosure states require boards to report reserve fund information in offering documents and annual reports, but do not mandate a study or specific funding level. New Jersey, New York, Illinois, Pennsylvania, and Connecticut are disclosure states (though New Jersey's zero-floor rule makes it a hybrid).

For a board in a mandate state, non-compliance is a current violation with an active penalty structure. California's daily fine, Florida's $5,000 per violation, Maryland's $10,000 are all real, enforceable penalties.

For a board in a disclosure state, the consequences of underfunding are less about statutory penalties and more about: homeowner challenges, Fannie Mae eligibility after January 4, 2027, property value impacts, and potential future legislation closing the gap (see Connecticut SB 212, Illinois HB 2563).

Disclosure-state boards sometimes use their status as a reason to defer reserve planning. That reasoning stops working in January 2027 when the Fannie Mae floor applies regardless of state law.
