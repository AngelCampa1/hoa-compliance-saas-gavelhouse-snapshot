A special assessment caused by reserve underfunding is a board governance failure, not just a financial event.

When a community issues a large special assessment because the reserve account does not have enough money to cover a required replacement, the question homeowners ask is: why did the board not plan for this?

That question has a governance answer. The board had the fiduciary duty to maintain adequate reserves. Reserve studies exist to project replacement costs. Annual funding is set to accumulate the required amount. When the reserve account falls short of a predictable replacement cost, the planning process failed.

Homeowners in several states can challenge a board's reserve decisions. In California, the annual percent-funded disclosure creates a public record of the reserve position over time. A board that disclosed 18% funded for five years, then issued a $2,000 per-unit special assessment, has a documented trail showing the underfunding was known and not corrected.

The liability exposure for board members in that scenario is real. The fiduciary duty standard asks what a reasonably careful person would have done. Repeatedly disclosing an underfunded reserve without taking corrective action is a difficult position to defend.
