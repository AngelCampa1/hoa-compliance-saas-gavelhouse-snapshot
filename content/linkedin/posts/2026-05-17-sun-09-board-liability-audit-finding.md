Receiving an audit finding is not the worst outcome. Receiving an audit finding and ignoring it is.

An audit finding of commingled reserve and operating funds creates a documented record that the violation existed, the board was informed, and the clock started. What the board does next determines the liability trajectory.

A board that receives a finding and immediately takes corrective action: opens a separate reserve account, restructures the accounting, and documents the corrective steps in meeting minutes, has demonstrated responsive fiduciary conduct. The finding still exists. The response is defensible.

A board that receives a finding and takes no corrective action, or explicitly declines to correct the problem, has created a different record. In that scenario, the finding becomes evidence of ongoing knowing non-compliance, not merely a past mistake.

The penalty structures in Florida ($5,000), California ($100-$500/day), and Maryland ($10,000) are all more likely to be applied in the knowing non-compliance scenario than in the responsive correction scenario.

The audit finding is information. The board's response to that information is what defines the liability exposure from that point on.

Clean financials before the audit is the optimal outcome. Clean financials after the audit, if the finding was addressed promptly, is the second-best outcome.
