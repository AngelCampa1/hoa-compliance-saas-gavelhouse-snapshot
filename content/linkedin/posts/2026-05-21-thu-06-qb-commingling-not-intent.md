Commingling violations don't require intent. They require a combined account. QuickBooks makes this easy to do accidentally.

Here's the scenario:

A prior treasurer set up QuickBooks with one checking account. Operating income goes in. Reserve contributions go in. Both get deposited to the same bank account. The treasurer uses QuickBooks categories to track which dollars are "for reserves."

This is not fund separation. The funds are commingled at the banking level. The categories in QuickBooks are labels. The statutory requirement in California, Florida, Maryland, New Jersey, and Washington is structural separation.

The incoming treasurer looks at the QuickBooks setup, sees separate budget categories, concludes the funds are handled correctly, and continues the same practice.

No one intended to commingle funds. The software structure made commingling the default.

When an auditor looks at the bank statements, there is one account. The commingling is evident regardless of what the QuickBooks categories show.

The penalty is not calculated based on whether the board knew they were in violation. California's $100 to $500 daily fine under §5510 runs from the date of the violation.

Structural fund separation means separate bank accounts. It means software that enforces the separation before transactions are recorded. It means a system where commingling by accident isn't possible.
