A homeowner can sue the board over reserve fund mismanagement. That is not a theoretical risk.

When a special assessment arrives and homeowners are looking at a $15,000 bill, the first question is: why weren't reserves adequate to cover this?

If the board failed to fund reserves at the level their own reserve study recommended, the answer is uncomfortable. If the board failed to commission a reserve study at all, it's worse. If the board commingled operating and reserve funds in a state that prohibits it, there's a statutory violation in the record.

Homeowners have standing to sue for breach of fiduciary duty. The standard in most jurisdictions is the business judgment rule: did the board act in good faith, with reasonable diligence, in the community's best interest?

"We ran the board on spreadsheets and didn't know about the reserve study requirement" doesn't satisfy that standard in states where the requirement is statutory.

Board members serving as volunteers did not sign up to be defendants. But the legal exposure is real and it follows from the decisions made during their term, not from their volunteer status.

The documentation that protects you is the same documentation that proves you did your job: funded reserves, current reserve study, clean separated accounts, accurate financial reports.
