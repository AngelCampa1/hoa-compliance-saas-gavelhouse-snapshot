QuickBooks is designed for business accounting. HOA reserve fund accounting has requirements that business accounting doesn't recognize.

In business accounting, the goal is accurate financial records: income, expenses, assets, liabilities. QuickBooks does this well.

In HOA reserve fund accounting, there are additional statutory requirements layered on top:

The reserve fund must be structurally separate from operating funds. Not categorically separate. Structurally separate, meaning the money cannot flow between funds without a documented board authorization. State law sets minimums for how much must be in reserves. The reserve study determines what the minimums should be based on component lifespans. The percent-funded calculation determines whether the current balance meets the reserve study recommendation. That percent-funded number must be disclosed to homeowners annually in states like California.

QuickBooks tracks money. It does not know about reserve studies, percent-funded calculations, state-specific disclosure requirements, or the difference between a categorical label and structural fund separation.

A treasurer who uses QuickBooks for HOA accounting and believes they're covered for reserve compliance has a gap between the tool's capabilities and the board's legal obligations.

Closing that gap requires either expertise or purpose-built software. Most volunteer boards have neither. That's the problem we're solving.
