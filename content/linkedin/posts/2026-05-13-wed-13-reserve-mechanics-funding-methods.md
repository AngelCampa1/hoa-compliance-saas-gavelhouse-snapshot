There are three reserve funding methods. Only one is fully funded. Most boards use the wrong one.

The threshold method sets a minimum balance and funds just enough to stay above it. This is the most common approach for self-managed boards because it minimizes current assessments. The problem is that a threshold balance does not necessarily cover actual replacement costs when they arrive.

The baseline method funds enough to prevent the balance from going negative. New Jersey statute S2760 (2024) actually mandates this as a floor. But baseline funding is the minimum, not an adequate reserve program.

The fully funded method calculates the current reserve balance as a percentage of the fully funded balance (what you should have saved at this point in every component's life cycle) and funds toward 100%. A fully funded community at 70% or above has strong financial health.

California's percent-funded disclosure under §5550 is specifically measuring progress toward the fully funded target. A community disclosing 30% funded is using the language of the fully funded method while being far from it.

The Fannie Mae 15% floor references reserve allocation as a percentage of the budget, which is a different metric than percent funded, but both ultimately measure whether reserves are adequate.

Understanding which method your board uses is the first step to understanding your reserve position.
