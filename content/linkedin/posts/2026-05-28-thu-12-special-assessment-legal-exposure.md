When an HOA board levies a special assessment, it is exercising significant authority over every homeowner's finances. That authority comes with procedural requirements that vary by state but share a common thread: the process must be documented.

The documentation that protects the board includes:

The reserve study or engineering report showing the need for the funds. A board that levies a special assessment without professional support for the cost estimate is exposed to challenges from homeowners who dispute the amount.

The board resolution authorizing the assessment. Verbal decisions at board meetings are not enough. The resolution must be formally adopted and recorded.

The notice to homeowners. Most state statutes and governing documents require advance notice of a special assessment, specifying the amount, the purpose, and the payment timeline.

The calculation methodology. How was the per-unit amount determined? Equal shares? Square footage? The method must match the governing documents.

If any of these elements is missing, a homeowner who objects to paying the assessment has procedural grounds to challenge it, independent of whether the assessment itself was justified.

A special assessment that is legitimate in substance but deficient in procedure can be enjoined, forcing the board to redo the process. That delay may leave the capital project unfunded while the dispute works through the courts.

Process is not bureaucracy here. It is protection.
