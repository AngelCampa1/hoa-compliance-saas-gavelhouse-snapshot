# HOA Owner Voting Rights: What Owners Decide vs What Boards Decide

We built Gavelhouse from the builder side of HOA governance — not as attorneys. But voting procedure is one of the areas where well-intentioned boards stumble into the most expensive mistakes. A board that imposes a $1,200-per-unit special assessment without the required member vote can have the assessment voided, refund collected funds, and face personal claims from the directors who approved it.

This guide separates board authority from owner authority and walks through the procedural rules that keep votes valid.

## The general rule

Boards have day-to-day operational authority. Owners retain authority over fundamental governance changes and significant financial impositions. The exact line is drawn by your CC&Rs, bylaws, and state statute, in that order — but the categories are consistent across most associations.

### Decisions owners typically vote on

- **Amendments to CC&Rs and bylaws.** Almost always requires supermajority (often 67% or 75%) of total voting power.
- **Special assessments above a threshold.** Most CC&Rs set a threshold (commonly 5% of annual budget, sometimes a flat dollar amount) above which member approval is required.
- **Election and recall of directors.** Owners elect directors and, in most states, can recall them under statutory procedures.
- **Material changes to common area use.** Selling, encumbering, or fundamentally repurposing common areas typically requires owner approval.
- **Approval of large capital improvements.** Often required when the project is funded by special assessment.

### Decisions boards typically make

- **Operating budgets** within authorized levels.
- **Vendor selection and contracts.**
- **Routine maintenance and reserve expenditures.**
- **Adoption of rules and regulations** (in most states, subject to notice and reasonableness limits).
- **Enforcement actions** including violation notices, fines, and lien filings.
- **Hiring and firing of management companies.**

## Quorum — the single most-litigated voting issue

A quorum is the minimum voting power required to conduct valid business. CC&Rs commonly require 25-50% of total voting power, present in person, by ballot, or by proxy.

Without quorum:

- Votes taken are voidable
- Decisions can be challenged for years afterward
- Adjourned meetings often have reduced quorum requirements (often 25%)

Always confirm quorum at the start of the meeting and document the count in minutes. A common mistake: counting attendees rather than voting power. Some communities have weighted voting (per unit, per square foot, etc.) and quorum must reflect the same weighting.

## Proxies and electronic voting

### Proxies

A proxy is a written authorization from one owner to another to vote on their behalf. Common rules:

- Must be in writing and dated
- Revocable unless coupled with an interest
- Some states limit how many proxies one person can hold
- Most CC&Rs require proxies be filed before the meeting

Defective proxies — undated, illegible, missing signature, exceeding the holder's limit — are a leading cause of overturned elections.

### Electronic voting

Several states permit electronic voting if the association formally adopts a procedure consistent with statutory requirements:

- **California** (Civil Code 5105, 5125): permits electronic voting with specific opt-in, secrecy, and authentication requirements
- **Florida** (Statute 718.128 for condos, 720.317 for HOAs): permits electronic voting with formal adoption resolution and platform requirements
- **Nevada** (NRS 116.31105): permits electronic voting for many association decisions

Electronic voting without formal adoption is invalid even if the platform itself is secure. The procedural step matters more than the technology.

## Recall procedures

Most states permit owners to recall directors before the end of their term. Common procedure:

1. Petition signed by a statutory percentage of owners (often 10-25%)
2. Notice of special meeting
3. Vote at the meeting (often majority, sometimes supermajority)
4. Removal effective immediately or per governing documents

Recall procedures are unforgiving on documentation. Petitions that lack proper signatures, dates, or unit identification are routinely thrown out. Boards facing recall should respond procedurally, not emotionally.

## The special assessment trap

This is where directors most commonly create personal liability.

Most CC&Rs allow the board to impose minor special assessments by board action alone — often up to 5% of annual budget. Above that threshold, member approval is required.

Boards that impose larger assessments by board action alone face:

- Assessment voided in court
- Required refund of collected funds
- Personal liability for directors who voted yes (business judgment rule does not protect knowing violations of governing documents)
- Insurance coverage denial in some cases

The fix is simple: read the threshold, schedule the vote, follow the notice procedure, document the result. The vote is rarely the obstacle homeowners think it is — most assessments pass when the board explains the need clearly.

## State-specific variations

Voting procedure varies meaningfully by state. A few highlights:

- **California:** Director elections require an inspector of elections in larger associations and have detailed secret ballot rules under Civil Code 5100-5145.
- **Florida:** HOA voting is governed by Statute 720, with specific notice, ballot, and tabulation requirements.
- **Nevada:** NRS 116 has prescriptive election and amendment rules, including specific ballot retention periods.
- **Texas:** Property Code Chapter 209 governs HOA voting; condos follow Chapter 82.

If you operate in multiple states or are unsure, consult an association attorney before any contested vote.

## Documenting the vote

Every vote belongs in the permanent record:

- Notice of meeting (with proof of delivery)
- Quorum confirmation
- Proxies received (kept for the statutory retention period)
- Ballot tabulation
- Inspector of elections report (where required)
- Final result in minutes

A board that cannot produce these documents when challenged usually loses, regardless of the underlying merits.

## The compliance lens

Voting rights exist because HOAs are governance structures, not corporations of convenience. Owners pay assessments, accept restrictions on their property, and elect representatives — and in exchange they retain authority over the most significant decisions.

Boards that respect that authority, follow the procedure, and document the result rarely face voting challenges. Boards that treat the procedure as a formality face the most expensive litigation in HOA practice.

When in doubt, hold the vote. The cost of the meeting is always lower than the cost of the lawsuit.
