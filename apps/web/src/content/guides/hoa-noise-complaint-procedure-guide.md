steps:
  - title: Require a written complaint, not a phone call
    content: >-
      Phone complaints disappear into board members'' memories and create
      inconsistent records. Adopt a written complaint form (paper or web) that
      captures date, time, duration, description of the noise, location of the
      complainant relative to the source, and the complainant''s name and unit
      number. Anonymous complaints should be logged but not acted on alone — a
      named complainant is required to support a hearing under most due-process
      provisions in state HOA acts. Keep every complaint, including those that
      do not lead to enforcement, for at least the statute of limitations on
      discrimination claims (two years in most states).
  - title: Use a two-source rule before issuing notice
    content: >-
      Before sending any violation notice, require either two independent
      complaints from different units or one complaint plus a board-witnessed
      observation. Single-source complaints often turn out to be neighbor
      disputes where the board is being weaponized. Two-source confirmation
      protects the board from being dragged into a personal feud and gives the
      hearing a defensible evidentiary basis. The two-source rule should be in
      the published enforcement policy so the board cannot be accused of
      applying it selectively.
  - title: Send a graduated notice sequence
    content: >-
      The defensible enforcement sequence is: courtesy letter (no fine, written
      notice of the rule and the report), formal notice with hearing offer
      (after second confirmed complaint, fine attaches if not cured), then fine
      and possible legal action. Each step must be in writing, dated, and
      delivered by a method that creates proof of delivery (certified mail,
      email with read receipt, posted on door with photograph). Skipping the
      courtesy step looks aggressive and is the basis many courts cite when
      striking fines for procedural unfairness.
  - title: Audit enforcement quarterly for selective patterns
    content: >-
      Once a quarter, pull the complaint log and the enforcement actions taken.
      Look at the pattern: are complaints from one block driving most actions?
      Are families with children disproportionately receiving notices? Are
      rentals over-represented relative to owner-occupied units? If the answer
      is yes, the pattern itself is the problem regardless of intent. Adjust
      either the rule or the enforcement approach before the pattern triggers
      a Fair Housing complaint.

## Noise complaints are not really about noise

Most noise complaints that reach an HOA board are about something else — a long-running dispute between two units, a new resident testing the limits, a renter the neighbors do not want there. The board gets pulled in because the complainant frames it as a rule violation. Once the board takes action, the association becomes a party to the dispute and inherits the legal exposure.

The exposure is rarely from the noise itself. It is from how the board handled the complaint. Did the board apply the same standard to similar complaints from other units? Did the board document the process? Did the board send written notice with an opportunity to be heard? Did the rule the board enforced actually exist in the CC&Rs or rules at the time of the violation?

## Why selective enforcement is the largest risk

The Fair Housing Act prohibits discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status, and national origin. "Familial status" means households with children under 18. Most board members do not realize that a noise rule enforced primarily against children playing in courtyards or running in halls is a textbook familial-status case. The intent does not matter. The pattern of enforcement is enough.

The same applies to disability. A resident whose disability causes pacing, vocalization, or unusual sleep patterns is protected. Enforcing a noise rule against that resident — even when the noise is real and disturbing — without considering a reasonable accommodation is a Fair Housing violation.

D&O coverage on Fair Housing claims varies. Many policies exclude "discriminatory acts" entirely. Others cover the defense but not punitive damages. Punitive damages in HUD enforcement actions can run into six figures. The board members named in the complaint will not learn about the exclusion until the carrier denies coverage.

## The complaint log is the defense

Every Fair Housing claim asks the same question: how did you treat similar complaints from other residents? The answer must be documentary. A written complaint log showing every complaint received, the action taken, and the disposition is the only document that defends against the claim.

The log should include:

- Date the complaint was received
- Source unit and complainant name
- Subject unit and resident name
- Nature of the complaint
- Action taken (none, courtesy letter, notice, fine, hearing)
- Outcome
- Any reasonable accommodation requested or granted

Keep the log for the longer of seven years or the state statute of limitations on Fair Housing claims. HUD complaints can be filed up to one year after the alleged act, but many state laws extend the window.

## When the board should not act

Some complaints should never reach board enforcement. The board should not act on:

- A single complaint with no corroboration
- A complaint about a normal residential activity (children playing during the day, a dog barking once)
- A complaint about a resident who is exercising a disability accommodation
- A complaint that is part of an ongoing personal dispute between two named residents who have not tried mediation

For each of these, the board response should be a written acknowledgment, an explanation of the enforcement standard, and either a referral to mediation or a request for additional documentation. This response is itself a defense — the board can show it considered the complaint and applied a consistent standard.

## When the board must act

Some complaints require board action regardless of how small the underlying noise is:

- A complaint about a violation of quiet hours specifically defined in the rules
- A complaint with two or more independent corroborating sources
- A complaint where the board itself has witnessed the violation
- A complaint involving a documented commercial activity (short-term rental party house, home-based business with traffic)

For these, the graduated notice sequence runs without exception. Skipping a step "because everyone knows the resident is the problem" is exactly the pattern that produces a procedural-fairness defense for the resident at hearing.

## The board''s real job

The board''s job is not to determine who is right in a noise dispute. The board''s job is to administer a process consistently. If the process produces a fine that is unfair in a particular case, that is the appeals or hearing officer''s problem, not the board''s. The board that documents complaints, applies the same standard to every unit, and sends written notice in sequence will rarely lose at hearing and will almost never lose a Fair Housing claim. The board that handles complaints in email threads and Slack DMs will eventually face one or both.
