## Why townhome maintenance is harder than condo maintenance

Condominium associations have a relatively simple maintenance model. The association owns and maintains everything outside the unit's interior surfaces. The owner maintains everything inside. The line is clean: paint inward, owner; paint outward, HOA.

Townhome HOAs do not work that way. Townhomes are typically fee-simple ownership — the owner owns the lot and the structure, including the exterior. The HOA maintains common areas and may maintain certain shared building components by declaration, but the default is owner responsibility for the building.

Then declarations modify the default. Some townhome declarations push close to the condo model — HOA maintains roofs, exterior walls, structural components. Others push close to single-family — HOA maintains only the grounds, sidewalks, and shared amenities. Most fall somewhere in between, often inconsistently.

This is why townhome maintenance disputes go to litigation more often than condo disputes. The boundary is not standardized, the declaration is often decades old, and modern components were never anticipated.

## The components and where the boundary usually runs

Here is a rough map of how most townhome declarations handle each component. This is a starting point — your declaration is the actual rule.

### Roofs

Most townhome declarations assign roofs to the HOA because the roof is structurally shared across attached units. Replacement is impractical to do unit-by-unit. But:

- Some declarations assign the portion of roof above each unit to that owner.
- Some declarations split the boundary at the roof decking — HOA owns shingles and underlayment, owner owns decking and trusses.
- Some declarations make roof maintenance owner responsibility but require uniform appearance.

### Exterior walls

This is the most variable category.

- Some declarations make the HOA responsible for everything outside the studs — siding, paint, sheathing, structural.
- Some make the HOA responsible for paint and siding only, with the owner responsible for substrate and structure.
- Some make the owner responsible for everything from the framing outward.

### Foundations

Often owner responsibility, but not always. Shared foundations between attached units sometimes become HOA responsibility for the shared portion.

### Windows and doors

Almost always owner responsibility, with HOA architectural review over appearance and material.

### Decks, patios, balconies

Usually exclusive-use common elements with split responsibility — HOA maintains the structure, owner maintains the surface and any modifications.

### Fences

Boundary fences between lots are often shared (each owner responsible for half). Fences along common areas are usually HOA. Privacy fences within an owner's lot are usually owner.

### Driveways and walkways

Often owner responsibility for the driveway, HOA responsibility for shared walkways. Some declarations make the entire pavement system HOA-maintained for uniformity.

### Landscaping

Front yard landscaping is often HOA-maintained for curb appeal uniformity. Backyard or rear-lot landscaping is often owner.

### Modern components

Solar panels, EV chargers, smart-home equipment, satellite dishes — none of these existed when many declarations were drafted. They are interpretation problems by default.

## The insurance gap nobody plans for

The maintenance boundary should match the insurance boundary. When it does not, owners and the HOA both end up exposed.

Three common gap patterns:

### Gap 1: HOA-maintained property excluded from master policy

The declaration says the HOA maintains exterior walls, but the master policy excludes anything beyond a certain depth into the wall. A water intrusion incident damages framing the master policy will not cover. Who pays?

### Gap 2: Owner-maintained property excluded from HO-6 policies

The owner is responsible for some component, but their HO-6 (condo-style) policy excludes it as a common element. The HOA master policy also excludes it. The component sits in a no-coverage zone.

### Gap 3: Limited common elements with mismatched coverage

A balcony is a limited common element. The declaration says the owner maintains the surface, the HOA maintains the structure. The master policy covers the structure but not the surface. The owner's HO-6 covers the surface but not the structure. A leak through both creates two claims, two deductibles, and an argument about apportionment.

The fix is an annual insurance audit against the maintenance boundary. The HOA master policy and the owner policies should jointly cover everything. Where they do not, either the master policy expands, or the board communicates clearly to owners what they need to add to their personal policies.

## Reading the declaration

Townhome declarations are dense, dated, and frequently contradictory across sections. A real reading requires:

1. **The maintenance section.** Often titled "Maintenance, Repair, and Replacement" or similar. This is the primary source.
2. **The definitions section.** "Common element," "limited common element," "unit," "lot" — definitions modify the maintenance section.
3. **The insurance section.** Sometimes the insurance section assigns coverage that contradicts the maintenance section. The contradiction itself is useful information.
4. **The architectural section.** Approval requirements for modifications interact with maintenance — an owner who modifies a component may take on maintenance responsibility for the modification.
5. **Amendments and clarifications.** Many declarations have been amended over the years. The current version may differ substantially from the recorded original.

If reading the declaration leaves you uncertain about responsibility for any major component, get a written attorney opinion. Document the opinion and apply it consistently.

## Consistent interpretation as a fiduciary duty

The legal risk is not just misreading the declaration. It is reading it inconsistently across owners.

If the board treats Owner A's siding repair as HOA responsibility and treats Owner B's identical issue as owner responsibility, Owner B has a discrimination claim regardless of what the declaration actually says. Inconsistency is itself a breach of fiduciary duty.

The fix:

- Document the boundary in writing.
- Apply the documented boundary to every case.
- When a case is genuinely novel, document the new interpretation and apply it going forward.
- Re-publish the boundary chart annually so owners know what to expect.

## When the declaration needs amendment

Sometimes the declaration is so out of step with modern practice that the right move is amendment, not interpretation. Examples:

- Declaration assigns roof to owners but the practical reality is HOA-managed shared replacement.
- Declaration is silent on solar panels, EV chargers, or other modern components.
- Declaration assigns maintenance in ways that have become financially impossible for typical owners.

Amendment requires a member vote per the declaration's amendment provision — typically 67-75%. The process is slow but the result is durable. Boards that operate against the declaration without amending it are accumulating legal risk that compounds over time.

## What good looks like

A well-managed townhome HOA has:

- A current, written maintenance boundary chart distributed annually.
- A written attorney opinion resolving every known ambiguity.
- An annual insurance audit confirming the master policy and recommended owner coverage jointly cover the boundary.
- A documented process for handling disputed cases, applied consistently.
- Periodic review of the declaration for amendments needed to keep up with modern building components.

Boards that maintain this discipline rarely end up in maintenance litigation. Boards that wing it routinely do.
