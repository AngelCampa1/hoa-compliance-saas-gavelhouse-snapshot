## What deconversion is and why it is happening

A condominium is a creature of statute. State law allows owners to subdivide a building into individually owned units with shared common elements. Deconversion is the reverse process: a buyer purchases every unit in the building, the condominium regime dissolves, and the building reverts to single ownership — typically as rental apartments or as a redevelopment site.

The economics drive this. In many older condominium buildings, the per-unit market value is significantly lower than the per-unit value an investor would pay to consolidate the building, renovate, and rent it. The math is simple: aggregating the building unlocks rental income and disposition value that fragmented ownership cannot capture.

Deconversion has been particularly active in older urban condominium buildings — Chicago saw dozens of deconversions in the late 2010s, which is what triggered Illinois's threshold increase. Florida, Hawaii, and parts of California have seen similar activity.

We do not have a position on whether deconversion is good or bad. We do have a position on whether boards understand the process before it lands on them. Most do not, and that creates fiduciary exposure.

## The supermajority threshold

Every state that allows deconversion sets a supermajority threshold. The threshold is usually expressed as a percentage of ownership interest (the percentage assigned to each unit in the declaration), not as a count of units. In a building with mixed unit sizes, these numbers diverge.

Common thresholds:

- **Illinois:** 85% of ownership interest, raised from 75% by Public Act 101-0132 in 2019.
- **Florida:** 80% under the Florida Condominium Act.
- **Hawaii:** 75% under HRS 514B-106.
- **Many UCIOA states:** 80% under the Uniform Common Interest Ownership Act default, which the declaration can adjust.

The declaration can set a higher threshold than statute. It generally cannot set a lower one. Some declarations require 100% approval, which effectively prohibits deconversion.

Counting the threshold correctly matters. Mixed-unit buildings where penthouses have 5x the ownership interest of studios mean that a small number of large units can swing the vote.

## The Illinois template

Illinois is the most studied case. Through the mid-2010s, investor groups bought up units in older Chicago condominium buildings, hit the 75% statutory threshold, and forced bulk sales. Many of the displaced owners were elderly residents who had lived in their units for decades and could not afford to repurchase comparable property in the same neighborhood.

Public Act 101-0132 made several changes effective June 2019:

1. Raised the threshold from 75% to 85% of ownership interest.
2. Required the buyer to pay each unit owner the greater of the bulk-sale allocation or the appraised individual unit value.
3. Required relocation assistance equal to one year of comparable rent for owner-occupants.
4. Strengthened notice and procedural requirements.

Other states are watching. Florida has had legislative proposals to raise its threshold. New York has stricter procedural protections already. The trend is toward more owner protections, not fewer.

## Dissenter rights

Owners who vote no — or who do not vote — become dissenters. They cannot block the sale, but they have substantive rights.

### Right to fair market value

Dissenters are entitled to the fair market value of their unit, calculated as of the date of the sale and as if the deconversion were not happening. The "as if" clause matters: the buyer cannot argue that the unit is worth less because deconversion is reducing the market.

In practice, the buyer commissions an appraisal, the dissenter can commission their own, and the parties negotiate. Unresolved disputes go to court valuation hearings.

### Right to relocation assistance

Some states require relocation assistance for owner-occupants. Illinois requires one year of comparable rent. Other states require 1-3 months. The relocation assistance is in addition to the unit purchase price.

### Right to procedural protection

Notice must be perfect. Vote mechanics must be perfect. The dissenter has standing to challenge any procedural defect — and procedural defects are the most common ground for overturning a deconversion. Boards that send notice to outdated addresses, hold the vote with insufficient quorum, or fail to disclose the buyer's identity create grounds for litigation.

## The board's role

The board does not vote on deconversion. Owners vote on deconversion. The board's job is to:

1. **Receive and evaluate the proposal.** When a buyer approaches with a bulk-sale offer, the board reviews terms, engages counsel, and prepares to facilitate the owner vote.
2. **Ensure procedural compliance.** Notice, meeting, vote mechanics, recordation — every step must comply with statute and the declaration.
3. **Protect minority interests in process, not outcome.** The board cannot kill a deconversion the supermajority approves, but it can ensure the dissenters' rights are honored.
4. **Wind down the association.** After closing, the board executes dissolution — final audit, vendor settlement, insurance run-off, distribution of remaining funds.

Board members carry fiduciary duty throughout. Voting against a deconversion personally is allowed; obstructing the process the supermajority has approved is not.

## How communities can prevent forced deconversion

If a community wants to block deconversion, the path is amending the declaration to raise the supermajority threshold above what the statute allows the buyer to reach.

Common amendment approaches:

- **Raise the threshold to 95% or 100%.** This makes deconversion functionally impossible without unanimous consent.
- **Add a right of first refusal.** The association gets the option to match any bulk-sale offer.
- **Require owner-occupant supermajority.** The threshold must be reached among owner-occupants specifically, not all owners. This blocks investor accumulation.
- **Add waiting periods.** Require a 12-month notice period and re-vote.

The amendment itself requires a supermajority. Communities that want to protect themselves should amend before an investor starts accumulating units, not after.

## Process timeline

A typical deconversion runs 6-18 months end to end:

- **Months 1-2:** Buyer due diligence and proposal preparation.
- **Month 3:** Board receives proposal, engages counsel, schedules owner meeting.
- **Months 4-5:** Notice period (30-90 days depending on state).
- **Month 5 or 6:** Owner vote.
- **Months 6-9:** Dissenter unit appraisals and buyout negotiations.
- **Months 9-12:** Closing.
- **Months 12-18:** Association dissolution, final audit, fund distribution.

Litigation from dissenters or procedural challenges can extend any phase by a year or more.

## What boards should do now

Even communities not facing active deconversion should prepare:

1. **Read your declaration.** Know your current threshold.
2. **Read your state statute.** Know whether your declaration can be amended to raise the threshold.
3. **Run a quiet ownership audit.** Track unit ownership over time. If the same buyer is acquiring multiple units, the board should know.
4. **Consider a defensive amendment.** If the community values long-term residency, a 95%+ threshold and right-of-first-refusal amendment is worth the effort.

A board that ignores deconversion until an investor proposes one is a board reacting under deadline pressure. A board that has read its declaration, amended thoughtfully, and tracked ownership is in a position to negotiate from strength.
