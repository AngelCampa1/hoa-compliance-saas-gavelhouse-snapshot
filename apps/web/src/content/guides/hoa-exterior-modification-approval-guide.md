## Why architectural review is the highest-litigation board function

Of all the things HOA boards do, architectural review generates the most lawsuits per dollar at stake. Reserve compliance is more financially serious. Board elections are more politically charged. But architectural disputes go to court at high frequency because:

1. The stakes feel personal — it is the homeowner's house.
2. The rules are often written badly or interpreted inconsistently.
3. State legislatures have layered on consumer protections (deemed approval, solar access, flag display, satellite reception).
4. Boards routinely make procedural mistakes that void otherwise valid decisions.

The good news: architectural review is also the most fixable function. A board that adopts written guidelines, runs a tracked intake process, decides within the window, and documents consistently rarely ends up in court. The discipline is unglamorous but cheap.

## Deemed approval: the procedural trap

Most states have deemed-approval provisions in their HOA statutes. The mechanics are straightforward:

1. Owner submits a complete architectural request.
2. The clock starts when the association receives the request.
3. The association has a defined window (typically 30-60 days) to issue a written decision.
4. If the window expires without a written decision, the modification is automatically approved.

### State examples

**Florida (FS 720.3035):** If the request is not denied within 30 days of submission, it is deemed approved.

**Texas (Property Code 209.00505):** The architectural committee must approve or disapprove a notice within a stated period; if no period is stated, 30 days.

**Virginia (Property Owners' Association Act):** Boards must respond within 45 days for most modifications.

**North Carolina (Planned Community Act):** Specific notice and response timelines apply.

The exact mechanics differ — some states require the request to be "complete" to start the clock, some allow extensions for complex requests, some define what counts as a written decision. But the pattern is the same: silence equals yes.

### How boards trigger deemed approval by accident

- Committee chair receives requests in personal email and forgets to forward.
- Committee meets monthly; a request received the day after a meeting has 28 days before the next opportunity to decide.
- Committee approves verbally without sending the written decision before the deadline.
- Committee asks the owner for more information without formally documenting that the clock has paused.
- Committee delivers the decision to the wrong address or relies on regular mail without confirmation.

Each of these patterns ends with the same result: the modification is approved by operation of law, the committee finds out later, and the only recourse is a lawsuit the association will probably lose.

## What boards can actually deny

Boards can deny modifications that violate:

- **Recorded design guidelines.** The specific written guideline provision must be cited.
- **The declaration.** Express prohibitions in the CC&R can be enforced.
- **Applicable law.** Building code, zoning, fire code, ADA.

Boards cannot deny based on:

- **Personal taste.** "I don't like the color" is not a valid denial.
- **Undocumented preferences.** "We have always done it this way" without a written guideline is not enforceable.
- **The owner's identity, status, or beliefs.** Discrimination is independently illegal.
- **Speculative future harm.** "This might lead to others doing the same thing" is not actionable.

Vague denials referencing "community character," "aesthetic harmony," or "neighborhood standards" without a specific written guideline are routinely overturned. The denial must point to a specific written rule the modification violates.

## State-protected modifications

Several categories of modifications have statutory protection that overrides HOA design guidelines.

### Solar panels

Most states have solar access laws limiting what HOAs can require. California's Solar Rights Act, Texas Property Code 202.010, Florida Statute 163.04, and similar provisions prohibit blanket solar bans. HOAs can impose reasonable aesthetic restrictions (placement, screening of conduits) but cannot meaningfully reduce system output or impose costs that substantially exceed the system's value.

### Flag display

The Freedom to Display the American Flag Act and similar state laws protect display of the U.S. flag. HOAs can regulate size, location, and timing within reasonable limits but cannot prohibit display.

### Satellite dishes and antennas

The FCC Over-the-Air Reception Devices rule preempts HOA bans on satellite dishes and TV antennas one meter or smaller. HOAs can impose limited restrictions for safety, historic preservation, or minimal aesthetic considerations.

### EV charging

Several states (California, Florida, Hawaii, others) have laws protecting installation of EV charging stations. HOAs can impose reasonable conditions but cannot prohibit.

### Drought-tolerant landscaping

Some states (California, Texas, Nevada) protect xeriscaping and drought-tolerant landscaping. HOAs cannot require water-intensive lawns.

A board denying any of these categories without specific legal grounds is courting a quick loss.

## The architectural review committee structure

Most successful communities run architectural review through a standing committee separate from the board.

### Committee charter

- 3-5 members appointed by the board.
- Written charter defining authority and procedures.
- Authority to approve modifications that comply with guidelines.
- Authority to deny modifications that violate guidelines.
- Decisions appealable to the board for denials only.

### Meeting cadence

For a 100-300 unit community, monthly meetings work if the committee can also issue electronic decisions between meetings for time-sensitive requests. Larger communities or those with high modification activity may need bi-weekly meetings.

### Decision documentation

Every decision must produce a written document containing:

- Owner name and unit identifier.
- Description of the requested modification.
- Date of request receipt.
- Date of decision.
- Decision (approve, approve with conditions, deny).
- Reasoning citing specific guideline provisions.
- Any conditions or expiration dates.
- Signatures or names of deciding committee members.

Store decisions in a tracked log. Make the log available to all board members, the manager (if any), and the owners involved.

## Process flow

A working modification process:

1. **Owner submits request** through a single intake channel — typically an online form or a single dedicated email address.
2. **System timestamps receipt** and creates a tracked record.
3. **Committee acknowledges** within 5 business days, confirms completeness, requests missing information if needed.
4. **Committee reviews** at the next meeting or via electronic decision.
5. **Decision issued in writing** to the owner before the deadline, with delivery confirmation.
6. **Owner proceeds** (if approved) or appeals to the board (if denied).
7. **Inspection on completion** to verify as-built matches approval.
8. **File closure** with as-built documentation in the tracked record.

Each step has documentation that protects the board from later disputes.

## What good looks like

A well-run architectural review function:

- Maintains current written design guidelines published on the community website.
- Tracks every request through a digital system with timestamps.
- Decides within the statutory window 100% of the time.
- Issues written decisions with specific guideline citations.
- Documents as-built modifications after completion.
- Enforces consistently, with the same outcome for every owner with the same fact pattern.

A poorly run function:

- Has no current written guidelines (or guidelines from 1995 that nobody follows).
- Receives requests in committee chair's personal email.
- Misses windows and inadvertently approves modifications.
- Issues vague verbal denials.
- Enforces selectively based on which owner is asking.

The difference between the two is not difficulty — it is discipline. The disciplined version takes less time to operate because it generates fewer disputes.
