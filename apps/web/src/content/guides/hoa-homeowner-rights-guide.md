## Why homeowner rights are easier than boards think

Most HOA litigation we read about starts with a procedural rights violation, not a substantive disagreement. A homeowner asks for records and gets stonewalled. A homeowner is fined without a hearing. A homeowner is excluded from a meeting they had a right to attend. The original dispute might have been minor; the rights violation is what generates the lawsuit.

The fix is not adversarial. It is procedural. Boards that respect homeowner rights — as a matter of routine, not in response to specific demands — almost never end up in court over those rights. The discipline costs almost nothing and prevents most of the litigation HOAs face.

We built Gavelhouse with the assumption that compliance is the default behavior. The records request gets logged, the meeting notice goes out, the enforcement action follows the notice-and-hearing sequence. None of that is heroic. It is just done.

## The records inspection right

This is the most enforceable homeowner right and the one boards violate most frequently.

### What records are inspectable

Under most state statutes, owners can inspect:

- **Financial statements:** Income statements, balance sheets, cash flow statements.
- **Bank reconciliations:** Monthly reconciliations of association accounts.
- **Vendor invoices and contracts:** Documentation of expenditures.
- **Board meeting minutes:** Both regular and executive (with redactions for protected content).
- **Governing documents:** Declaration, bylaws, design guidelines, rules.
- **Membership lists:** With some restrictions on commercial use.
- **Reserve studies:** Most recent and historical.
- **Audit reports:** Most recent and historical.
- **Annual budgets:** Adopted and historical.
- **Insurance policies:** Master and supplemental.
- **Capital improvement records.**

Some states have specific exclusions — attorney-client privileged communications, personnel records, individual delinquent account details (with respect to other members' accounts), and active litigation work product.

### Response timelines

- **California:** 10 business days for most records, 30 days for older records. Civil Code 5200-5240 sets the framework.
- **Florida:** 10 working days. FS 720.303(5) sets the requirement.
- **Texas:** Reasonable time, generally interpreted as 10-30 days.
- **Most other states:** 5-30 days depending on statute.

Pull your state's statute. Set the response process to meet the shorter of the statutory window and any declaration provision.

### Penalties for refusal

California imposes statutory penalties of $500 per request for willful failure to produce records, plus actual damages and attorney fees. Florida allows damages and attorney fees. Most states have similar provisions. The penalties make refusal economically irrational.

### The right way to handle requests

1. Acknowledge receipt within 2 business days.
2. Identify responsive records.
3. Produce records within the statutory window.
4. Charge only actual reproduction cost (typically capped per page).
5. Document everything.

### The wrong way

- Ignoring the request.
- Asking the homeowner to justify why they want the records.
- Charging excessive copy fees.
- Producing partial records without explanation.
- Conditioning production on the homeowner agreeing not to share with others.

## The right to attend meetings

Open-meeting laws guarantee owner attendance at board meetings.

### What is covered

- Regular monthly or quarterly board meetings.
- Special board meetings.
- Annual membership meetings.
- Committee meetings (in some states).

### What is excluded

Executive sessions for narrow purposes:

- Pending or threatened litigation.
- Personnel matters affecting specific employees.
- Confidential discussion of delinquent accounts (with that owner's privacy protected).
- Contract negotiations where confidentiality affects pricing.

The board must announce that an executive session will be held, the topic category, and the duration. Going into executive session for non-permitted purposes is a violation.

### Notice requirements

Notice of board meetings must typically be posted 4-7 days in advance, with the agenda. Annual meetings require longer notice — typically 14-30 days. The notice should include time, location, agenda, and instructions for virtual participation if applicable.

### What owners can do at meetings

Most state laws guarantee owners the right to attend, but rights to speak vary. Many declarations and statutes provide for a designated open-comment period — often capped at 3-5 minutes per owner. Owners do not have the right to interrupt or to vote on board motions (only members vote on member motions).

## Voting rights

Owners vote on:

- Board elections.
- Amendments to governing documents.
- Capital improvements above thresholds.
- Special assessments above thresholds.
- Recall or removal of board members (per declaration).
- Other matters specified in the declaration.

Each owner generally has one vote per unit, weighted by ownership interest in some communities. Voting must be conducted with proper notice, secret ballot where required, and accurate counting.

Boards that mishandle elections — improper notice, lost ballots, miscounts — face election challenges that can void outcomes. Election integrity is a board responsibility separate from the substance of who wins.

## The right to notice and hearing before enforcement

Before fining a homeowner or imposing other sanctions, the board must follow due process.

### Required steps

1. **Written notice** describing the alleged violation, citing the specific governing document provision, identifying the cure required.
2. **Cure period** — typically 14-30 days during which the homeowner can fix the problem without sanction.
3. **Notice of hearing** — if the homeowner does not cure, written notice of the hearing date and time, with the right to attend and present evidence.
4. **Hearing** before the board or a hearing committee, with the homeowner given opportunity to be heard.
5. **Written decision** after the hearing, with reasoning.
6. **Right to appeal** in some declarations.

### Common procedural failures

- Imposing fines without prior written notice.
- Holding the hearing without the homeowner present and without proof of notice.
- Imposing sanctions disproportionate to the violation.
- Selective enforcement (sanctioning one owner while ignoring identical violations by others).

Each failure exposes the enforcement action to legal challenge. Courts routinely void enforcement that skips procedure.

## State-specific provisions worth knowing

### California (Davis-Stirling Common Interest Development Act)

- Civil Code 5200-5240: detailed records access provisions with statutory penalties.
- Civil Code 5800: limited liability for volunteer board members acting in good faith.
- Civil Code 5650-5740: detailed assessment, lien, and foreclosure procedures.
- Civil Code 4800-5000: governing documents and amendment procedures.

### Florida (FS Chapter 720 for HOAs, FS Chapter 718 for condos)

- FS 720.303(5) and FS 718.111(12): records access with 10-working-day timeline.
- FS 720.303(2) and FS 718.112(2): open meeting requirements.
- FS 720.305 and FS 718.303: enforcement procedures including notice and hearing.

### Texas (Property Code Chapters 202, 209)

- Property Code 202.004: records access provisions.
- Property Code 209.0051: meeting notice requirements.
- Property Code 209.006-209.0061: notice and hearing before enforcement.

### Most UCIOA states

The Uniform Common Interest Ownership Act provides default frameworks for records, meetings, voting, and enforcement that many states have adopted with variations.

## What good looks like

A board that respects homeowner rights operates with:

- A documented records-request response process meeting state statutory windows.
- Open meetings with timely notice and full minutes.
- Executive sessions only for permitted purposes, documented as such.
- Notice-and-hearing procedure for every enforcement action.
- A compliance log capturing every rights-relevant interaction.
- Annual board training on the rights statute and the declaration.

The cost is administrative discipline. The benefit is dramatically reduced litigation exposure and a community that perceives the board as legitimate. Both matter for fiduciary protection and for the day-to-day functioning of the community.
