steps:
  - title: "January-February: Annual financial report and audit preparation"
    content: >-
      The fiscal year just closed. Your first task is producing the year-end
      financial statements that most state HOA statutes require to be
      distributed to homeowners within 60-120 days of fiscal year end. These
      statements should include a balance sheet, income statement,
      and--critically--a reserve fund status report showing your reserve balance
      and whether it is adequate relative to your most recent reserve study. If
      your HOA is subject to audit requirements (varies by state and community
      size), contact your CPA by February 1 to schedule the engagement. Audit
      preparation requires organizing bank statements, reconciliations, and
      transaction records from the prior year. Boards that maintain
      fund-separated accounting throughout the year will find this process
      significantly less burdensome than boards that must reconstruct which
      transactions belong to operating versus reserve funds.
  - title: "March-April: Reserve fund status review and contribution rate assessment"
    content: >-
      Review your current reserve fund balance against the target set by your
      most recent reserve study. Calculate your current percent-funded status.
      If your community is below the target funding level from the reserve
      study, your board needs to discuss whether to increase the reserve
      contribution rate in the next budget cycle. Most reserve studies recommend
      annual contributions that keep the reserve on track for the next 30 years.
      Communities that have been underfunding their reserves for several years
      face a correction--either gradual increases over multiple budget cycles or
      a special assessment. Document the review in board meeting minutes,
      including the current balance, the reserve study target, and the
      percent-funded ratio. This documentation protects board members who review
      the information and take action from personal liability claims tied to
      reserve fund management.
  - title: "May-June: Mid-year budget variance review"
    content: >-
      Compare actual income and expense against the approved budget through May.
      Flag any line items where actual spending is more than 15% above or below
      budget. Common variance sources: emergency maintenance that was not
      anticipated, assessment collection shortfalls if any homeowners are
      significantly delinquent, or insurance premium changes. If operating
      expenses are tracking significantly above budget, review whether a budget
      amendment is warranted before the mid-year board meeting. Delinquency
      management belongs on the agenda if any homeowner is more than 60 days
      past due. Most HOA governing documents specify a late fee and lien
      process--make sure that process is being followed consistently to protect
      the community's revenue position.
  - title: "July-August: Reserve study renewal evaluation"
    content: >-
      Most reserve studies require renewal every three to five years. Review the
      date of your most recent reserve study. If it is approaching three years
      old, begin the process of engaging a licensed reserve specialist for an
      updated study. Reserve study providers typically need 30-60 days to
      schedule and complete an on-site inspection. An updated study is needed
      before the next budget cycle if the current study is more than three years
      old--states that mandate reserve studies may penalize boards that operate
      on outdated studies. Also review whether any major capital projects have
      been completed since the last study. A completed roof replacement,
      resurfaced parking lot, or installed fence changes the reserve component
      inventory and may alter the funding schedule.
  - title: >-
      September-October: Annual budget preparation and reserve contribution
      planning
    content: >-
      The annual budget process begins here for communities with a January 1
      fiscal year start. Your budget needs two core components: the operating
      budget (covering recurring expenses for the next year) and the reserve
      contribution (the amount the board intends to transfer to the reserve fund
      during the year). The reserve contribution should be consistent with the
      funding schedule from your most current reserve study. Budget approval
      processes vary by state--most require homeowner notice and a board vote,
      some require a homeowner ratification vote. Build in sufficient time for
      notice requirements before your fiscal year start. If your reserve study
      identifies a significant shortfall in the current funding level, address
      it in the budget discussion rather than deferring it. Boards that
      consistently approve reserve contributions below the reserve study's
      recommended level while knowing about the shortfall face greater personal
      liability exposure than boards that document the shortfall, discuss
      options, and make an informed decision.
  - title: "November-December: Budget finalization and homeowner notice"
    content: >-
      Finalize and distribute the approved budget to homeowners within the
      timeframe required by your state's HOA statute. Most states require notice
      30-60 days before the new fiscal year. The notice must typically include
      the operating budget and the reserve fund contribution amount. Some states
      require the notice to include the current reserve fund balance and the
      percent-funded status from the most recent reserve study--verify your
      state's requirements. Set up the new fiscal year in your accounting
      software before January 1, including any changes to assessment amounts,
      fund allocation ratios between operating and reserve, and the reserve
      contribution schedule. Update the reserve tracking dashboard with any
      reserve study changes.
  - title: "Ongoing: Monthly tasks that cannot wait for the annual cycle"
    content: >-
      Reserve fund balance reporting belongs on every board meeting agenda, not
      just the annual review. Report the current reserve balance and
      percent-funded status at each meeting and document it in the minutes.
      Reconcile bank statements monthly--both operating and reserve
      accounts--and confirm that the accounting record matches the bank balance.
      Review delinquency reports monthly and initiate collections consistent
      with your governing documents. If your state requires the treasurer to
      certify that reserve and operating funds are properly separated, maintain
      that documentation throughout the year. The annual compliance review is
      easier when the monthly hygiene is consistent.
definitions:
  - term: Percent Funded
    definition: >-
      The ratio of the current reserve fund balance to the fully funded amount
      recommended by the reserve study. A percent-funded of 100% means your
      reserve holds exactly what the study recommends. Below 70% is generally
      considered underfunded and creates special assessment risk.
  - term: Reserve Contribution
    definition: >-
      The amount the board transfers to the reserve fund during a budget year.
      Reserve contributions are calculated based on the reserve study's funding
      schedule and should be reported as a line item in the annual budget.
  - term: Fund Separation
    definition: >-
      The accounting practice of maintaining operating funds and reserve funds
      in separate ledgers (and ideally separate bank accounts). Fund separation
      prevents commingling and allows the reserve balance to be reported as a
      distinct figure for state disclosure requirements.
  - term: Budget Variance
    definition: >-
      The difference between actual income or spending and the approved budget
      for the same period. Monitoring budget variance monthly allows the board
      to identify operational issues before they become financial problems.

faqs:
  - q: >-
      What financial reports should an HOA treasurer produce at each board
      meeting?
    a: >-
      Minimum: a balance sheet showing operating and reserve fund balances as
      separate line items, an income and expense statement comparing actual
      versus budgeted amounts for the period, a reserve fund status report
      showing current balance and percent-funded status against the most recent
      reserve study, and a delinquency report showing homeowners with past-due
      assessments. These four reports give the board the information needed to
      make financial decisions and document that the board is exercising
      fiduciary oversight.
  - q: What state deadlines do HOA treasurers typically need to track?
    a: >-
      State-specific deadlines vary, but the most common: annual financial
      report distribution (60-120 days after fiscal year end in most states),
      reserve study renewal (every 3-5 years depending on state), homeowner
      budget notice (30-60 days before new fiscal year), and reserve fund
      disclosure at unit sale (required in states like California and Nevada).
      Check your state's HOA statute for specific deadlines--these are legal
      requirements, not best practices.
  - q: How should a treasurer document reserve fund decisions in board minutes?
    a: >-
      Every board meeting where reserve fund status is reviewed should include
      in the minutes: the current reserve balance, the reserve study target for
      that year, the calculated percent-funded ratio, and a notation that the
      board reviewed the information. If the board decides to adjust the reserve
      contribution rate, document the rationale. This documentation record
      protects board members individually if reserve adequacy is ever
      challenged.
  - q: What happens if the treasurer misses a state filing deadline?
    a: >-
      Consequences vary by state and the type of deadline missed. Missing the
      annual financial report distribution to homeowners may expose the board to
      homeowner complaints and, in some states, board member removal actions.
      Missing Florida's reserve study and funding deadlines for condo
      associations has specific legal consequences under post-Surfside
      legislation. Missing the annual budget notice period may delay the ability
      to collect the new assessment rate. Track all deadlines in a shared board
      calendar with advance reminders.

## How to use this checklist

This checklist covers the compliance calendar that applies to most HOA treasurers in most states. Individual state requirements add specific deadlines--Florida's condo association requirements, California's reserve disclosure obligations at unit sale, Nevada's reserve fund disclosure rules--that you will need to layer on top of the universal tasks.

The goal is to eliminate surprises. Most HOA treasurer compliance problems are not complicated--they are calendar failures. An audit finding for commingled funds is usually not the result of complex fraud; it is the result of nobody setting up fund-separated accounting when the board took over. A failed special assessment vote is often traceable to inadequate reserve documentation in the budget process. A homeowner complaint about the annual report being late is preventable with a calendar reminder in November.

## The documentation imperative

What distinguishes a board that weathers an audit or a homeowner complaint from one that faces personal liability claims is usually documentation. Every reserve fund review should be in the minutes. Every budget variance discussion should be in the minutes. Every decision to defer a reserve contribution increase should be in the minutes with a stated rationale.

Documentation is the treasurer's protection. Software that makes it easy to produce these records--with timestamps, board member names, and figures that tie to the accounting records--is worth the subscription cost.
