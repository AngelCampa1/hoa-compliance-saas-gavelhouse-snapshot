## Why HOA audit preparation matters more than the audit itself

The audit opinion that lands on the cover of your year-end financials matters less than what shows up in the management letter, the findings list, and the resale disclosure documents. Buyers, lenders, FHA underwriters, and Fannie Mae project reviewers all read that disclosure before they fund a loan in your community. A clean opinion with no findings says the board is running the books to professional standards. A qualified opinion or a list of findings — especially anything related to reserve funds — sits in the file forever and shows up at every resale.

The 90-day pre-audit timeline below is the playbook a self-managed board can run without a management company. Everything in it assumes you do not have a CFO on staff and the treasurer has a day job.

## The compliance stakes

State HOA statutes in California, Florida, Nevada, Arizona, North Carolina, and several other states require audited or reviewed financial statements above specific revenue thresholds. Below the threshold, the governing documents almost always require some level of annual financial review by a CPA. The board cannot opt out of either requirement without an owner vote.

Beyond the statutes, fiduciary duty law applies: a volunteer board member who signs off on financial statements that turn out to be materially wrong has a personal liability problem. D&O insurance covers the defense costs in most cases but requires the board to have followed a reasonable process. A documented audit prep cycle is part of that process.

## Reserve fund reconciliation: the make-or-break step

The reserve fund reconciliation is the audit. If reserves do not tie out, nothing else in the financial statements is reliable. Auditors know this and start there. Boards know this and avoid it because it is hard work in QuickBooks, which was never designed for fund accounting. In a typical small HOA on QuickBooks, the operating and reserve transactions share a single chart of accounts; the only thing distinguishing reserve activity is a class tag or a category. Class tags can be edited after the fact, and they get edited — accidentally and intentionally. The result is a reserve trial balance that drifts away from the actual reserve bank balance over the year.

We built Gavelhouse because the alternative is broken. Fund accounting is enforced at the database layer: operating and reserve are separate accounting entities with separate balance sheets, separate income statements, and separate trial balances. Transfers between them require explicit journal entries. There is no class tag to edit. The reserve reconciliation runs in seconds because the books and the bank are designed to match.

## Owner ledger aging: where small problems hide

Every year-end, generate an aging report and walk through it line by line. The questions auditors ask:

- How old is the oldest receivable on the report? Anything over 12 months that has not been written off or sent to collections raises a collectibility question.
- Are there owners with credit balances that sat for the entire year? Unallocated credits are a liability — the HOA owes that money back to the owner.
- Was the collection policy applied consistently? If the bylaws say liens go on accounts after 90 days delinquent and your report shows 12 accounts at 180+ days with no liens, the auditor flags inconsistent policy application.

This is a treasurer-level task. It cannot be delegated to the bookkeeper because policy interpretation requires board context.

## Vendor file and 1099s

By the end of January each year, the HOA must issue 1099-NEC forms to every unincorporated vendor paid $600 or more during the prior calendar year. Auditors test this. They pull the vendor master, sort by year-to-date payments, and ask why a particular vendor over the threshold did not receive a 1099. The defense is a W-9 on file showing the vendor is incorporated. No W-9, no defense, and the IRS imposes penalties of up to several hundred dollars per missed form.

At day 30, pull the vendor list and resolve every gap. Get W-9s from any vendor that does not have one on file. Issue 1099s where required.

## Board minutes: the audit trail of decisions

Auditors do not just look at the books — they look at the decisions behind the books. Every major capital expenditure, every special assessment, every reserve study adoption, every contract above the board approval threshold should appear in board meeting minutes with a recorded vote. If the audit period includes a $42,000 roof contract and the minutes do not show board approval, the auditor flags it as an unauthorized expenditure.

Compile the full minutes packet for the audit period. Include regular meetings, special meetings, and references to executive session minutes (with actions taken — executive session deliberation does not need to be disclosed, but actions taken in executive session must be ratified in open session and reflected in minutes).

## The internal controls memo

A short written document — one page is plenty for a small board — describing the financial controls in place. The structure most boards use:

- **Cash receipts:** Who opens mail. Who deposits checks. Who records the deposit in the books.
- **Disbursements:** Who has check-signing authority. The dual-signature threshold (we recommend $1,000 for any HOA with revenue above $50,000).
- **Bank reconciliations:** Who reconciles each account monthly. Who reviews the reconciliation (must be a different person than the preparer).
- **Reserve transfers:** What documentation is required. Whether board approval is required for each transfer or whether the board sets a monthly transfer policy.
- **Approval thresholds:** Spending levels that require board approval before commitment.

Auditors do not expect a five-person volunteer board to have the controls of a Fortune 500 company. They expect to see something in writing that demonstrates the board has thought about controls and is following them.

## After the audit: closing out findings

If the audit produces findings, the board's response in writing matters as much as the findings themselves. For each finding, document the corrective action, the responsible officer, and the target completion date. The next year's audit will test whether the corrective action was implemented. A finding that recurs year over year is the path to D&O coverage being denied at renewal.

Boards that run this 90-day cycle, year after year, end up with audit files that resale buyers and lenders read with confidence. That is the actual product of audit preparation — not the opinion letter, but the reputation that comes with a clean compliance record.
