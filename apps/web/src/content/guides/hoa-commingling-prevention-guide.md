  Fund commingling--using a single account or single ledger to hold both
  operating and reserve funds--is the most common source of personal liability
  for volunteer HOA board members. Most state HOA statutes require fund
  separation, and most audits of small self-managed HOAs find at least some
  level of commingling. The fix is not complicated, but it requires the right
  accounting structure. General-purpose software like QuickBooks cannot enforce
  it. Purpose-built HOA accounting software with fund accounting can.
steps:
  - title: Determine whether your HOA currently has commingled funds
    content: >-
      Pull your most recent bank statement for each account your HOA maintains.
      Count the accounts: one operating checking account and one separate
      reserve savings or money market account is the standard structure. If your
      HOA has only one bank account for all purposes, you have commingled funds
      by structure--both current expenses and reserve savings are sitting in the
      same account. If you have two bank accounts but a single accounting ledger
      (such as a QuickBooks setup where both accounts are tracked but reserve
      transactions are just categorized differently within the same ledger), you
      may have partial separation at the banking level but insufficient
      separation at the accounting level for state disclosure purposes. If you
      have two accounts and your accounting software treats them as two separate
      funds with independent balance sheets and reports, you have proper fund
      separation.
  - title: Check whether your state mandates fund separation
    content: >-
      Most state HOA statutes require, at minimum, that reserve funds be
      disclosed separately from operating funds in annual financial reports.
      Many states go further and require that reserve funds be held in accounts
      that cannot be easily converted to operating use without board
      authorization. California's HOA statute requires reserve funds to be held
      in separate accounts from operating funds. Nevada, Florida, and several
      other states have similar requirements. Even in states without a specific
      mandate, commingled funds create personal liability risk because courts
      have held board members personally liable for damages resulting from
      improper reserve fund management--and commingling is evidence of improper
      management. If your state does not explicitly require separation, the risk
      argument still applies. Search your state's HOA or condo statute for
      'reserve fund' and 'separate account' to determine your specific legal
      requirements.
  - title: Open a separate reserve bank account if one does not exist
    content: >-
      If your HOA maintains a single bank account or a single operating account
      without a separate reserve account, opening a dedicated reserve account is
      the first physical step in fixing commingling. A standard setup: a
      checking account for operating funds (assessments in, vendor payments
      out), and a savings or money market account for reserve funds (monthly
      reserve contribution transfers in, capital project expenditures out). Some
      communities maintain reserve funds in certificates of deposit or
      short-term investments for better returns--this is acceptable as long as
      the investment is clearly designated as reserve funds and is accessible
      for capital expenditures on appropriate notice. Once you have two bank
      accounts, transfer the reserve balance from the operating account to the
      new reserve account. The reserve balance is the total accumulated in your
      accounting records as reserve contributions minus reserve expenditures.
      Your most recent reserve study update or your accounting records will show
      the figure. Document the transfer in board meeting minutes with board
      authorization.
  - title: Fix the accounting structure to enforce fund separation
    content: >-
      Opening separate bank accounts addresses physical separation.
      Accounting-level separation requires that your accounting software treats
      operating and reserve funds as distinct pools--not as accounts within a
      single balance sheet, but as separate fund entities with independent
      balance sheets and reports. In purpose-built HOA accounting software, this
      is the default fund accounting structure. Operating transactions post to
      the operating fund. Reserve transactions post to the reserve fund. The
      software prevents (or at minimum flags) cross-fund transactions. In
      QuickBooks, true fund separation requires a more complex configuration:
      two sets of equity accounts, class tracking enforced on every transaction,
      and careful setup that most QuickBooks templates do not provide by
      default. If your board uses QuickBooks, engage a CPA with HOA accounting
      experience to review your current setup and reconfigure it if needed. If
      you are evaluating new software, look specifically for platforms with
      native HOA fund accounting--not general accounting tools that require
      manual configuration.
  - title: Configure how assessments are allocated between funds
    content: >-
      In proper HOA fund accounting, each month's assessment collection is split
      at the time of receipt between the operating fund and the reserve fund.
      The split is based on your budget: if the annual assessment is $3,600 per
      unit ($300/month) and the reserve contribution is $720/year ($60/month),
      then 20% of each payment goes to reserves and 80% to operations. This
      allocation is configured once in your accounting software. When a
      homeowner makes a payment, the operating portion posts to the operating
      fund and the reserve portion posts to the reserve fund automatically. This
      automated allocation eliminates the manual step that creates commingling
      risk. In a QuickBooks or single-ledger setup, the treasurer must manually
      split each payment--or post to operations and then manually transfer the
      reserve portion. Every month without the manual transfer is a month of
      commingled assessment income.
  - title: Establish controls to prevent future commingling
    content: >-
      After fixing the structure, establish controls that prevent new
      commingling. The most important: no operating expenses paid from the
      reserve account without board authorization documented in meeting minutes.
      No reserve transfers to cover operating shortfalls without board
      authorization. All signatories on accounts informed of which account is
      for what purpose. In software with fund accounting, these controls are
      partially automated--the system flags or prevents cross-fund transactions.
      But physical bank account controls matter too: most banks will allow you
      to set up the reserve account with wire transfer or check-writing
      restrictions that require dual signatures, adding a procedural barrier to
      unauthorized reserve fund access. Document your controls policy in a
      one-page document approved by the board. Include it in the annual board
      orientation for new members. The controls exist so that a new treasurer
      inheriting the accounts does not accidentally treat the reserve account as
      accessible for operating shortfalls.
  - title: Document the remediation in board minutes
    content: >-
      Whatever commingling you find and fix should be documented in board
      meeting minutes before and after the remediation. The pre-remediation
      documentation should describe what was found: 'The board reviewed the
      current accounting structure and determined that operating and reserve
      funds have not been properly separated. Reserve contributions totaling $X
      were held in the operating account without a separate reserve account.'
      The post-remediation documentation should describe what was done: 'The
      board authorized the transfer of $X to a newly opened reserve account and
      directed that accounting records be reconfigured to treat reserve and
      operating funds as separate ledgers going forward.' This documentation
      creates a record that the board identified the problem and corrected it.
      It is not a guarantee against further liability, but it demonstrates the
      good-faith remediation that courts consider when evaluating board member
      personal liability claims.
definitions:
  - term: Fund Commingling
    definition: >-
      The practice of holding operating funds and reserve funds in the same bank
      account or treating them as a single accounting pool without separate
      balance sheets. Prohibited or restricted in most states, and grounds for
      personal liability claims against board members.
  - term: Fund Separation
    definition: >-
      The accounting and banking structure that maintains operating and reserve
      funds as distinct pools with independent balances, reports, and
      (typically) separate bank accounts. The standard structure for HOA
      compliance in most states.
  - term: Reserve Transfer
    definition: >-
      The monthly or periodic movement of funds from the operating account to
      the reserve account equal to the reserve contribution for the period. When
      properly structured, reserve transfers are automatic allocations at the
      time of assessment collection--not manual transfers that happen
      separately.
  - term: Cross-Fund Transaction
    definition: >-
      A transaction that moves money from one fund to another (e.g., using
      reserve funds to pay operating expenses). Cross-fund transactions should
      require board authorization and be documented in minutes. In proper HOA
      fund accounting software, they require explicit approval.

faqs:
  - q: What is the difference between commingling and improper reserve spending?
    a: >-
      Commingling is a structural problem: operating and reserve funds are not
      separated in accounting or banking. Improper reserve spending is a
      governance problem: reserve funds are spent on operating expenses or
      non-capital items without board authorization. Both are compliance issues,
      but they require different fixes. Commingling is fixed by creating proper
      fund accounting structure. Improper reserve spending is fixed by
      governance controls--requiring board authorization for reserve
      expenditures and documenting those authorizations.
  - q: Can the board be sued for fund commingling?
    a: >-
      Yes. HOA litigation involving fund commingling is documented in
      California, Florida, Nevada, and other states. Personal liability claims
      typically arise when a homeowner suffers harm traceable to inadequate
      reserves that were depleted or mismanaged--a special assessment after a
      capital failure, a property value impact from deferred maintenance. Courts
      have held individual board members personally liable when they knew about
      commingling and did not correct it. Directors and officers insurance
      provides some protection, but it does not cover intentional misconduct and
      may not cover all commingling-related claims.
  - q: How does commingling affect an HOA's ability to levy a special assessment?
    a: >-
      In states that require reserve fund disclosures at unit sale or in annual
      reports, a board that cannot demonstrate proper fund separation may face
      challenges levying a special assessment. Homeowners who object to a
      special assessment will scrutinize reserve fund history. If the reserves
      were commingled and the current balance is indeterminate, the legal basis
      for the special assessment is weaker. Courts have voided special
      assessments challenged on reserve fund management grounds.
  - q: >-
      Is it possible to fix commingling in QuickBooks without switching
      software?
    a: >-
      Partially. A CPA can reconfigure a QuickBooks setup to better mimic
      fund separation using class tracking and separate bank accounts. But
      QuickBooks does not enforce separation structurally--a single
      mis-categorized entry bypasses the configuration. The reconfiguration also
      requires expertise that most volunteer treasurers do not have.
      Purpose-built HOA accounting software enforces separation at the software
      level, which is more reliable than a QuickBooks configuration that depends
      on consistent manual discipline.

## Why commingling is so common in small self-managed HOAs

Commingling is almost never intentional. It happens because a volunteer treasurer several board cycles ago set up the simplest possible structure--one bank account, one QuickBooks file--and subsequent treasurers inherited it without questioning whether it satisfied legal requirements. Each year the structure continued, the exposure compounded.

By the time a new treasurer with accounting awareness looks at the setup, there may be years of mixed transactions to unpack. The good news is that you do not need to unpack them. The remediation is prospective: fix the structure going forward, document the current reserve balance based on bank statements, and establish controls that prevent future commingling.

## The software problem

QuickBooks is the most common tool in HOA commingling situations because it is the most common accounting software generally, and it was not designed for HOA fund accounting. QuickBooks workarounds--classes, sub-accounts--look like fund separation but do not enforce it. Every month, the treasurer must manually ensure that reserve contributions are allocated correctly. One month of missed allocation is commingling.

Purpose-built HOA accounting software with native fund accounting eliminates this risk by design. The allocation is configured once. Every transaction posts to the correct fund automatically. There is no monthly manual step to forget.

## The personal liability argument

Most volunteer board members are surprised to learn that fund commingling creates personal liability exposure--not just organizational risk for the HOA. The surprise reflects how common the problem is and how rarely it results in visible consequences. Consequences surface when a capital project failure triggers a large special assessment, when an HOA is audited in connection with a unit sale dispute, or when a homeowner who loses money challenges the board's reserve management directly.

Documentation that the board identified commingling and corrected it--with dates, authorized transfers, and accounting structure changes documented in minutes--is the board member's primary protection. The remediation steps in this guide are designed to create that documentation.
