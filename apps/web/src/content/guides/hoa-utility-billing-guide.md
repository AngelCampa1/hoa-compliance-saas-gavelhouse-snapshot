## What Is HOA Utility Billing?

Most HOA governing documents give the association some authority over utilities that serve the common areas — exterior lighting, irrigation, pool heating, hallway electricity. But in many communities, especially older condo buildings and townhome developments, that authority extends further: the HOA pays a single master utility bill for the entire property, then turns around and re-bills each unit for its share.

That billing arrangement is not just an accounting exercise. When an HOA collects money from homeowners to cover utility costs, it is acting as an intermediary between the utility company and the end users. In Texas, California, and several other states, that intermediary role triggers regulations that apply to utility resellers — rules that most HOA boards have never heard of.

This guide covers the two main utility billing structures used in HOA communities, how the RUBS allocation method works, what state regulations apply, and what disclosures boards must make to stay on the right side of the law.

## Master-Metered vs. Sub-Metered Communities

### Master-Metered Communities

A master-metered community has a single utility account that records consumption for the entire property. The utility company bills the HOA (or the management company) for total consumption. The HOA then calculates each unit's share and bills owners separately.

In older condo buildings built before the 1990s, this is the default configuration. Individual unit meters were not installed during construction, and retrofitting them is expensive. The master meter structure is common for water/sewer in townhome communities and for electricity in some condo towers.

### Sub-Metered Communities

A sub-metered community installs individual meters for each unit behind the master meter. Each unit's actual consumption is measured. The HOA still pays the utility company through the master account, but billing to each owner is based on real usage data rather than an allocation formula.

Sub-metering is more equitable — owners pay for what they actually use, which reduces disputes and encourages conservation. The downside is the capital cost of meter installation ($500–$2,000 per unit is typical) and the ongoing cost of meter reading and billing administration.

### Why the Distinction Matters Legally

The legal significance of master-metered billing is this: when the HOA re-bills homeowners for utility costs that were incurred through a master account, the HOA is effectively reselling utility service. State public utilities commissions (PUCs) regulate utility resellers, and some states require HOAs in this position to register, follow specific billing rules, and provide detailed disclosures. Boards that skip these steps are not exempt — ignorance of the regulatory framework does not create a carve-out.

## How RUBS Works

RUBS — Ratio Utility Billing System — is the allocation method used in master-metered communities that do not have sub-meters. Instead of measuring individual consumption, the HOA applies a formula to divide the master meter bill among units.

### Common RUBS Formulas

**Square footage allocation:** Each unit's share is proportional to its square footage as a percentage of total residential square footage. A 1,200 sq ft unit in a building with 100,000 total square feet bears 1.2% of the utility cost.

**Occupant count allocation:** Each unit's share is based on the number of residents. A 3-occupant unit in a building with 200 total occupants bears 1.5% of the cost. This method is considered more equitable for water billing because consumption tracks more closely with people than square footage.

**Blended formula:** Some HOAs use a formula that weights both square footage and occupant count. For example, 50% of the bill allocated by square footage and 50% by occupant count.

**Equal split:** The simplest approach — every unit pays an equal share regardless of size or occupancy. This is also the most inequitable and the most likely to generate disputes from owners in smaller units.

### The Administrative Fee

RUBS billing creates administrative work: collecting occupant counts, running the allocation calculation each billing period, generating individual bills, chasing collections. Most HOAs charge an administrative fee on top of the allocated utility cost to cover this work. State regulations in Texas cap the admin fee for water service. An industry standard is 5–10% of the utility charge per unit per billing period.

### RUBS Limitations

RUBS is an approximation. Owners in master-metered buildings consistently report frustration that their bill does not reflect their actual behavior — a unit with one retired person pays the same as a unit with four working adults when the formula is based on square footage. That disconnect drives disputes, which cost board time and sometimes legal fees.

## State Utility Billing Regulations

### Texas

Texas has the most detailed framework for master-metered utility billing in the country. The Public Utility Commission of Texas (PUCT) regulates water and sewer utility billing under Chapter 291 of the Texas Water Code and the associated administrative code.

Key Texas requirements for master-metered HOAs:

- Registration with the PUCT may be required depending on the structure of the billing arrangement
- Written notice to residents before beginning master-metered billing
- Billing statements must include the total master meter charge, the allocation formula, and each unit's calculated share
- The administrative fee is capped at a percentage set by PUCT rule
- Retaliatory disconnection or service interruption is prohibited

The PUCT publishes guidance and application forms on its website. Texas boards that have never reviewed this framework should do so before the next billing cycle — the penalties for non-compliance are real and the regulatory agency does investigate complaints.

### California

California AB 2446, enacted in 2004 and codified in the California Civil Code, requires written disclosure of sub-metering or RUBS billing arrangements before a resident occupies a unit. The disclosure must explain how the billing formula works, what charges the resident will be responsible for, and how disputes are handled.

The practical implication for HOA boards: every new owner or tenant must receive and acknowledge this disclosure before move-in. If the HOA has been operating a master-metered billing system without providing pre-occupancy disclosures, there is potential exposure to individual claims from current and former residents.

### Florida

Florida's HOA Act and the Florida Condominium Act both require certain financial disclosures to unit owners, but Florida does not have a PUC regulatory framework specifically for HOA utility billing equivalent to Texas. However, billing homeowners for utility costs in excess of actual costs is a breach of fiduciary duty under Florida law. The association must keep records of utility expenses and make them available for homeowner inspection.

### Arizona and Colorado

Arizona and Colorado have HOA transparency statutes that require boards to disclose the basis for any charge assessed to homeowners. For utility billing, this means the allocation formula and the underlying master meter data must be available for owner review on request. Neither state has a formal PUC registration requirement specifically for HOAs.

## Disclosure Requirements and What to Include

Regardless of what state you operate in, best practice for HOA utility billing includes providing homeowners with a billing notice that contains:

1. **The total master meter charge** for the billing period (what the HOA actually paid the utility company)
2. **The allocation formula** used to divide the cost among units
3. **Each unit's allocated share** before the administrative fee
4. **The administrative fee** percentage and dollar amount
5. **The final amount owed** by the unit
6. **Dispute resolution contact** and timeline

Providing this information on every bill, not just at move-in, is the standard that protects boards against claims that allocation methods were hidden or that charges were inflated.

## What Boards Can and Cannot Charge

The general principle across all state frameworks is that HOAs operating master-metered billing systems cannot charge homeowners more than the actual utility cost plus a reasonable administrative fee. You cannot profit from utility billing.

Practices that create liability:

- Adding a markup above the administrative fee percentage
- Using an allocation formula that systematically overcharges certain unit types
- Failing to pass through rate decreases or rebates from the utility company
- Charging for estimated usage rather than actual consumption without disclosure

If the HOA receives a rebate, credit, or billing adjustment from the utility company, that adjustment must be passed through to homeowners in proportion to their allocated shares.

## When to Consider Converting to Sub-Metering

RUBS billing works for many communities, but the administrative burden and dispute frequency stack up over time. Boards should evaluate converting to sub-metering when:

- Homeowner disputes about billing fairness are consuming meeting time
- Your state has introduced or tightened RUBS regulations
- The community is undergoing a significant capital improvement project (the construction access makes meter installation cheaper)
- Water conservation goals require consumption-based accountability

A sub-metering contractor can assess installation feasibility and cost for your specific building configuration. The capital cost is a one-time expenditure; weigh it against the ongoing staff time, dispute management costs, and regulatory compliance burden of RUBS.

## The Fiduciary Duty Connection

HOA boards owe a fiduciary duty to homeowners. When it comes to utility billing, that duty requires the board to:

- Implement the most equitable allocation formula available given the building's physical constraints
- Keep accurate records of all utility charges and pass them through without inflation
- Provide sufficient transparency for homeowners to verify they are being billed correctly
- Stay current on state regulations that govern master-metered billing

Boards that delegate utility billing entirely to a management company without understanding the regulatory framework remain personally liable for violations. "The management company handled it" is not a defense against a homeowner claim or a PUC regulatory action. The board sets policy; the management company executes it.

## Practical Steps for Boards Starting from Scratch

If your community has been running master-metered billing without formal compliance review, here is how to approach the cleanup:

1. Pull three years of master utility bills and compare them against what was actually charged to homeowners to verify no markup exists
2. Document the current allocation formula in writing
3. Have a local attorney review whether your state requires registration, disclosure, or other specific compliance steps
4. Update billing notices to include all required disclosure elements
5. Provide the updated notice to all current homeowners in writing and retain acknowledgments
6. If sub-metering is feasible, get two or three contractor quotes and bring the analysis to the board for a capital improvement vote

This cleanup process is not glamorous board work, but it eliminates a category of legal exposure that most boards do not realize they carry.
