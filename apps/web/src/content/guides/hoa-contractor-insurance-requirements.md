# HOA Contractor Insurance Requirements: COIs, Coverage Minimums, and Liability

We built Gavelhouse from the builder side of HOA governance — and one of the recurring patterns we see is boards that approve vendor contracts without ever seeing the vendor's insurance. The contractor seems reputable, the bid is competitive, the work needs to start. The board approves. Six months later, a roofer falls from the second story, claims the HOA was effectively his employer, and the association's D&O carrier denies the claim because the board approved an uninsured vendor.

This guide is about preventing that scenario. Insurance verification is not paperwork. It is the difference between a contractor's claim and a board lawsuit.

## Why HOAs need contractor insurance

Three categories of risk:

### Worker injury

If a contractor employee is injured on HOA property and the contractor lacks workers compensation coverage, the association can be treated as the statutory employer in many states. The HOA becomes liable for medical bills, lost wages, and disability — costs that can run into hundreds of thousands of dollars for a serious injury.

### Property damage to the community

A contractor causing damage to common areas, neighboring units, or owner property creates claims. Without contractor insurance, the association's master policy pays — and renews at higher premium, or with reduced coverage, the next year.

### Third-party injury

A delivery person, owner, or guest injured by contractor work or equipment creates third-party liability. If the contractor is uninsured, the claim flows to the association.

## Coverage minimums

Industry baseline for typical HOA vendors:

| Coverage                                 | Minimum    |
| ---------------------------------------- | ---------- |
| General liability per occurrence         | $1,000,000 |
| General liability aggregate              | $2,000,000 |
| Workers compensation employers liability | $1,000,000 |
| Auto liability (if driving on property)  | $1,000,000 |

For higher-risk work — roofing, structural, electrical, plumbing inside walls, tree work, work above one story, pool work — increase general liability to $2,000,000 per occurrence or higher.

For very large projects (capital reconstruction, building envelope work), insurance brokers often recommend $5,000,000 per occurrence with umbrella coverage.

## The Certificate of Insurance (COI)

A COI is a one-page document issued by the contractor's insurance broker. It includes:

- Insurance company names
- Policy numbers
- Coverage types and limits
- Effective and expiration dates
- Certificate holder (the HOA)
- Additional insured listings
- Producer (broker) name and contact

The COI is evidence of coverage at the time it was issued. It is not a guarantee of continued coverage.

### Common COI mistakes

- **Accepting an outdated COI.** Always check effective and expiration dates against the work period.
- **Confusing certificate holder with additional insured.** The HOA listed only as certificate holder is not covered by the contractor's policy. Both fields matter for different reasons.
- **Failing to verify with the broker.** COIs are easy to forge. A 60-second call to the broker confirms authenticity.
- **Treating COI mention as additional insured coverage.** A COI saying "HOA listed as additional insured" is not the actual endorsement. Get the endorsement form.

## Additional insured endorsements

An additional insured endorsement is a formal modification of the contractor's policy that names the HOA as an additional insured. When a claim arises from the contractor's operations:

- The contractor's insurer pays first
- The HOA's master policy is preserved
- The association is named in defense and indemnity for covered claims

For meaningful projects, do not rely on COI mention alone. Require the actual endorsement form (often labeled CG 20 10 or CG 20 37 in standard policy language) to be filed with the association.

## Workers compensation specifically

Workers comp is the single most important coverage for HOA contractor risk. State law generally requires it for any business with employees. Contractors who claim "I'm a sole proprietor with no employees" are sometimes legitimately exempt — but the moment they bring a helper, they need coverage.

Practical rule: require workers comp for any contractor with one or more workers on the property, regardless of how the contractor classifies them. The legal classification (employee vs independent contractor) is contested in many states and you do not want the dispute happening on top of an injury.

## Tracking expirations

Multi-year vendors generate ongoing risk because their COIs expire annually:

- Landscape contractors
- Pool service
- Security
- Pest control
- Janitorial
- Emergency response vendors on retainer

A vendor whose COI lapsed three months ago is uninsured for those three months. Track expiration dates and require updated COIs at least 30 days before expiration.

## Written contractor insurance policy

The single most effective protection is a written board policy specifying:

- Minimum coverage by category of work
- COI submission requirements before work begins
- Additional insured endorsement requirements above a project threshold
- Waiver of subrogation language where appropriate
- Annual renewal requirements
- Consequences for vendors without current COIs (removed from approved list)

The policy is referenced in every contract and bid request. Once the policy exists, insurance verification stops being a judgment call and becomes a procedural requirement.

## What happens during an audit or claim

When a claim hits, the carrier asks two questions:

1. Did the board verify the contractor's insurance?
2. Did the board follow its written policy?

A board with a documented policy, current COIs on file, and additional insured endorsements for major projects has clear answers. The claim flows to the contractor's insurer where it belongs.

A board with no policy and no COIs has inherited the claim. The master policy may pay or may deny coverage, the D&O carrier may decline as well, and the association absorbs the cost — often through a special assessment.

## Common board failure modes

### "We've always used this guy"

Long-tenured vendors often skip the documentation because trust replaced procedure. When the long-tenured guy gets injured on the property, trust does not pay the medical bills.

### "It was a small project"

Small projects produce big claims. A handyman fixing a fence on a Saturday can fall and break his back. The size of the project does not bound the size of the liability.

### "The bid was the lowest"

Uninsured contractors bid lower because they do not pay insurance premiums. The savings on the bid is exactly the risk transferred to the association.

### "He said he had insurance"

A verbal statement is not evidence. The COI is.

## The compliance lens

Contractor insurance verification is not a high-effort process. A written policy, a tracking sheet, and a 60-second broker call per major project handle most of the risk. Boards that build the process into their standard vendor onboarding rarely face contractor-related liability. Boards that treat it as paperwork-when-we-remember are one workplace injury away from a special assessment they did not budget for.

When the next vendor pulls onto the property, the board should be able to answer one question instantly: is their COI on file, and does it match our written requirements? If the answer is yes, the board has done its job. If the answer is "let me check," the answer is no.
