## Why EV Charging Has Become an HOA Governance Issue

Electric vehicle adoption in the United States has been doubling roughly every two to three years. As of 2024, more than 3 million EVs are registered in California alone, and the national fleet is growing faster than charging infrastructure. For HOA communities — particularly condominiums and townhome developments with shared parking — EV charging has shifted from an occasional homeowner request to a policy question that every board should have a written answer to.

The complicating factor is that homeowner expectations about EV charging access have been shaped by state law in a growing number of states. Seven states now have right-to-charge statutes that limit what HOAs can do. Boards operating without a formal policy are making ad hoc decisions that create legal exposure.

This guide walks through the legal landscape, what boards can and cannot require, and how to build a policy that is both legally sound and practically manageable.

## The Right-to-Charge Legal Landscape

Seven states have enacted statutes specifically limiting HOA authority to restrict EV charging:

### California (Civil Code Section 4745)

California's statute is the most detailed and the most homeowner-protective. It provides that any provision of a homeowners association that "effectively prohibits" EV charging is void and unenforceable. The HOA can impose reasonable requirements but cannot unreasonably restrict charging access.

California also requires HOAs to act on EV charging applications within 60 days. The attorney fee provision means homeowners who win right-to-charge disputes recover their legal costs from the HOA.

### Colorado (HB 21-1167)

Colorado enacted its right-to-charge statute in 2021. It prohibits HOAs from unreasonably restricting the installation of EV charging equipment in an owner's designated parking space. The statute provides a civil remedy with attorney fees for violations.

### Florida (Statute 559.915)

Florida's statute prohibits "unreasonable restrictions" on EV charging and was updated in recent years to expand its scope. Florida boards can require reasonable aesthetic standards for installation but cannot deny requests on grounds that essentially amount to opposition to EV adoption.

### Oregon, Washington, Virginia, and New York

These states all have statutes at various stages of development limiting HOA authority over EV charging. The specific requirements differ, and boards in these states should confirm the current state of the law with local counsel.

### States Without Right-to-Charge Statutes

If your state is not on this list, you currently have more authority to set EV charging policy — but the legal environment is changing rapidly. More states enact right-to-charge protections every year. A policy drafted today should be written with an eye toward future-proofing, not just current law.

## What Boards Can Still Require

Even in states with the strongest right-to-charge protections, boards retain meaningful authority.

### Application and Review Process

Boards can require a written application before any EV charging installation. The application can reasonably require:

- Description of the proposed installation (charger model, electrical specifications)
- Site plan showing where wiring will run and where the charger will be mounted
- Name and license number of the electrical contractor
- Evidence of building permit application or permit number
- Proof of liability insurance coverage

### Safety and Code Standards

Boards can require that all installations comply with applicable electrical codes, be performed by licensed contractors, and pass required inspections. These are not restrictions — they are safety requirements that protect the community.

### Cost Allocation

All right-to-charge statutes provide that the installing owner bears the cost of their individual installation. Boards can require that the owner also pay for any electrical infrastructure upgrades that are solely necessitated by and benefit only their installation. If a panel upgrade is needed to serve the entire building's future charging needs — not just this one installation — cost allocation becomes more complex.

### Insurance Requirements

Boards can require homeowners to maintain specified levels of liability insurance for their EV charging equipment and to name the HOA as an additional insured. This is a legitimate risk management requirement.

## What Boards Cannot Require (In Right-to-Charge States)

In states with right-to-charge statutes, the following requirements have been found unreasonable or void:

- Requiring specific charger brands or models that cost significantly more than equivalent alternatives
- Setting fees or cost requirements that make installation economically infeasible
- Requiring installation in a different parking space than the owner's designated space without justification
- Imposing aesthetic requirements that require custom equipment with no legitimate visual benefit
- Simply failing to respond to applications within the statutory window

## The Condo-Specific Problem: Electrical Capacity

Individual homes and townhomes with private garages typically have adequate electrical panel capacity for one Level 2 EV charger — a 240V, 32-amp or 48-amp circuit. The installation is straightforward and the cost is usually $500–$1,500.

Condominiums are different. Condo parking structures were designed to a specific electrical load assumption, and that assumption predates widespread EV adoption. Most condo buildings built before 2010 have electrical panels that cannot support more than a handful of Level 2 EV chargers without panel upgrades.

This creates a real infrastructure challenge:

**Panel capacity is finite and shared.** When the first owner installs an EV charger, their dedicated circuit reduces available capacity for subsequent owners. As adoption grows, the building hits a capacity ceiling before all interested owners can be accommodated.

**Panel upgrades are expensive.** Upgrading the electrical service entrance to a multi-unit building can cost $50,000–$200,000 or more depending on the building size and local utility requirements. This is a capital project, not a routine maintenance expense.

**Load management systems help.** Modern load management systems dynamically allocate available electrical capacity among multiple chargers, allowing more chargers to operate on the same panel by ensuring they do not all draw maximum current simultaneously. A load management system typically costs $5,000–$20,000 but can effectively double the number of chargers a building can support.

Boards that receive their first EV charging application should immediately commission an electrical capacity assessment before responding. That assessment tells you whether you can accommodate the request within current capacity, and what future requests will eventually require.

## The Make-Ready Infrastructure Approach

The most cost-efficient way to handle community-wide EV charging demand is to invest in make-ready infrastructure before individual requests pile up. Make-ready means running conduit and wiring to parking spaces in advance — creating the electrical backbone that chargers will connect to — without installing the charger heads themselves.

When an owner wants to add a charger, they purchase and install only the charger head (cost: $300–$800 for the unit plus $200–$500 labor). Without make-ready infrastructure, that same owner would also pay for all the wiring and conduit work, typically $1,500–$4,000 per installation.

The math is compelling: installing make-ready infrastructure for 50 parking spaces in a single project typically costs $30,000–$60,000 — roughly equivalent to 10–15 individual installations. The community gets infrastructure for 50 spaces at a cost that scales much more efficiently.

Make-ready infrastructure is a capital improvement. It adds new capacity and infrastructure that did not exist before, which means it typically cannot be funded from the reserve account (which covers replacement of existing components) and should be funded through a special assessment or a capital improvement fund.

## Building a Policy That Holds Up

A legally defensible EV charging policy for an HOA operating in a right-to-charge state should:

1. Acknowledge the statutory right to install EV charging and state that the board will process applications in good faith
2. Define what constitutes a complete application
3. State the review timeline (aligned with or shorter than the statutory window)
4. Specify the safety, contractor, and insurance requirements the board will impose
5. Address cost allocation for infrastructure upgrades with a clear formula
6. Define how disputes will be handled
7. Be reviewed by an HOA attorney before adoption

Boards that invest time in a written policy upfront avoid the much larger time and legal cost of handling contentious individual applications without clear standards.
