The control is mechanical, not cumbersome — modern HOA software automates the approval routing so the second approver gets a notification and approves with one click.
  - title: "Establish quarterly reserve review"
    content: >-
      Once per quarter, the board reviews the reserve fund: current balance, percent-funded versus the reserve study, year-to-date contributions versus budget, and any pending capital projects. Document the review in board minutes with a yes/no decision on whether the reserve trajectory remains aligned with the study. This single practice provides the strongest possible defense in any future fiduciary challenge — it demonstrates that the board was actively tracking the fiduciary obligation rather than addressing reserves only at audit time. The review takes 15 minutes when the data is in software; it takes hours when reserves live in spreadsheets.
  - title: "Adopt a written cybersecurity policy"
    content: >-
      A two-page policy covering: who has access to what systems (treasurer, president, secretary), password requirements (minimum length, MFA where supported), what to do if a board member''s account is compromised (immediate revocation, password reset, audit log review), and what owner data the board does and does not store.     content: >-

## What 2026 baseline looks like

Three structural practices define a board operating at 2026 baseline:

1. **Dual-signature disbursement controls** above a defined threshold.
2. **Quarterly documented reserve review.**
3. **Written cybersecurity policy** covering owner data handling.

A board with all three is operating at insurance-friendly, audit-friendly, owner-confidence levels. A board missing any one is visibly behind, and the gap is going to surface during the next D&O renewal, audit, or owner dispute.

The other practices in this guide — conflict-of-interest discipline, executive session controls, electronic voting compliance, AI disclosure — are also expected, but they are second-tier. Get the three baseline practices in place first.

## Why insurance carriers tightened in 2024–2025

D&O underwriting for HOA boards tightened significantly in the 2024–2025 cycle. Carriers reacted to a multi-year increase in claims involving:

- Reserve fund mismanagement (post-Surfside scrutiny)
- Owner data breaches (ACH credentials harvested from unsecured board email)
- Vendor self-dealing (board members awarding contracts to family companies without disclosure)
- Special assessment disputes (owners alleging board failure to fund reserves)

Each claim category now has a corresponding underwriting question. Boards without dual-signature controls, written cybersecurity policies, and documented conflict-of-interest procedures are answering "no" to those questions, which translates to higher premiums or coverage exclusions.

The fix is procedural, not expensive. A board that adopts the three baseline practices in a single meeting and documents the adoption in minutes typically sees premium quotes improve at the next renewal.

## AI-generated meeting summaries: disclose, don''t hide

AI meeting summary tools became common in 2024–2025. Many boards now use them. The 2026 norm is to disclose:

- The official minutes note that an AI tool was used to assist drafting
- A named board member reviewed and approved the AI output before adoption
- The original recording (if any) is retained per the document retention policy

Undisclosed AI minutes have started appearing in litigation as discovery items. Plaintiffs argue (sometimes successfully) that AI-assisted minutes are unreliable evidence of board intent. The disclosure converts the AI tool from a litigation risk into an efficiency gain — it''s not the AI use that''s problematic, it''s the absence of disclosure.

## Electronic voting under updated state laws

Most states have updated condo and HOA statutes since 2022 to explicitly permit electronic voting, but the procedural requirements vary. Common requirements:

- Identity verification (typically email plus owner-of-record check)
- Ballot secrecy where required by statute
- Retention of vote records for the period required by state law
- Notice to owners of the electronic voting option, with a non-electronic alternative

Boards using electronic voting for binding decisions (election of directors, special assessment approval) need to confirm their state''s current statute. The fact that electronic voting is "permitted" in your state does not mean any specific implementation is compliant.

## The conflict-of-interest discipline

This is the single most-undervalued practice. Conflict-of-interest disclosures take 60 seconds at a board meeting. Their absence creates personal liability that survives the board member''s term.

The pattern: board member''s spouse owns a landscaping company. Board awards the landscaping contract. Six months later, an owner FOIAs the contract and the bidding records, doesn''t find a disclosure, and sues. The board member''s personal assets are now in scope because D&O coverage explicitly excludes self-dealing without disclosure.

The fix is one minute of paperwork at the relevant board meeting. There is no excuse for skipping it.

## Executive session minutes as a separate record

Executive sessions cover sensitive topics: pending litigation, personnel matters, owner-specific delinquency, contract negotiations. State law typically requires:

- That executive sessions be documented
- That the documentation be kept confidential
- That the documentation be accessible to authorized parties (typically board members, sometimes auditors)

The 2026 best practice is to keep executive session minutes in an access-controlled document, separate from general minutes. Software with role-based access does this naturally. Shared Google Docs and email-attached Word files do not.

The risk: an executive session minute discussing pending litigation gets shared with an owner via inadvertent broad access on a Google Drive folder. The owner forwards it to opposing counsel. Privilege is now compromised. This pattern is well-documented in HOA litigation; the prevention is access control.

## Cybersecurity policy minimum content

A two-page cybersecurity policy adequate for most self-managed HOAs covers:

1. **Roles and access.** Who has admin access to what systems. List by role, not by name.
2. **Authentication standards.** Password length, complexity, MFA where supported, session timeouts.
3. **Data handling.** What owner data the board collects, where it is stored, how long it is retained.
4. **Incident response.** Who to contact if a board account is compromised, what immediate actions to take, when state breach notification rules trigger.
5. **Third-party data sharing.** Limits on sharing owner data with vendors or other parties.
6. **Annual review.** Policy reviewed and re-adopted annually.

This is a one-meeting drafting exercise. The cost is staff time. The benefit is having a defensible artifact during the next audit, insurance renewal, or breach incident.

## What 2018-era governance looks like

For contrast, a board still operating at 2018 baseline:

- Single-signer disbursements
- Reserve review only at audit time
- Owner data passed around in unencrypted email attachments
- Conflict-of-interest "we all know each other so it''s fine"
- Executive session content in general minutes
- No written cybersecurity policy

A board operating this way in 2026 is functional but exposed. The exposure surfaces unpredictably — a single owner dispute, a single carrier underwriting cycle, a single attempted ACH fraud — and when it does, the board scrambles. The fix is structural, takes one meeting to adopt, and converts the exposure into routine operation.
