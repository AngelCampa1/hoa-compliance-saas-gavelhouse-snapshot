## Why dissolving a working HOA is harder than starting one

Homeowners ask about dissolving the HOA more often than boards realize. The conversation usually starts with a frustration: a difficult board, an unwelcome rule, an unaffordable special assessment. It almost always ends with the same conclusion — dissolving a functioning HOA is procedurally close to impossible. Before a board spends time on a dissolution proposal, it helps to understand why most attempts fail.

The barriers are not arbitrary. They exist because dissolving the HOA affects three groups beyond the current owners: the mortgage lenders whose collateral depends on common-area maintenance, the local government that may inherit private infrastructure, and future buyers who relied on the HOA''s existence when purchasing. Each group has legal standing to block a dissolution that affects their interests, and the law gives each meaningful veto power.

## The vote threshold problem

Most CC&Rs require a supermajority — 67%, 75%, 80%, or unanimous — to terminate the declaration. The threshold is set high because dissolution permanently changes the property regime for every owner. State statutes often layer a minimum on top: Nevada requires 80% under NRS 116.2118; Florida requires 80% for residential condo termination under FS 718.117; Texas Property Code 209.0058 imposes detailed termination procedures.

Hitting the supermajority is hard. In a 100-unit community needing 80%, dissolution requires 80 affirmative votes. Apathetic non-voters count as no votes. A small organized opposition can defeat the proposal by getting 21 owners to vote no or stay home. Most attempts never reach quorum, much less the supermajority.

## The lender-consent problem

This is where most dissolutions die. Mortgages are secured by the unit and a proportional interest in the common areas. The mortgage instrument typically requires the lender''s consent before the borrower agrees to terminate the HOA, because terminating the HOA could eliminate the maintenance regime that preserves the lender''s collateral.

Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, which buy a meaningful share of all conforming residential mortgages, both include anti-dissolution language in their standard mortgage forms. Portfolio lenders typically follow suit. The result: every unit with a mortgage requires the lender''s written consent. In a 100-unit community where 70 units are mortgaged, 70 separate lenders must consent in writing. Many of those lenders will be aggregators or servicers who do not have a clear process for evaluating dissolution requests; the request often sits in a queue indefinitely.

Some HOAs have tried to dissolve without lender consent on the theory that state law overrides the mortgage. This usually fails — courts treat the mortgage as a contract the borrower agreed to, separate from the state HOA act. A dissolution recorded without lender consent often produces individual loan-acceleration notices to the affected owners.

## The common-area problem

The HOA owns common areas — usually streets, sidewalks, retention ponds, amenities, landscaping, and easements. When the HOA dissolves, this property must transfer somewhere. Each option has problems.

**Conveyance to the city or county.** Most municipalities do not want private streets, retention ponds, or amenities. The infrastructure is usually below city standards (private streets are often narrower, with shallower drainage). Ponds carry Stormwater Phase II compliance obligations the city does not want to inherit. Amenities like pools become municipal liabilities. Most cities decline.

**Sale to a third party.** A sale converts common areas to private property. Proceeds distribute among owners. The buyer may or may not maintain amenities owners assumed would persist. The conversion may also violate restrictive covenants on the original parcel — common areas dedicated as open space in a subdivision plat usually cannot be sold for development.

**Partition among owners as tenants in common.** Each owner gets a fractional interest. Maintenance becomes a coordination problem with no enforcement mechanism. This is rarely workable.

**Successor association.** A new entity limited to common-area maintenance can hold the property. But this is essentially a smaller HOA, which means the dissolution did not really change the ownership structure — it just renamed it.

## Tax and reporting wind-down

The HOA is a corporate entity. Dissolving it requires final tax filings: IRS Form 990 (or 1120-H, depending on filing history) marked "Final Return"; state corporate tax dissolution; cancellation of state corporate registration; final accounting to owners showing the disposition of association funds, reserves, and any liabilities. Reserves typically distribute proportionally among owners after debts are paid, but state HOA acts vary on the disposition rules.

A failed dissolution attempt that does not complete these filings leaves the HOA legally in existence even if it has stopped operating. Owners then face the worst of both worlds: no functioning association to maintain common areas, but continuing legal obligations under the still-recorded CC&Rs.

## When dissolution can actually work

Successful dissolutions share a profile: small community (often under 30 units), few or no mortgages, simple common areas the owners are willing to take as tenants in common, unanimous or near-unanimous owner support, and a willing attorney to manage the conveyance and filings. Even with all those factors, the process commonly runs 18-36 months and costs $20,000-$75,000 in legal and title work.

Boards considering dissolution as an answer to operational dysfunction usually find that fixing the operational issues — better governance, transparent accounting, professional reserve studies, fair enforcement — is faster, cheaper, and more reliable than termination. The fact that the HOA is hard to dissolve is also the fact that makes a properly run HOA durable for the owners who depend on it.
