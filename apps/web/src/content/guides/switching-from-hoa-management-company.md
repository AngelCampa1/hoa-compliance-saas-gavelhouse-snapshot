## Why boards leave management companies

The most common reasons boards we talked to gave for leaving a management company:

- **Cost.** Management fees in the $8 to $25 per unit per month range, plus per-transaction surcharges, plus pass-through costs, often cost a small HOA $25,000 to $80,000 annually for service the board could deliver themselves with $3,000 of software.
- **Responsiveness.** Management companies optimize for portfolio efficiency, not individual community attention. A 60-unit HOA inside a 200-community management portfolio gets the attention its revenue justifies.
- **Misalignment.** Management companies have incentives the board does not — they earn from work orders, vendor relationships, and add-on services. The board's interests and the company's are not always identical.
- **Compliance gaps.** Boards discover during an audit or owner inspection that the management company has been running practices the board would not have approved if asked.

Whatever the reason, the transition itself is what determines whether self-managed is sustainable. Boards that get the transition wrong end up returning to management within 18 months and paying setup fees twice.

## The records retrieval trap

This is where most transitions go badly. The board sends notice, the management company acknowledges, and then the records show up in a format that makes them nearly unusable: PDF screenshots of accounting reports, a single Excel file with multiple years of data flattened into one sheet, owner correspondence delivered as scanned image files without OCR, board meeting minutes missing for two of the last five years.

The contract is the operative document. Many management contracts include language like:

- "Manager shall provide copies of records at termination at Manager's then-prevailing copy charges."
- "Manager shall retain original records for the period required by law and provide copies upon request."
- "Records transfer shall occur within 90 days of termination notice."

None of these clauses are illegal, and most are negotiated into the contract years before the board ever thinks about leaving. By the time the board reads them with intent, it is too late to negotiate. The defensive move: read the contract before sending notice, and budget for the retrieval costs and timeline that the contract actually specifies.

State law sets a floor: association records belong to the association, period. State statutes including California Civil Code 5200, Florida Statute 720.303, and similar provisions in most states grant the association ownership of its records. But statutes establish ownership; they do not always specify format, timeline, or cost of transfer. Contracts can fill the gap in ways that favor the vendor.

The escalation path when a vendor delays or conditions the transfer:

1. **Written demand citing state statute.** Send a formal letter citing the specific statute and the records demanded, with a deadline and delivery method.
2. **Copy state regulator.** In states with a real estate or common interest community regulator (California's DRE-equivalent agencies, Nevada's NRED, Arizona's DRE), copy the regulator on the demand.
3. **File a complaint.** Many state regulators investigate management company conduct on records issues.
4. **Seek injunctive relief.** Where the regulatory path is too slow and a critical deadline (audit, FHA recertification) is approaching, file for an injunction to compel records production.

Most management companies will comply once the demand is formal and copied. The minority that resist are the ones that prove they were the right company to leave.

## Bank account transfers: the 30-day reality

Boards underestimate how long bank account transfers take. The sequence:

1. Open new accounts in the association's name. Requires resolution of the board, EIN, governing documents, and signature cards.
2. Authorize transfer of funds from the management company's trust account. Requires written authorization from the board and the management company's cooperation.
3. Wait for the management company to process the transfer. Can take 5–15 business days even with full cooperation.
4. Reconcile the transfer once funds arrive. Verify the amount matches the closing balance reported by the management company.

Plan 30 days minimum, 45 days realistic. During the window, the association has limited spending capacity. Vendor payments due during the transfer should be flagged in advance.

## Lockbox cutover: the owner communication piece

Owner assessment payments will continue arriving at the old lockbox for 60–90 days regardless of how clearly the board communicates the new instructions. Plan for this:

- Send the new payment instructions to every owner in writing with at least 30 days advance notice.
- Send a second notice 7 days before cutover.
- Send a third notice the day of cutover.
- Maintain a forwarding arrangement with the management company for misdirected payments for at least 90 days.
- Track misdirected payments and resolve them weekly.

The board cannot process the misdirected payments themselves — they hit the management company's account first. The management company has to forward them, and the cooperation level determines whether owners experience late fees they should not face.
