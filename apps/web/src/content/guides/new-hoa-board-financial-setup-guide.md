## What "new board" means for this guide

Three scenarios produce a new HOA board with day-one financial setup work:

- **Newly elected board.** A self-managed HOA holds its annual election; some or all officers turn over. The new officers inherit existing accounts but need to refresh signatures, access, and process documentation.
- **First board after developer turnover.** A new community transitions from developer control to homeowner control. The board inherits whatever the developer set up, which is rarely organized for self-managed operation.
- **First board after leaving management.** An association leaves a management company and the new board takes operational control of finances for the first time.

The three scenarios share the same structural moves but differ in starting state. Newly elected boards work with existing infrastructure; turnover boards rebuild from scratch; post-management boards untangle whatever the management company set up.

## The compliance baseline most boards miss

Self-managed HOA boards are fiduciaries. The fiduciary duty starts the day the new board takes office — not after they "get oriented" or "figure out the existing setup." Day-one moves that document the opening state and establish proper structure are the artifacts that defend the board if a question arises later.

The minimum day-one documentation:

- Signed opening balance memo with bank statements
- Dated copy of governing documents currently in effect
- Copy of the most recent reserve study (or note that no study exists)
- Copy of the most recent audited or reviewed financial statements
- Copy of current insurance certificates (D&O, general liability, fidelity bond)
- List of active vendors with contracts and contact info
- Owner database with current contact info and balances

Most of this exists somewhere. The act of pulling it together and signing a memo of receipt is what creates a defensible baseline.

## Why different banks for operating and reserve

A board can hold operating and reserve at the same bank in two separate accounts and still have legal fund separation. But operational best practice is two banks. The reasons:

- **Pooled cash treatment.** Banks sometimes pool affiliated accounts for cash management purposes. With reserve at a different bank, no pooling can happen.
- **Accidental sweep.** Treasury management features can sweep "excess" balances into a credit line. If the operating account has a credit line, the bank could sweep reserves to pay it down. Different banks eliminates this risk.
- **Reconciliation discipline.** Two separate online banking logins, two separate statement cycles, two separate reconciliations. The administrative friction is mild and produces cleaner audit trails.
- **Diversification.** If a bank has a problem, half the association's cash is somewhere else.

The reserve bank should be a community bank, credit union, or HOA-specialized bank that pays interest on reserve deposits. Major national banks rarely pay competitive rates on the kind of balances most small HOAs hold.

## Chart of accounts: the structure decision

The chart of accounts is the categorization system for every transaction. A purpose-built HOA chart enforces the structure that fund accounting requires. A generic small-business chart does not.

The minimum HOA chart structure:

**Assets:**

- Operating cash (per account)
- Reserve cash (per account)
- Owner receivables — operating
- Reserve interest receivable

**Liabilities:**

- Vendor payables
- Owner credit balances
- Reserve restricted (offsetting reserve cash)

**Equity:**

- Operating fund balance
- Reserve fund balance

**Income:**

- Assessment income — operating
- Assessment income — reserve
- Interest income — operating
- Interest income — reserve
- Late fees, fines, transfer fees

**Expenses (operating):**

- Utilities, landscaping, insurance, administrative, professional fees, repairs, etc.

**Reserve activity:**

- Reserve contributions (transfer in)
- Reserve disbursements (component replacements)

The structure produces separate operating and reserve income statements. Software that enforces this at the database layer makes commingling structurally impossible. Software that treats reserve as a "category" leaves room for tag edits to mix the two.

## The 90-day setup timeline

A practical sequence the new board can run in parallel with regular operations:

**Days 1–7:**

- Document opening balances
- Update bank signature cards
- Notify vendors of new board officers
- Schedule a 30-day kickoff meeting to review setup status

**Days 8–30:**

- Select and onboard HOA software
- Migrate owner ledger and historical data
- Set up payment processing with reserve splits
- Open separate operating/reserve accounts if needed
- Commission reserve study

**Days 31–60:**

- Complete software migration
- Run first month-end close on new system
- Cut over owner payments to new processor
- Receive draft reserve study
- Adopt updated insurance coverage

**Days 61–90:**

- Run second month-end close
- Hold first owner town-hall meeting under new board
- Adopt or update reserve funding plan based on study
- Document policies in writing: collection, reserve transfer, expenditure approval

By day 90, the new board should be running cleanly and have generated 60+ days of audit trail under the new infrastructure. That trail is the artifact that defends the board's tenure regardless of what happens next.

## Where boards trip up in the first year

Common failures we hear from boards after the first year:

- Skipping the reserve study and budgeting from the prior board's numbers, then discovering at audit time that contributions are inadequate.
- Setting up software but not actually doing month-end closes, so the books drift away from reality and reconcile becomes a quarterly emergency.
- Granting permanent admin access to the bookkeeper and forgetting to revoke when the engagement ends.
- Using a personal email address for the treasurer role, which means the next treasurer inherits nothing.
- Failing to document policies in writing, so every decision is relitigated when membership changes.

Each of these is preventable with day-one structural moves. Boards that invest the first 90 days in setup spend the next several years on actual governance. Boards that skip setup spend the next several years cleaning up.
