faqs:
  - q: What is a reserve study?
    a: >-
      A reserve study is an assessment of your community's common elements
      (roofs, paving, pools, elevators, fencing) that estimates their remaining
      useful life and the cost to replace each one. It calculates how much your
      HOA should contribute to the reserve fund each year to have money
      available when replacements come due. States that mandate reserve studies
      typically require them every three to five years.
  - q: Which states require HOA reserve studies?
    a: >-
      Twelve states mandate reserve studies: California, Colorado, Delaware,
      Florida, Hawaii, Maryland, Nevada, Oregon, Tennessee, Utah, Virginia, and
      Washington. Requirements vary. Some states mandate the study only for
      associations above a certain size, others apply to all HOAs and condo
      associations. Florida's requirements are the strictest in the country
      after 2021.
  - q: What happens if a board ignores reserve requirements?
    a: >-
      Consequences depend on the state. In California, an HOA that does not
      maintain a reserve study must disclose that fact to homeowners, and board
      members can face personal liability for damages resulting from inadequate
      reserves. In Florida, condo associations that miss the new post-Surfside
      milestones face restrictions on meetings and assessments. In any state, a
      special assessment that blindsides homeowners because reserves were
      underfunded is the most common catalyst for board member removal and
      litigation.
definitions:
  - term: Reserve Fund
    definition: >-
      A separate savings account maintained by an HOA to cover major repairs and
      replacements of common area components, as required by most state HOA
      statutes. Reserve funds must be kept separate from operating funds and
      funded according to a reserve study schedule.
  - term: Reserve Study
    definition: >-
      An assessment of a community's common elements (roofs, paving, pools,
      elevators, fencing) that estimates their remaining useful life and the
      cost to replace each one. It calculates how much the HOA should contribute
      to the reserve fund each year. States that mandate reserve studies
      typically require them every three to five years.
  - term: Percent Funded
    definition: >-
      The ratio of the current reserve fund balance to the fully funded amount
      recommended by the reserve study. Most reserve studies target 70-100%
      funded. Below 70% indicates underfunding that creates special assessment
      risk and potential personal liability for board members who knew about the
      shortfall.
  - term: Fund Accounting
    definition: >-
      An accounting method that tracks money in separate pools called funds. An
      HOA typically has an operating fund and a reserve fund, each with its own
      balance and reports. Fund accounting keeps these separate at the ledger
      level, satisfying state reserve disclosure requirements.

  - question: What is a reserve study and why does it matter?
    answer: >-
      A reserve study assesses your community's common elements, estimates their
      remaining useful life, and calculates the annual contribution needed to
      fund replacements. It provides the baseline for determining whether your
      reserve fund is adequately funded and satisfies state disclosure
      requirements that ask boards to report funding levels.
  - question: How do you fix reserve fund commingling in QuickBooks?
    answer: >-
      If your board uses QuickBooks or a single general ledger for both
      operating expenses and reserve savings, switching to purpose-built HOA
      accounting software with native fund accounting is the cleaner path. In
      QuickBooks, mimicking fund accounting with class tracking is possible
      but fragile and prone to entry errors that bypass fund separation.
  - question: How often should a board review reserve fund status?
    answer: >-
      Reserve fund status should be reviewed at every board meeting, not just at
      year-end. The reserve balance should be reported as a percentage of the
      reserve study's recommended fully-funded amount. Reserve studies should be
      updated every three to five years, or annually in some states.

steps:
  - title: Check your state's requirements
    content: >-
      Reserve requirements vary significantly. Twelve states mandate reserve
      studies: California, Colorado, Delaware, Florida, Hawaii, Maryland,
      Nevada, Oregon, Tennessee, Utah, Virginia, and Washington. Beyond those
      twelve, many states require reserve fund disclosures in annual financial
      reports without mandating a formal study. Your state HOA statute is the
      authoritative source. Search for your state name plus 'HOA reserve fund
      statute' to find the relevant section. Florida's requirements deserve
      separate attention if you manage a condo association. After the 2021
      Surfside building collapse, Florida passed SB 4-D, which requires condo
      associations in buildings three stories or higher to complete structural
      integrity reserve studies by December 31, 2024, and begin fully funding
      those reserves by December 31, 2025. Milestone inspection deadlines also
      apply. These are hard legal dates, not guidelines.
  - title: Audit your current reserve balance
    content: >-
      Pull your current reserve fund balance from your bank statements and
      accounting records. Compare it to the recommended funding level from your
      most recent reserve study. The ratio between your current balance and the
      fully funded amount is your percent funded. Most reserve studies target
      70-100% funded. Below 70%, your board should discuss a funding plan. Below
      50%, you have underfunding that creates both special assessment risk and
      potential personal liability for board members who knew about the
      shortfall and did not act. If your board has never conducted a reserve
      study, you do not have a baseline to compare against. Budget for one
      before your next annual meeting.
  - title: Fix your accounting to separate funds
    content: >-
      If your board uses QuickBooks or a single general ledger to track both
      operating expenses and reserve savings, you have commingling risk.
      Commingling means operating funds and reserve funds are not kept separate
      at the accounting level. A transfer to reserves is just an internal
      transaction, not a segregated fund. States that require reserve fund
      disclosures expect to see your reserve balance as a distinct figure. Fund
      accounting (where operating and reserve funds are tracked as separate
      pools with independent balances and reports) is the correct structure for
      HOA finances. Fixing this in QuickBooks is possible but requires careful
      chart-of-accounts reconfiguration that many QuickBooks setups do not have.
      Purpose-built HOA accounting tools with native fund accounting are the
      cleaner path.
  - title: Set up annual review triggers
    content: >-
      Reserve compliance is not a one-time fix. Your reserve study should be
      updated every three to five years (annually in some states). Your reserve
      fund balance should be reviewed at every board meeting, not just at
      year-end. Set calendar reminders for reserve study renewal, annual budget
      approval (which should include a reserve contribution line), and any state
      filing deadlines. Track the reserve balance as a percentage of the reserve
      study's recommended fully-funded amount, and report that figure in your
      board meeting minutes. If you cannot answer 'what is our current
      percent-funded?' at any board meeting, your tracking process needs work.
      Board members who cannot answer that question and face a special
      assessment dispute later are in a harder legal position than those who
      tracked it and documented the review.
