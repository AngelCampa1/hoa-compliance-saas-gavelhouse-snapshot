faqs:
  - q: What is an HOA estoppel letter?
    a: >-
      An HOA estoppel letter, also called a statement of account or estoppel
      certificate, is a written document the association provides to a seller,
      buyer, or lender at or before closing. It certifies the current assessment
      balance on a specific unit, any delinquent amounts, outstanding fines,
      pending special assessments, capital contributions, transfer fees, and any
      other amounts the new owner will be responsible for. The document is
      binding: if the estoppel understates what is owed, the association is
      generally barred from collecting the understated amount from the
      purchaser.
  - q: How long does an HOA have to respond to an estoppel request in Florida?
    a: >-
      Florida Statutes 720.30851 (HOAs) and 718.116(8) (condominiums) require
      the association to provide the estoppel certificate within 10 business
      days of receiving the written request. An association that fails to
      deliver within 10 business days forfeits the right to charge a fee for the
      estoppel. For expedited delivery within 3 business days, the statute
      allows an additional fee up to $100 on top of the standard cap.
  - q: What is the maximum fee an HOA can charge for an estoppel in Florida?
    a: >-
      Florida caps the estoppel fee at $299 for a unit with no outstanding
      delinquency. If the account is delinquent, the cap rises to $349. An
      additional $100 may be charged for expedited delivery within 3 business
      days. These caps apply to both HOAs under Chapter 720 and condominiums
      under Chapter 718. An association cannot charge more than these statutory
      caps regardless of what the governing documents say.
  - q: What does Texas law require for HOA estoppel certificates?
    a: >-
      Texas Property Code Section 209.004 requires a property owners association
      to provide a resale certificate within 7 days of receiving a written
      request. The fee is capped at $375. The resale certificate must include
      current assessment amounts, any unpaid fees, pending special assessments,
      any violations the association is aware of, and certain governing document
      information. Texas also requires the association to provide an update
      certificate at no additional charge if any information changes before
      closing.
  - q: What does California require for HOA disclosure at a resale?
    a: >-
      California Civil Code Section 4528 (Davis-Stirling Act) requires the
      association to provide a disclosure package to the seller within 10 days
      of a written request. The package includes the current assessment amounts,
      any delinquent assessments on the unit, pending special assessments,
      pending litigation, the most recent reserve study summary, and the current
      operating and reserve fund balances. California does not set a single fee
      cap by statute - the association may charge a fee reasonable to cover the
      actual cost of preparation.
  - q: What happens if the HOA makes an error in the estoppel letter?
    a: >-
      A materially incorrect estoppel letter binds the association by the
      doctrine of estoppel. If the letter understates the assessment balance and
      a buyer closes in reliance on that figure, the association cannot pursue
      the buyer for the understated amount. The loss falls on the association,
      which means it falls on the remaining homeowners through higher
      assessments or a write-off. The association may have a claim against the
      individual who prepared or signed an incorrect estoppel, which is why
      board members and managers should treat estoppel preparation with the same
      care as financial reporting.
  - q: Who is authorized to sign an HOA estoppel letter?
    a: >-
      Most state statutes and governing documents authorize the estoppel to be
      signed by an officer of the association (president, treasurer, or
      secretary) or by an authorized management company representative. Florida
      520.30851 allows the association's authorized agent to execute the
      certificate. Because the document is binding, the signer should have
      direct access to the association's accounting records and should verify
      each figure before signing. Do not delegate estoppel preparation to a
      volunteer without accounting access.
  - q: Does an estoppel letter expire?
    a: >-
      Yes. Florida estoppel certificates are effective for 30 days if delivered
      electronically and 35 days if delivered by mail. If closing does not occur
      within that window, a new estoppel is required. Texas requires an update
      certificate if any information in the original changes before closing.
      California's disclosure package does not carry a fixed statutory
      expiration but should be refreshed if assessments or violations change
      materially before closing. Most title companies and lenders require a
      fresh estoppel if more than 30 days have passed.
  - q: Can the HOA charge a fee for an estoppel?
    a: >-
      Yes, within statutory limits. Florida caps fees at $299 to $349 plus a
      $100 expedite option. Texas caps at $375. California allows a reasonable
      fee to cover preparation costs but does not set a statutory maximum. The
      fee is typically paid by the seller at or before closing. An association
      that misses the statutory response deadline in Florida forfeits the right
      to collect any fee. Boards should set a policy on estoppel fees in their
      annual resolution and document it in the collection policy.
  - q: What information must the estoppel letter include?
    a: >-
      At minimum, a complete estoppel should include the unit address and legal
      description, current owner of record, total monthly or quarterly
      assessment amount, the assessment due date, any unpaid assessments with
      the amount and dates outstanding, any fines or charges outstanding, any
      pending special assessments (amount and expected due date), any capital
      contribution or transfer fee required at closing, the name and contact of
      the person or management company preparing the certificate, the date of
      preparation, and the expiration date of the certificate. Florida and Texas
      statutes specify required fields in detail - boards should use a form
      that mirrors the statutory checklist.

Most HOA boards do not think much about estoppel letters until a title company calls with a 3-day deadline and no one knows who is supposed to prepare it. At that point the board discovers that what looks like a simple form is actually a binding legal document - one where a single data-entry error can cost the association thousands of dollars in uncollectable assessments.

We built Gavelhouse partly because we kept seeing self-managed communities absorb collection losses that had nothing to do with owner bad faith. The owner paid. The estoppel was wrong. The association wrote off the gap. A better process would have caught it.

This guide covers what an estoppel letter must contain, statutory response deadlines and fee caps in Florida, Texas, and California, who should sign it, and what goes wrong when boards treat it as an afterthought.

## What an estoppel letter actually does

An estoppel certificate is a snapshot of financial reality on a unit at a specific point in time. The title company uses it to confirm that the buyer will not inherit undisclosed assessment debt and to collect any outstanding balances or closing-day fees from the seller's proceeds.

The document carries legal force because of the doctrine of estoppel: the association cannot later assert a position inconsistent with what it certified. If the estoppel states the unit owes $0 in assessments and the buyer closes in reliance on that, the association cannot pursue the buyer for $2,400 that was actually outstanding. The loss is the association's. The remaining homeowners cover it through slightly higher future assessments or a write-off.

This is what makes estoppel preparation a fiduciary task, not a clerical one. The person who prepares and signs the certificate is making a representation that binds the entire association.

## What the letter must contain

State law defines the minimum contents. A complete estoppel should include every field on the statutory checklist for your state, plus anything the governing documents require.

The core fields that appear in Florida, Texas, and California requirements:

- Unit address and legal description or lot number
- Current owner of record
- Regular assessment amount and frequency (monthly, quarterly, annually)
- Assessment due date and grace period before late fees apply
- Any unpaid assessments - amount and the dates they became due
- Any outstanding fines or violation charges
- Any pending special assessments - the amount, purpose, and expected collection date
- Capital contribution or working capital fee due at closing, if applicable
- Transfer fee, if applicable
- Name, contact, and signature of the authorized preparer
- Date the certificate was prepared
- Expiration date

Florida adds a requirement to disclose whether the association is subject to a pending lawsuit. Texas requires disclosure of violations the association is aware of for the unit. California's disclosure package is the broadest - it includes the reserve study summary, reserve account balance, operating budget, and pending litigation.

Do not abbreviate the disclosure because the form feels long. A buyer or their attorney who discovers undisclosed amounts after closing has a clear legal basis to challenge the transfer. The association's defense - that it was trying to save time - will not hold up.

## State response timeframes and fee caps

The table below summarizes statutory requirements for the three states with the most self-managed HOA activity.

| State           | Statute            | Standard deadline | Fee cap (standard)                | Expedite option           |
| --------------- | ------------------ | ----------------- | --------------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| Florida (HOA)   | F.S. 720.30851     | 10 business days  | $299 (current), $349 (delinquent) | +$100 for 3 business days |
| Florida (Condo) | F.S. 718.116(8)    | 10 business days  | $299 (current), $349 (delinquent) | +$100 for 3 business days |
| Texas           | Prop. Code 209.004 | 7 days            | $375                              | Not separately defined    |
| California      | Civ. Code 4528     | 10 days           | Reasonable actual cost            | Not separately defined    |

**Florida** is the most prescriptive. The statute defines the response window as 10 business days from receipt of a written request. An association that misses the window forfeits the right to collect any fee - it must still deliver the certificate, it just cannot charge for it. Expedited delivery within 3 business days carries a surcharge up to $100. The certificate is effective for 30 days if delivered electronically or 35 days if mailed. A new certificate is required if the window expires before closing.

**Texas** gives associations 7 days. The resale certificate must cover not just financial balances but also any violations the association is currently pursuing against the unit. Texas also requires the association to provide a no-charge update if any information in the original certificate changes before closing. This means the association needs a process to catch post-issuance changes - an approved special assessment, a new fine, a payment that posts or bounces - and reissue the certificate before the closing date.

**California** under the Davis-Stirling Act takes a broader approach. The disclosure package is not just a financial statement - it is a package that includes the governing documents, a summary of the reserve study findings, the current reserve balance and percent-funded figure, the most recent operating budget, and pending litigation. The 10-day clock runs from written request. California does not set a statutory fee cap, but the fee must reflect actual preparation costs.

For states not listed - Arizona, Nevada, Washington, and others - the applicable statutes vary. If your community operates in a state not covered here, pull the applicable property owners association or condominium act from the state legislature's website and build your response process around those requirements.

## Common errors that create liability

**Stale data from a separate ledger.** The most frequent error is a preparer who pulls the assessment balance from a spreadsheet that does not match the actual accounting records. If payments post to the bank but the spreadsheet has not been updated, the estoppel understates the balance. The association issued a binding certification based on bad data.

**Omitting pending special assessments.** A board that has passed a motion to levy a special assessment - even if the first payment is not due for four months - must disclose it. An assessment is "pending" once authorized. A buyer who closes without knowing about a $3,000 special assessment coming due in 90 days will have a strong argument that the association failed to disclose.

**Wrong unit.** In communities with multiple buildings or similar street addresses, it is possible to pull the wrong unit's record. A certificate that certifies the wrong unit's balance is still binding as issued - the association bears the consequences of its own error.

**Missing transfer fee or capital contribution.** These closing-day charges are often set in a separate resolution rather than the main assessment schedule. A preparer who only looks at the recurring assessment line misses them. The title company relies on the estoppel to collect these amounts; if they are not on the certificate, they typically are not collected at closing.

**Signing without authority.** A certificate signed by someone who is not an officer or authorized agent of the association may not be binding in the way the association intends - but it may still be binding on the association under apparent authority principles. Boards should document in a board resolution who is authorized to execute estoppel certificates.

## Who signs the estoppel

Florida and Texas statutes authorize the association's authorized agent to execute the certificate. Most governing documents authorize any officer of the association - president, treasurer, or secretary - to act on the association's behalf in transactions of this kind.

In practice, the treasurer is the most appropriate signer because the treasurer has direct access to accounting records and can verify figures before certifying them. The secretary may prepare the document and the treasurer signs after verifying the account balances.

If the community uses a management company, the management agreement typically grants the manager authority to prepare and execute estoppels on the association's behalf. The manager must be using current, reconciled records - not a ledger that has not been updated since last month's board meeting.

Whoever signs the estoppel should be able to answer these questions before signing:

- What is the current assessment balance in the accounting system, not a separate spreadsheet?
- Have all payments received through today been applied?
- Has the board authorized any special assessments in the last 90 days?
- Are there any outstanding fines on this unit in the violation tracking system?
- Does the closing day fall before or after the next assessment due date?

If any of these questions cannot be answered with certainty, the estoppel is not ready to sign.

## The case for keeping estoppel data in the accounting system

The root cause of most estoppel errors is that assessment tracking lives in one place - a spreadsheet, a QuickBooks file, a notebook - and the board treats estoppel preparation as pulling data out of that system manually, introducing the opportunity for transcription error.

## What to do when a request comes in

Most closing requests arrive with a title company deadline. The request typically specifies the closing date and asks for delivery by a certain date. Here is the checklist:

1. Confirm the request is in writing - verbal requests do not start the statutory clock.
2. Log the receipt date and calculate the response deadline (10 business days in Florida, 7 days in Texas, 10 days in California).
3. Identify the unit and pull the current account balance from the accounting system, not from memory or a side spreadsheet.
4. Apply any payments received since the last statement.
5. Check the violation records for any open fines on the unit.
6. Check board minutes for any special assessments authorized in the past 12 months that have not yet been fully collected.
7. Confirm the capital contribution and transfer fee amounts from the current fee schedule.
8. Prepare the certificate using a form that meets the statutory requirements for your state.
9. Have the authorized officer review and sign.
10. Deliver within the statutory window and retain a copy with the delivery confirmation.

If the request comes in with a 3-business-day deadline and your state allows an expedite fee, charge it. The fee exists precisely because fast turnaround requires prioritizing the task over other board work.

## Recordkeeping after closing

Retain a copy of every estoppel certificate you issue. The retention period should match your state's statute of limitations for contract claims - typically 4 to 6 years. If a dispute arises after closing about what was disclosed, you will need the signed certificate with its delivery timestamp. Digital records stored in the same system as the accounting records are preferable to paper copies that can be lost.

Update your records when ownership transfers. The new owner's account should show the transfer date, the balance at transfer, and the estoppel certificate number so you can trace back to the original disclosure if needed.

The estoppel is the association's public commitment to what it is owed. Preparing it well, delivering it on time, and keeping a clean record is part of running the association like the business it is.
