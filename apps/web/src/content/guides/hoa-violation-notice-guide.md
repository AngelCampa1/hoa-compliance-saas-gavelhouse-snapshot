A violation notice is not just a courtesy letter. It is a legal document that initiates a formal enforcement process. If it fails to meet procedural requirements, the entire enforcement action--including any subsequent fine--can be thrown out. Getting the notice right is not optional.

## What a Valid Violation Notice Must Include

Every element of the notice serves a function. Missing even one can create a successful challenge.

**Identification of the property and owner.** The notice must go to the right person. Use the county records to confirm the current owner's name and mailing address. If the property is rented, the owner--not the tenant--receives the notice. Include the property address in the notice itself.

**Description of the specific violation.** Vague descriptions fail. Not "your property is not in compliance" but "the grass on your front lawn has exceeded the maximum height of six inches permitted under Section 4.2 of the Declaration." Describe what you observed, when you observed it, and where on the property the violation exists.

**Citation to the governing document provision.** Name the specific document (Declaration, Rules and Regulations, etc.) and the specific section number or provision that the conduct violates. Do not paraphrase or summarize--cite directly so the homeowner can look it up. "Violation of Section 4.2 of the Declaration of Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions, recorded at Book 1234, Page 567 of the county recorder's records."

**The cure deadline.** A specific calendar date, not a relative reference like "within 30 days." Set the date from the delivery date of the notice, not the sending date. If you send the notice via certified mail and it is not delivered for three days, the cure period starts on delivery.

**The fine amount.** State the dollar amount that will be imposed if the violation is not cured by the deadline. This must be consistent with your adopted fine schedule. If your fine schedule has not been formally adopted and published, you have a problem--fines imposed without a pre-existing schedule are vulnerable to challenge.

**Hearing rights notice.** The owner must be informed of their right to request a hearing before the board to contest the violation or fine. Include the deadline for requesting a hearing (typically five to ten days before the fine date) and instructions for how to make the request.

**Contact information.** Who the homeowner should contact with questions, and how. Name, address, phone, email.

## How to Document Violations Before Sending Notice

The notice is only as strong as the underlying documentation. A homeowner who disputes whether a violation occurred will challenge your evidence.

**Photograph the violation.** Take multiple photos from different angles. Ensure the date and time stamp on your camera or phone is accurate--this creates a record of when the violation existed. Include a photo that shows the property address (a street sign or house number in the frame) to establish location.

**Note the inspection method.** Was the violation discovered during a routine community inspection, reported by a neighbor, or observed during a board member's drive-through? Document which. If it was a neighbor complaint, note the complaint but do not identify the neighbor by name in the violation notice--that can create privacy issues and inflame the dispute.

**Keep a violation log.** Every violation should have a written record entry: the date first observed, the location, the provision violated, photos attached, and the enforcement steps taken. This log becomes your evidence file if the dispute escalates.

## Delivery Requirements

The delivery method for a violation notice is not just a formality--it is a legal requirement with real consequences.

**Certified mail with return receipt.** This is the gold standard and is required or strongly preferred in most states. The green card (or electronic confirmation) from USPS proves the date of delivery, which starts the cure period and documents service in a dispute.

**Personal service.** Delivery of the notice directly to the owner (or a responsible adult at the property) by a board member, management company representative, or process server. Requires a signed certificate of service documenting the date, time, location, and who received it.

**Posting.** Some states permit posting the notice at the property if certified mail is returned undelivered. Check your state law before relying on posting alone.

**Email.** Only valid where the owner has provided written consent to receive notices electronically. Do not use email as the primary delivery method for notices with fine consequences unless you have documented consent.

Nevada NRS 116 sets specific delivery requirements for Nevada community associations. California's Davis-Stirling Act has its own notice requirements. Know the statute in your state before choosing a delivery method.

## What Happens When Homeowners Ignore Violation Notices

Non-response is not an unusual outcome. Homeowners sometimes hope that if they do not acknowledge the notice, the issue will go away.

It should not. Document that the cure deadline passed without the violation being cured. Take follow-up photographs after the cure deadline to prove the violation continued. Then proceed with the next enforcement step per your governing documents.

If the violation is still unresolved after the cure deadline:

1. Formally impose the fine in writing, referencing the original notice and confirming that the violation was not cured.
2. If the homeowner has not requested a hearing, note that the hearing right was provided and not exercised.
3. Send the fine notice with a payment deadline.
4. If the fine is unpaid after the payment deadline, consider escalation--additional fines per your progressive schedule, suspension of amenity access, or in appropriate cases, a lien on the property.

Each step requires its own written documentation.

## Common Mistakes That Invalidate Violation Notices

**Using vague language.** "Your property does not meet HOA standards" is not an enforceable violation notice. Be specific.

**Failing to cite the governing document provision.** Without a citation, the homeowner cannot verify what rule they allegedly violated, and a court cannot determine whether the rule exists.

**Setting cure deadlines that start at the sending date rather than delivery.** The cure period must give the homeowner a realistic opportunity to comply. If the notice takes five days to deliver and the cure period is ten days, the homeowner effectively has five days--which may not be enough.

**Skipping the hearing rights notice.** Even if you are confident the violation is clear-cut, you must inform the homeowner of their right to contest. A fine imposed without informing the homeowner of their hearing rights is legally vulnerable in almost every state.

**Sending to the wrong address.** If the property is rented, the owner's mailing address in the county records may differ from the property address. Always send to the owner's address of record, not just the property.

With 365,000+ HOAs nationwide and 74 million Americans in HOA communities (CAI, 2024), these procedural failures generate a steady stream of litigation. The associations that defend enforcement challenges successfully are the ones that document carefully and follow their process exactly.
