# HOA New Homeowner Welcome Packet: Required Disclosures and Delivery Timing

We built Gavelhouse because self-managed boards keep getting tripped up by procedural requirements that nobody trained them on. The welcome packet is one of those requirements — quietly mandatory in several states, easy to forget in a busy month, and a direct path to personal liability when a board signs a clean resale certificate while a homeowner never received the documents that explain what they bought.

This guide covers what belongs in the packet, when it must be delivered, and how to document the delivery so it can survive a challenge.

## Why the welcome packet matters

The welcome packet does three things at once:

1. **Satisfies state disclosure law** — California, Florida, Nevada, Texas, and several other states require specific documents to be delivered to new owners within statutory windows.
2. **Creates enforceability** — a homeowner who never received the parking rule cannot be reasonably fined for violating it. The packet establishes notice.
3. **Protects the board from misrepresentation claims** — when the resale package and the welcome packet together accurately reflect the community's financial condition, buyers cannot later claim they were misled.

Boards that skip the packet often save themselves an hour of work and inherit thousands of dollars of risk.

## What the packet should contain

Every welcome packet should include:

### Governing documents

- Current CC&Rs (with all recorded amendments)
- Current bylaws
- Current rules and regulations

### Financial documents

- Current annual budget
- Most recent financial statements (typically last 12 months)
- Reserve study summary or reserve disclosure
- Schedule of regular dues and any pending special assessments

### Operational information

- Board roster with contact information
- Management company contact information (if applicable)
- Dues payment instructions and methods
- Architectural review process and forms
- Violation procedure and fine schedule

### Insurance information

- Summary of master insurance policy (what it covers)
- Coverage gaps requiring homeowner HO-6 or similar
- Claim procedure

### State-specific disclosures

- California: Annual Budget Report and Annual Policy Statement (Civil Code 5300, 5310)
- Florida: Q&A summary, structural and reserve disclosures (post-Surfside)
- Nevada: Public Offering Statement equivalents (NRS 116)
- Other states: check local requirements

## State-specific timing rules

### California

Civil Code 5300 requires the Annual Budget Report and Annual Policy Statement to be distributed annually. For new owners, the resale disclosure package (often delivered through escrow) covers most documents at closing. After closing, the association generally has 5-10 business days to provide additional documents on request, and the new owner enters the regular annual distribution cycle.

### Florida

Florida Statute 720 (HOAs) and 718 (condos) require specific disclosures during sale and to new owners. Post-Surfside legislation tightened condo structural and reserve disclosure requirements. New condo owners must receive the latest milestone inspection summary and reserve disclosure.

### Nevada

NRS 116 requires associations to provide governing documents and current financials to new owners within set timeframes after transfer notice.

### Texas

Property Code Chapter 209 requires resale certificates with specific content. The certificate is typically delivered through closing, and the welcome packet supplements it.

### Other states

Even where state law is silent, governing documents often require some form of new-owner disclosure. Check your CC&Rs and bylaws.

## Physical vs digital delivery

Both work when state law permits.

### Physical

- Certified mail with return receipt
- Hand delivery with signature
- Pros: universally accepted, simple proof of delivery
- Cons: printing cost, slower update cycles, easier to lose

### Digital

- Email or portal delivery with verifiable receipt
- California, Florida, and several other states permit electronic delivery with proper opt-in
- Pros: lower cost, instant updates, automatic audit trail
- Cons: requires opt-in consent, technical reliability matters

Communities that go digital generally need to confirm:

1. State law permits electronic delivery for the documents in question
2. The homeowner has formally consented to electronic notice
3. The platform produces a verifiable delivery record

## Common failure modes

### No process at all

The most common failure: no one is responsible for sending welcome packets. Some new owners get them by accident, others never do. Enforcement weakens because notice is inconsistent.

### Out-of-date packet

Boards build a template once and never update it. New owners receive expired budgets, superseded rules, or pre-amendment CC&Rs. When the homeowner relies on the outdated rule, the board has weakened its own enforcement.

### No proof of delivery

The packet went out, but nobody saved the receipt. Two years later, the homeowner challenges a fine claiming they never received the rule. The board cannot prove otherwise.

### Missing state disclosures

The packet has the basics but skips state-specific requirements (California reserve disclosure, Florida structural disclosure). The board has technically failed compliance even though the new owner got "a packet."

## Documentation discipline

Every welcome packet sent should produce a record:

- Date of delivery
- Method of delivery
- Documents included (versioned by date)
- Acknowledgment form returned (if used)

Keep these records for the statutory retention period — typically 3-7 years depending on state. Many associations keep them for the duration of ownership plus 7 years.

## The acknowledgment form

A simple acknowledgment form returned by the new owner — confirming receipt of CC&Rs, rules, and key disclosures — strengthens every future enforcement action. It also prompts the new owner to actually read the documents.

The form does not need to be elaborate. One page, clear list of documents acknowledged, signature, date.

## The compliance lens

The welcome packet is small enough to seem like a courtesy and big enough to create real liability when skipped. Boards that build a maintained template, trigger delivery automatically on transfer notice, and document receipt rarely face notice or disclosure challenges.

Boards that handle it ad-hoc — printing whatever is on the desktop the day they remember — eventually inherit a homeowner who claims they never received the rule, the fee schedule, or the reserve disclosure. By then, the cost of the missing documentation dwarfs the cost of building the process correctly.
