## Why Your Rental Cap Policy Is a Financing Decision, Not Just a Lifestyle Decision

Most boards think about rental caps in terms of community character — owner-occupied neighbors tend to invest more in shared spaces, take better care of common areas, and attend meetings. That framing isn't wrong, but it misses the bigger structural risk: the FHA certification threshold.

Under HUD guidelines, a condominium or HOA project loses FHA project approval when more than 35% of units are renter-occupied or investor-owned. Once that threshold is crossed, no buyer in the community — not the person buying unit 1, not the person buying unit 47 — can use an FHA-insured mortgage. FHA loans represent roughly 12–15% of total purchase mortgage volume nationally, and a higher share among first-time buyers who drive entry-level demand. Losing FHA eligibility means losing that entire segment of the buyer market, which puts downward pressure on prices for every owner.

The rental cap policy your board sets today directly controls whether your community stays under that 35% threshold tomorrow.

## How FHA Calculates Rental Concentration

FHA counts any unit not occupied by the owner as a primary residence toward the concentration ratio. That includes:

- Units rented to long-term tenants
- Units listed on short-term rental platforms (Airbnb, VRBO) and not owner-occupied
- Vacant units owned by investors
- Units in foreclosure proceedings owned by banks or other entities

What does not count: units occupied by owners who also rent out a bedroom (owner-occupied with a tenant in part of the unit is still counted as owner-occupied).

The calculation is straightforward: non-owner-occupied units ÷ total units = rental concentration percentage. HUD uses this figure when evaluating or renewing FHA project approval, and lenders running their own spot checks may pull it as well.

### The Practical Threshold to Target

At 35% you lose certification. At 30% you have meaningful headroom. Most communities targeting long-term FHA eligibility run a formal rental cap in the 25–30% range, which gives the board enforcement authority before the FHA threshold is reached rather than after.

## State-Specific Rental Cap Restrictions

### California

California Civil Code Section 4741, added in 2012, prohibits HOAs from adopting or enforcing CC&R provisions that prohibit the rental of a separate interest entirely, or that impose a rental cap below 25% of units. The statute applies to all associations in California regardless of when they were formed.

Any CC&R provision that sets a lower cap or a total rental ban is void and unenforceable. Courts will not enforce it, and owners who are denied the right to rent can seek declaratory relief.

SB 9, enacted in 2021, complicates rental cap math in single-family HOAs. The law allows lot splitting and the construction of additional units on many residential parcels. HOAs cannot prohibit SB 9 development outright, but they can apply reasonable architectural and design standards. New SB 9 units count as separate interests for FHA and rental concentration purposes, potentially pushing a community toward the 35% threshold without any change in board policy.

### Florida

Chapter 720, Florida Statutes governs HOAs (condos fall under Chapter 718). For HOAs, any new rental restriction or cap must either be in the original declaration or be added by a recorded amendment approved by the membership. The board cannot impose a rental restriction by resolution alone.

Florida added important protections for existing owners in 2021: if an HOA adopts a rental restriction amendment after July 1, 2021, owners who voted against the amendment or did not participate in the vote retain the right to rent under the prior rules for as long as they own the unit. The restriction applies to future purchasers but not to owners who were grandfathered under the old rules. Boards drafting Florida rental cap amendments must account for this two-tier ownership structure.

### Other Notable States

Nevada requires a 67% membership vote to add rental restrictions to CC&Rs. Arizona HOA statutes (ARS Title 33, Chapter 16) do not cap how restrictive rental policies can be, but require all restrictions to be in the recorded CC&Rs. Texas follows a similar pattern — restrictions must be in the declaration, adopted by the required member vote percentage specified in the existing CC&Rs.

## Amending Your CC&Rs to Add or Modify a Rental Cap

### What the Amendment Must Cover

A well-drafted rental cap clause defines:

1. **Scope of the restriction** — does the cap apply to long-term leases only, or does it also cover short-term rentals? Define "rental" in the amendment.
2. **The cap percentage** — expressed as a maximum percentage of total units, not a maximum number (unit counts can change).
3. **Calculation methodology** — how often is the count taken, who maintains the registry, and what evidence satisfies the board that a unit is or is not renter-occupied?
4. **Application process** — if demand for rental exceeds the cap, do you maintain a waiting list? First-come-first-served? Preference for existing owners converting to rental vs. new purchasers?
5. **Grandfathering** — how are current renters and current rental agreements handled on the effective date?
6. **Hardship exceptions** — does the board have discretion to approve rentals above the cap in documented hardship cases?

### The Voting and Recording Process

Check your existing CC&Rs for the amendment threshold. If the CC&Rs are silent or your state has a default, most states require between 67% and 75% of all voting interests (not just a quorum). "All voting interests" means the denominator is the total unit count, not the number of owners who show up.

Advance notice is required — typically 14 days minimum, often 30 days for amendments. Send notice by mail or the method specified in your CC&Rs, and document delivery carefully.

After the vote, the amendment must be notarized and recorded with the county recorder. A rental cap in a written board resolution that was never recorded in the CC&Rs has no binding effect on future purchasers and is difficult to enforce against current owners.

## When the Cap Is Hit: Enforcement and Waiting Lists

A rental cap that cannot be enforced is worse than no cap at all — it creates the appearance of control without the substance, and owners who are denied a rental slot they expected may have legal claims if the cap isn't administered consistently.

Establish a written process before the cap fills:

- A centralized rental registry maintained by the board or management company
- A documented waiting list managed on a first-request basis with timestamped entries
- A written approval or denial letter for every rental request
- Annual recertification of all active rentals to confirm the unit remains renter-occupied

Consistent administration protects the board. Inconsistent administration — approving some rentals above the cap informally while denying others — creates selective enforcement claims that are both expensive to defend and often losable.

## Balancing FHA Compliance Against Community Character Goals

A rental cap set too low will suppress FHA eligibility (you need to stay below 35%, not just at 35%), but setting it too high defeats the board's goal of maintaining owner-occupancy ratios. The practical range most HOA attorneys recommend is 20–30% depending on the community's starting point and the state's floor.
