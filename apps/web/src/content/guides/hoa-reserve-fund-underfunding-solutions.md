## Why underfunding is a board-level emergency

Most underfunded HOAs got there gradually. A reserve study from 2018 said contribute $74,000 a year; the board contributed $52,000 because raising assessments was politically hard. Inflation and deferred contributions compounded. By 2026, the study has not been updated, the actual contribution gap has grown, and the funded ratio is sitting at 28%. Nobody on the current board was on the board in 2018, but they inherited the problem and now own the liability.

This is the most common pattern we hear from treasurers who reach out about Gavelhouse. The remediation playbook below is the one we recommend, and it is the one that defends the board against personal liability claims if a major component fails before reserves recover.

## The funded ratio thresholds that matter

The reserve study profession uses three thresholds to characterize funded status:

- **Above 70% funded:** Strong. Special assessments are unlikely; the funding plan can run on autopilot.
- **30–70% funded:** Fair. The board should be running a multi-year funding plan to move the ratio upward.
- **Below 30% funded:** Weak. Special assessments and deferred components are likely. State disclosure requirements may apply.

Lenders use these same thresholds. Fannie Mae's project review guidelines flag projects with reserves below 10% of annual operating budget — a different formula than funded ratio, but pointed at the same problem. FHA condo project approval similarly requires reserve adequacy. A community below 30% funded faces both a fiduciary duty problem and a marketability problem: buyers cannot get conventional financing.

## State-reportable disclosure requirements

The states with the strictest reserve disclosure rules:

- **California** (Civil Code 5550, 5300, 5565): Annual reserve study every three years, on-site update annually. Annual budget disclosure must include funded ratio, recommended contribution, actual contribution, and any special assessment plan.
- **Florida** (Statute 720.303 for HOAs, 718.112 for condos): Annual budget must include reserve schedules. Reserve waivers require member vote and disclosure to subsequent buyers. Statute 718.112(2)(f) requires a Structural Integrity Reserve Study (SIRS) for buildings three stories or higher.
- **Nevada** (NRS 116.31152): Reserve study every five years, updated annually. Annual budget must include reserve allocation.
- **Arizona** (ARS 33-1804 for condos): Disclosure of reserves to prospective buyers; inadequate reserves must be disclosed in the resale package.

In each of these states, the failure mode that creates personal liability is not "we are underfunded." It is "we are underfunded and the board failed to disclose, study, or adopt a remediation plan." The plan is the defense.

## The three-tier remediation plan, with numbers

A worked example for a 60-unit HOA with a $720,000 annual operating budget, currently 22% funded, with a reserve study showing a $94,000 annual contribution gap and $1.4M in components scheduled for replacement in the next ten years.

**Tier 1 — Increase reserve contribution.** Raise monthly assessments by $65 per unit, generating $46,800 annually in new reserve contributions. This closes about half the contribution gap. Document the increase in the annual budget filed with the state.

**Tier 2 — Engineer-reviewed deferrals.** Engage a reserve specialist to identify non-critical components that can be deferred 18–36 months without safety risk. In our example, deferring two scheduled exterior paint cycles by 18 months and one elevator modernization by 24 months might free $180,000 in near-term reserve demand. The deferrals are documented in writing with engineer sign-off.

**Tier 3 — Special assessment.** A $4,500-per-unit special assessment payable over 36 months ($125 per unit per month) generates $270,000 over the assessment period. Combined with Tier 1 and Tier 2, the funded ratio reaches 52% by year three.

The plan is adopted by board vote in open session, distributed to owners in writing, and filed with state disclosures where applicable. This is the artifact that defends the board.

## Lines of credit: the bridge financing option

For underfunded HOAs facing a critical capital project before reserves can recover, an HOA line of credit through a community association lender can bridge the gap. Standard terms run 5–7 years, with the loan documents requiring the board to maintain reserve contributions during the loan period. The line of credit is disclosed in resale packages and audited financial statements.

Lines of credit are not a substitute for a funding plan; they are a financing tool within a plan. Boards that take a loan without raising assessments end up replacing one underfunding crisis with another in five years.

## Communication: the part that protects the board

Most HOA litigation we have read about over reserve fund management traces back to concealment, not to the funding gap itself. Owners discovered the gap through resale disclosures, FHA project denials, or their own document inspection — not from the board. By the time owners learned, they felt blindsided, and the board's communication record made them sympathetic plaintiffs.

The defensive move is the opposite: tell owners early, tell them in writing, hold an open meeting, answer questions on the record.
