## Why pools are the highest-liability amenity in any HOA

A community pool is, statistically, the single most dangerous thing an HOA owns. The Consumer Product Safety Commission tracks roughly 6,400 pool-related drownings and near-drownings of children under 15 in residential settings each year. A meaningful share occur in HOA community pools. When a drowning or entrapment happens, two questions determine personal liability for the directors: did the association have written safety procedures, and did the association comply with federal and state pool law?

The Virginia Graeme Baker Act is named for the granddaughter of former Secretary of State James Baker. She drowned in a hot tub in 2002 when the suction from a non-compliant drain held her under. Congress passed the act in 2007. Sixteen years later, many HOA pools still have non-compliant drain covers because the board does not know the act applies to them. It does.

## VGB Act compliance step by step

The act has two main requirements for HOA pools and spas.

**Drain cover standard.** Every drain cover (sometimes called a sump cover or grate) must meet ANSI/ASME A112.19.8. Covers are stamped with a manufacturer date and have a service life of 5-7 years. After that, the cover must be replaced even if it looks intact. A board cannot rely on the pool service company to track this; the board should require written certification annually with a copy of the cover stamp and the install date.

**Single-drain pools.** A pool with only one main drain on the pool floor must have a secondary anti-entrapment device. The most common is a Safety Vacuum Release System (SVRS) — a pressure sensor that shuts off the pump if a vacuum builds. Other options include suction-limiting vent systems and gravity drainage. Adding a secondary system to an existing single-drain pool runs $1,500 to $3,500. Replacing the pool to add a second drain is far more expensive.

Compliance is not technically optional. The penalty under federal law for non-compliance is up to $100,000 per violation. The real exposure, though, is the post-injury negligence claim. Plaintiff''s counsel will subpoena the maintenance records, the drain-cover stamps, and the board minutes that approved (or failed to approve) compliance work. A board that documented its compliance audit and acted on it is defensible. A board that did not is not.

## ADA at HOA pools

The 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design require fixed lifts or sloped entries at most public pools. The Department of Justice has consistently treated HOA pools open to non-residents (guest passes, day-rental, special events) as public accommodations subject to ADA. Pure resident-only pools have a narrower ADA exposure, but state public-accommodations law may still apply.

A compliant fixed lift runs $4,000 to $7,000. A portable lift is not compliant under the 2010 standards because the requirement is that the lift be available without staff assistance. Boards that approved a portable lift years ago should reconfirm with counsel — the standards have been clarified since.

## Lifeguards, age cutoffs, and the rules at the gate

Most states do not require lifeguards at HOA pools. The default rule is "No Lifeguard On Duty — Swim At Your Own Risk" signage, plus posted rules. The rules at the gate become the operating standard. They should match the rules in the governing documents and the rules the board actually enforces.

Common rule provisions:

- Age cutoff for unsupervised entry, commonly 14 or 16. Confirm state law before setting.
- Guest limits per resident.
- Pool hours, with after-hours signage.
- No glass containers.
- Shower-before-entry requirement (many state health codes).
- Children in diapers must wear swim diapers.
- No pets except service animals.

The rules need to be enforced consistently. A pool rule applied to one family''s children and not another''s is a Fair Housing case under familial-status protection. The board''s defense in any pool-related Fair Housing claim is the documented enforcement record.

## Insurance is necessary but not sufficient

A general liability policy with a clear pool extension and a sized-appropriate umbrella is the floor. But insurance does not eliminate director exposure. D&O policies almost always carve out bodily injury, so a director named personally in a drowning lawsuit relies on the GL policy''s defense duty, which is owed to the association, not the individual. Some carriers will defend named directors as additional insureds; others will not. The board should confirm in writing.

The single best risk-reduction practice — better than any insurance increase — is the documented daily safety check during the swim season. A signed daily checklist, retained for the statute of limitations, is the documentary record that turns a "duty of care" question into a documented "due care" defense. Boards that move daily safety checks into their HOA management software, with photo capture and time stamps, see better outcomes when claims occur.
