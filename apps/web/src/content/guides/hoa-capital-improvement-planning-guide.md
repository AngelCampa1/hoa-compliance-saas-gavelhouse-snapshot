## Why this distinction is the most misunderstood topic on HOA boards

We have read hundreds of HOA budget meeting minutes. The single most common fiduciary misstep is some version of this:

> "We need to upgrade the pool area with a new spa. We have $80,000 in reserves. Motion to approve."

That motion is illegal in most states. Reserves are restricted funds. Adding a spa where there was no spa before is a capital improvement, not a reserve replacement. Funding it from reserves without a member vote can be characterized as misappropriation of restricted funds, and individual board members carry personal liability when courts find against them.

The fix is conceptual, not technical. Boards need to internalize the difference between replacement and improvement, and apply the right funding source to each.

## Replacement vs improvement: the working definition

### Reserve replacement

A reserve replacement is the substantially-like-for-like replacement of an existing component identified in the reserve study at the end of its useful life. Examples:

- Replacing a 25-year-old asphalt-shingle roof with a new asphalt-shingle roof.
- Resurfacing a swimming pool that has reached the end of its surface life.
- Replacing rotted wood fencing with new wood fencing of similar specification.
- Repaving an asphalt driveway with new asphalt.

Reserve replacements come from the reserve fund. They are authorized by the reserve study and do not require a member vote.

### Capital improvement

A capital improvement adds new value or capability beyond what previously existed. Examples:

- Adding paddle courts to a community that did not have them.
- Building a new clubhouse extension.
- Installing EV chargers in the parking lot for the first time.
- Upgrading a basic swimming pool to a resort-style amenity with new features.
- Adding a fitness center where none existed.
- Installing a new security gate system on a previously ungated community.

Capital improvements come from operating funds, special assessments, capital improvement funds, or financing — and they typically require a member vote above a threshold.

### The gray zone

Some projects sit on the line. Replacing a roof with a slightly higher-quality material is generally fine. Replacing a basic pool with a resort-style amenity is generally not. The test is materiality — does the new component substantially exceed the function and value of the old component, or is it a reasonable modern equivalent?

When in doubt, document the reasoning, get an attorney's opinion, and lean toward member approval rather than against it. The cost of running a vote you didn't strictly need is much smaller than the cost of skipping a vote that was required.

## State statutes and member-approval thresholds

Most states require member approval for capital improvements above a threshold. The threshold can be:

- A fixed dollar amount in the declaration (commonly $5,000-$25,000).
- A percentage of annual operating budget (commonly 5%-15%).
- A statutory threshold under state law.

### Florida

Florida Statute 720.303 requires owner approval for material alterations or substantial additions to common elements. The threshold and process are defined by the declaration but cannot be lower than statute provides.

### California

California's Davis-Stirling Act requires member approval for capital improvements that exceed 5% of the budgeted gross expenses for the year. The 5% trigger captures most meaningful additions.

### Most UCIOA states

The Uniform Common Interest Ownership Act default requires member approval for capital improvements above a percentage threshold the declaration sets.

Pull your state statute. Pull your declaration. Identify the threshold. Document it in board policy.

## The 5-year capital plan

A rolling 5-year capital plan is what separates financially mature boards from reactive ones. It is a single document that lists:

- Every potential capital project for the next 5 years.
- The year of expected need or desired completion.
- The cost estimate (refresh annually).
- The funding source (operating budget, reserves with study justification, special assessment, or capital improvement fund).
- The approval pathway (board-only or member-approved).
- The approval status.

The capital plan complements the reserve study but is distinct. The reserve study answers "what will fail and when?" The capital plan answers "what will we add or upgrade and when?"

### Common categories

**Amenity additions:** Paddle courts, dog parks, community gardens, EV charging.

**Technology upgrades:** Security cameras, smart access control, electronic gate systems, smart utility meters.

**Accessibility:** ADA-compliant ramps, pool lifts, parking modifications.

**Energy efficiency:** Solar installations, LED lighting retrofits, smart irrigation, HVAC modernization.

**Aesthetic upgrades:** Landscape redesigns, signage refresh, common area renovation.

### Annual review cadence

Update the capital plan during the budget cycle. Sequence:

1. Reserve study update or annual review.
2. Capital plan update.
3. Operating budget draft (incorporates capital contributions and reserve contributions).
4. Member input period.
5. Board adoption and member ratification per declaration.

## Funding sources and how to match them

### Operating budget

Small capital improvements (below the member-approval threshold) can come from operating budget surplus or a dedicated line item. Useful for projects in the $1,000-$10,000 range.

### Capital improvement fund

A separate fund — distinct from reserves — that accumulates contributions specifically for future capital improvements. Funded through annual budget contributions, often $5-$20 per unit per month. Owners approved the fund's establishment, so withdrawals for capital improvements within the fund's purpose do not require additional votes.

### Special assessment

A one-time charge to all owners to fund a specific project. Requires member approval above thresholds set by statute and the declaration. Useful when the project is too large to absorb in operating budget but smaller than warrants long-term financing.

### Financing

Larger capital projects can be financed through HOA loans secured by future assessment streams. Member approval is typically required, and the loan terms become a long-running liability against the association.

### Reserves (only with strict justification)

Reserves can fund a capital improvement only when:

1. The improvement is specifically identified in the reserve study, OR
2. Member approval has been obtained for the specific use of reserve funds for the improvement.

Without one of these, reserve funding for a new amenity is at risk.

## Worked example: the paddle court question

Suppose a board is considering adding two paddle courts at an estimated cost of $90,000.

**Wrong approach:**
"Reserves have $250,000. We can fund this from reserves. Motion to approve."

**Right approach:**

1. Classify: paddle courts are a new amenity. This is a capital improvement.
2. Verify thresholds: state statute and the declaration require member approval for capital improvements above 5% of annual budget. Annual budget is $400,000. 5% is $20,000. The $90,000 project is well above the threshold.
3. Identify funding source: not reserves (no member vote on reserve use for new amenity). Options are special assessment, operating-funded capital improvement fund (if it exists and has the balance), or financing.
4. Plan member vote: prepare a written proposal describing the project, the cost, the funding mechanism, and the timeline. Notice the meeting per declaration requirements.
5. Hold the vote. If approved, execute. If denied, the project does not proceed.

This sequence takes longer than "motion to approve from reserves." It is also the sequence that does not generate personal liability for the board members.

## Where boards get into trouble

The fact patterns we see repeatedly:

- **Reserve raid.** Board funds a new amenity from reserves without a member vote. Discovered by the next year's auditor or by a homeowner reviewing financials.
- **Pre-vote contracts.** Board signs the contractor before the member vote. The vote ratifies a fait accompli, which courts have voided.
- **Misclassified improvements.** Board calls a major upgrade a "replacement" to avoid the vote requirement. The auditor or a litigant disagrees.
- **No capital plan.** Board makes capital decisions ad hoc, without context, and ends up surprised by funding gaps.

The fix to all four is the same: classify projects properly, match funding to project type, run member votes when required, and maintain the 5-year capital plan as a living document. This is what fiduciary duty actually looks like at the financial layer.
