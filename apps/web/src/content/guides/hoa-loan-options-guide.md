definitions:
  - term: Capital Improvement Loan
    definition: >-
      A term loan or line of credit extended to an HOA specifically to fund major repairs, replacements, or new construction on common areas. The loan is typically secured by the HOA's assessment revenue — not real property — and repaid through dues or a separate assessment.
  - term: Reserve Deficiency
    definition: >-
      The gap between an HOA's current reserve fund balance and the amount recommended by the reserve study to cover future major repairs. States with reserve mandate statutes require boards to address deficiencies through a documented funding plan; failing to do so can constitute a breach of fiduciary duty.
  - term: Special Assessment
    definition: >-
      A one-time charge levied on homeowners above the regular dues, used to fund a specific capital project or cover an unexpected expense. Most CC&Rs cap the amount a board can levy without a member vote — typically expressed as a percentage of the annual operating budget.
  - term: Reserve Study
    definition: >-
      A professional analysis of an HOA's common area components, their remaining useful life, estimated replacement costs, and the funding plan required to cover those costs. Many states require reserve studies on a defined cycle; the report is the primary input for capital planning decisions.

steps:
  - title: Determine whether your CC&Rs authorize borrowing
    content: >-
      Before approaching any lender, pull your HOA's CC&Rs and bylaws and search for language around "borrowing," "debt," "loans," and "indebtedness." Many governing documents from the 1970s and 1980s either prohibit borrowing outright or require a supermajority member vote above a stated dollar threshold. If your documents are silent on borrowing authority, consult your HOA attorney before proceeding — some states imply borrowing authority for associations; others do not. Resolving this question before you approach a lender avoids a wasted application and a potentially embarrassing member challenge after the fact.
  - title: Commission or update your reserve study before applying
    content: >-
      Lenders require a current reserve study — typically completed within the last three years — as part of underwriting. If yours is older, commission an update before applying. Beyond satisfying lenders, the updated study will confirm whether the capital project is already accounted for in the reserve plan (in which case a loan may not be needed) or represents a new scope. The study also quantifies the reserve funding percentage that lenders use to determine your interest rate and loan-to-income ratios. A well-funded association with a current study is in a materially better position than one with a stale or missing report.
  - title: Solicit project bids and establish a total project cost
    content: >-
      Capital improvement loans are sized against a specific project with a defined scope and cost. Before applying, obtain at least three competitive bids from licensed contractors and establish a total project cost including a 10–15% contingency. Lenders are skeptical of projects without current bids — they've seen associations underestimate costs by 30–50% on aging infrastructure. Your loan application will include the bids, and the lender may condition the loan on using a bonded contractor and/or a construction disbursement schedule rather than a single lump-sum draw.
  - title: Compare loan structures against special assessment cost
    content: >-
      Model both options before committing. A $500,000 roof replacement funded by a 10-year loan at 8% costs the association approximately $73,000 per year in debt service — spread across 200 units, that's roughly $365 per unit per year or about $30/month in dues increase. The same project as a special assessment requires each of those 200 owners to contribute $2,500 in a single payment. Neither is universally better; the right answer depends on whether your CC&Rs require a member vote for the loan, whether a large lump-sum assessment would generate delinquencies, and your reserve fund's current health. Document this comparison in your board minutes as evidence of fiduciary analysis.
  - title: Obtain member approval if required, then close the loan
    content: >-
      If your analysis in step one revealed a vote threshold, schedule and conduct the member vote before closing the loan. Present the financial model, the project bids, the proposed dues impact, and the alternative scenarios. Boards that skip or rush this step face injunctions filed by homeowners — which can delay projects for months and generate legal fees that eclipse the interest savings from moving quickly. Once approved, work with your HOA attorney to execute the loan documents and ensure the assignment of assessment revenue is properly structured as security. Record the board resolution authorizing the loan in your minutes.
  - title: Track the loan separately from operating and reserve funds
    content: >-
      Loan proceeds must not commingle with operating or reserve funds. Open a dedicated project account for construction disbursements and track principal balance, interest expense, and repayment schedule as line items in your annual budget. Most states with reserve mandates require the annual budget disclosure to include existing debt service — burying the loan repayment inside general operating line items creates disclosure problems at the annual meeting and obscures the true cost of ownership for prospective buyers reviewing HOA financials.

## Why Capital Improvement Financing Is a Fiduciary Issue

Capital improvement financing rarely gets treated as a liability issue until something goes wrong. A board defers a roof replacement for three years because the reserves aren't quite there. They finally fund it by drawing the reserve account down to near-zero. Two years later, a pipe failure that was in the reserve study's five-year horizon comes due — and the reserves don't exist. The special assessment that follows is large, contentious, and in several states, evidence of a breach of the board's duty to maintain reserves.

The legal standard varies by state. California Civil Code 5550 requires a formal reserve study and annual update. Florida Statute 718.112 (condominiums) and 720.303 (HOAs) both impose reserve funding and disclosure requirements. Nevada Revised Statutes Chapter 116 requires reserve studies and funding plans. In all three states, courts have found individual board members liable in situations where reserve fund neglect was clear and documented.

The financing question — loan, special assessment, reserve draw, or some combination — is ultimately a fiduciary analysis the board must document. Choosing the cheapest option without modeling the reserve impact isn't adequate governance. What follows is how we've seen boards work through that analysis.

## Understanding the Four Funding Paths

### Reserve Draws

A planned reserve draw is the lowest-friction option. If the project is in your reserve study, the funds should be accumulating. The problem arises when projects are accelerated, scope-creep, or when the study hasn't been updated in five years and the cost estimates are no longer accurate.

The key question is what the reserve fund balance will be after the draw, expressed as a percentage of the actuarially recommended balance. Below 30% is considered critically underfunded; Fannie Mae's conventional lending guidelines require at least 10% of the annual budget be allocated to reserves, and a post-draw percentage below that threshold can trigger underwriting problems for buyers in your community — affecting resale values association-wide.

If the draw will drop you below 50% funded, you should at minimum document a board resolution with a specific replenishment timeline. In states with reserve mandate statutes, that documentation may be legally required.

### Special Assessments

Special assessments are fast and interest-free. The downside is concentration of cash demand on individual owners at a single point in time. A $3,000 special assessment hits retirees and owners on fixed income the same as it hits dual-income households with high liquidity.

Most CC&Rs cap what a board can levy without a member vote — typically expressed as a percentage of the annual operating budget. Florida Statute 720.303(6) sets the threshold at 115% of the prior year's total annual budget for the assessment to require a member vote. California HOA statutes under Civil Code 5600 give boards authority to levy special assessments up to 5% of the annual budget without a member vote. Boards that exceed these thresholds without a vote face legal challenges that can include injunctions and reversal of the assessment.

Payment plans reduce hardship but require board policy — and in some states (Washington, for example), the right to a payment plan is codified in statute.

### Bank Loans and Lines of Credit

HOA-specific lending has grown significantly since 2015. Community banks, credit unions, and specialty lenders like Popular Association Banking and Alliance Association Bank offer products designed around HOA cash flows. Loans are typically:

- **Term loans** for specific projects: 5–20 year amortization, fixed or floating rates, secured by assignment of assessment revenue rather than real property
- **Lines of credit**: Revolving, typically 1–5 year terms, useful for phased projects or when project scope is still being defined. Rates float, introducing interest rate risk.

Interest rates as of 2025 run approximately 6.5–9.5% for term loans, depending on reserve health, delinquency rate, and loan term. That's materially cheaper than the cost of a large special assessment that generates delinquencies and collection costs.

Lenders require: (1) CC&R authority to borrow, (2) a current reserve study, (3) at least three years of financial statements, (4) a delinquency rate below 5–10%, and (5) a certified board resolution authorizing the loan.

### Dues Increases

A sustained dues increase to fund a capital project is the slowest option and is most appropriate for lower-urgency projects where the reserve study's timeline allows for multi-year accumulation. It avoids interest cost and distributes the burden equitably over time. The limitation is that it requires homeowner acceptance, is slow to accumulate meaningful capital, and doesn't help with an urgent repair.

## State-Specific Considerations

### California

California Civil Code 5550 mandates a reserve study with visual inspection every three years and an annual review. The law requires the reserve funding plan to be disclosed in the annual budget report. Boards must disclose the current funding percentage and, if below 30%, the plan to address the deficiency. Willful failure to fund reserves in the face of a known deficiency has supported individual board member liability in California courts.

California also has a specific limitation on special assessments: Civil Code 5600 permits boards to levy up to 5% of the annual budget without a member vote. Amounts above 5% require a member vote under Civil Code 5610.

### Florida

Florida operates separate statutory schemes for condominiums (Florida Statute 718) and HOAs (Florida Statute 720). Both impose reserve funding requirements. For condominiums, Senate Bill 4-D (2022) — passed in response to the Surfside collapse — significantly strengthened reserve funding requirements, requiring condominiums three stories or more to be fully funded by 2025.

Florida Statute 720.303(6) caps HOA board authority to levy special assessments at 115% of the prior year's annual budget. Above that threshold, a member vote is required. Boards considering a loan repaid through a special assessment need to analyze whether the repayment structure triggers this requirement.

### Texas

Texas Property Code Chapter 209 governs HOAs and is somewhat less prescriptive on reserve requirements than California or Florida, but does impose notice, election, and enforcement requirements that affect capital project financing. Texas does not have a statewide reserve study mandate, but most well-advised HOA attorneys recommend maintaining one regardless.

Texas is notable for its towing and liens laws — relevant context when boards consider special assessments, as delinquent owners can complicate lien enforcement in Texas compared to states like Nevada where the process is more streamlined.

## The Fiduciary Analysis Your Board Should Document

When boards we've spoken with during Gavelhouse's development faced capital project decisions, the ones that avoided post-project controversy were those that documented a genuine multi-option analysis in their minutes. That analysis should include:

1. **Total project cost** with three contractor bids and a contingency
2. **Reserve fund impact** of a draw option, with post-draw funding percentage
3. **Per-unit cost comparison** of loan vs. assessment vs. dues increase
4. **Vote threshold analysis** — does any option trigger a member vote under your CC&Rs or state law?
5. **Timeline** — when does the project need to start, and which financing path can achieve that timeline?
6. **Interest cost** of the loan option vs. the delinquency risk cost of a large special assessment

This analysis doesn't have to be elaborate. A one-page board memo attached to the minutes that covers these six points demonstrates the fiduciary process that protects board members individually if the decision is later challenged.

## Avoiding the Most Common Financing Mistakes

The reserve draw without a replenishment plan is the most common mistake we see reflected in reserve studies. The second most common is a board that proceeds with a loan without confirming CC&R authority to borrow — only to have a disgruntled homeowner file an injunction two days before closing.

The third is using loan proceeds to fund projects that should have been covered by reserves all along — deferring the reserve study update because it would show a deficiency, using a loan to paper over the gap, and then facing a larger loan balance AND a reserve deficiency simultaneously.

Capital improvement financing done correctly is a legitimate and often board-member-protective tool. It distributes cost over time, reduces the hardship of large special assessments, and can actually improve property values by ensuring timely maintenance of common areas. The key is documentation, proper authority, and a clear-eyed analysis of the reserve fund impact.
