      A special assessment is a one-time charge levied on homeowners above regular dues, used to fund a specific capital project or cover an unexpected expense. Boards can levy special assessments when: (1) the CC&Rs authorize it, (2) the amount doesn't exceed the threshold requiring a member vote, and (3) proper notice is given.     a: >-

Non-payment can result in late fees, interest, and ultimately a lien on the property — followed by potential foreclosure in states that permit HOA lien foreclosure. Homeowners who dispute a special assessment must challenge it through a legal process, not simply refuse to pay while the dispute is pending.
  - q: Can an HOA offer payment plans for special assessments?
    a: >-
      Boards have broad discretion to offer installment payment plans, and some states — including Washington and California — give homeowners a statutory right to request one. Even where not required, offering payment plans reduces delinquency rates, reduces collection costs, and reduces the community tension that large lump-sum assessments generate. Payment plan terms typically range from 6 to 24 months with or without interest.

## The Vote Threshold Problem Boards Overlook

Special assessment authority sounds simple — the board needs money, it levies an assessment. The reality is more complicated, and the complication has cost boards real money and legal exposure.

Every HOA's CC&Rs include language limiting unilateral board authority to levy special assessments. These limits exist because special assessments directly impact homeowner finances without the annual budget approval process that members participate in. State legislatures have added statutory floors in many states that apply even when CC&Rs are more permissive.

Getting this wrong doesn't just create a governance problem — it creates a legal one. Courts in California, Florida, and Texas have enjoined special assessments levied without required member votes, sometimes after partial collection had already begun. Refunding partial collections, covering legal fees, and then re-running a member vote process can cost an HOA more than the assessment itself was meant to raise.

## How Vote Thresholds Work in Practice

### California

California Civil Code 5600 gives boards authority to levy special assessments up to 5% of the annual budget without a member vote. That sounds like a reasonable limit, but on a small association with a $100,000 annual budget, it caps board authority at $5,000. A roof repair for a 50-unit community rarely comes in at $5,000.

For amounts above 5%, the board must hold a member election by written ballot under Civil Code 5100-5130. The threshold for passage is a majority of a quorum. Written ballot elections take time — typically 60–90 days from notice to close. Boards facing urgent projects often try to compress this timeline, and that compression is where procedural errors happen.

### Florida

Florida Statute 720.303(6) sets the HOA threshold at 115% of the prior year's annual budget. For an HOA with a $500,000 annual budget, the board can levy up to $575,000 in special assessments in a given year without a member vote. That's a relatively high threshold and gives Florida boards more flexibility than their California counterparts for most projects.

Florida condominiums operate under Statute 718, which has different thresholds and was significantly amended by Senate Bill 4-D in 2022 following the Surfside collapse. Condominium boards in Florida should be analyzing their reserve funding requirements under the amended statute, not the pre-2022 rules.

### Other States

Washington State requires a member vote for any special assessment exceeding $500 per unit unless the governing documents provide a higher threshold. Texas Property Code Chapter 209 requires notice but is less prescriptive on vote requirements — the CC&Rs govern. Nevada Revised Statutes Chapter 116 requires a member vote for assessments above a threshold defined in NRS 116.3115. Always work from your state's current statute and your specific CC&Rs, not generalizations.

## Homeowner Financing Options: What Boards Should Know and Share

When a large special assessment is levied, some homeowners will ask the board how they're supposed to pay it. Boards that provide financing information as part of the assessment notice reduce collection disputes significantly.

### HELOC and Home Equity Loans

For homeowners with sufficient equity in their property, a HELOC is typically the lowest-cost financing option. As of 2025, HELOC rates range from approximately 7–9% for well-qualified borrowers. Home equity loans offer fixed rates on a fixed term. Either option allows the homeowner to spread the cost over years rather than paying a lump sum, with interest that may be tax-deductible in some circumstances (consult a tax advisor — the HOA should not be giving tax advice).

The board doesn't endorse or arrange HELOC financing. Providing general information about the option in the assessment notice is appropriate; connecting homeowners to specific lenders is not.

### Personal Loans

Unsecured personal loans from banks, credit unions, or online lenders are available in amounts that cover most special assessments. Rates are higher than secured options — typically 10–20% for qualifying borrowers — but the application process is faster and doesn't require property equity. For homeowners without equity or who prefer not to encumber their property, personal loans are often the fastest path to meeting the assessment deadline.

### The Board's Own Payment Plan

Offering a formal payment plan eliminates the need for most homeowners to seek outside financing. A 12–24 month installment plan at 0% interest (or nominal interest to cover administrative costs) is often more cost-effective for the homeowner than any external loan. The board bears some risk — a homeowner who defaults on a payment plan still owes the full balance, and collection enforcement must follow the delinquency procedures in your governing documents.

California Civil Code 5655 gives homeowners a statutory right to request a payment plan for non-emergency special assessments. The payment plan must be offered on written request, and the board's plan must be adopted before the first payment is due. Boards that don't have a documented payment plan policy before levying the assessment in California are setting themselves up for a procedural dispute.

## Structuring the Assessment for Minimum Legal Exposure

The way an assessment is documented matters as much as the dollar amount. Board resolutions for special assessments should be specific enough that anyone reading them — including a judge reviewing a challenge — can understand what the board decided and why.

A well-drafted resolution includes:

- The specific project name and scope
- The total project cost with bids attached
- The per-unit assessment amount and how it was calculated
- The payment due date or installment schedule
- A statement confirming the amount is within board authority (with cite to CC&R section) or that a member vote was conducted and passed
- Whether a payment plan is available and how to request it

Vague resolutions create ambiguity that homeowner attorneys exploit. "Special assessment for building repairs — $2,000 per unit, due March 1" is inadequate. Specificity is protection.

## Tracking Collections: The Operational Side

Special assessment collections create an accounting obligation that persists until the assessment is fully paid. Unlike regular dues, which reset monthly, a special assessment has a fixed total — and the board has a fiduciary obligation to pursue collection of unpaid amounts.

Best practice is a dedicated tracking register that shows, for each unit: assessment amount, payment received, balance outstanding, payment plan status (if applicable), and date of last activity. This register should be reviewed at each board meeting until the assessment is closed.

Commingling special assessment proceeds with the operating account is a common mistake on smaller boards. It obscures the true state of project funding, makes audits complicated, and in states with reserve fund segregation requirements, can create compliance problems. Open a dedicated bank account for the project — most banks will do this at no cost for HOA clients.

When boards build their reserve and operating budgets in the same platform, the financing analysis — loan vs. assessment vs. reserve draw — is a direct output rather than a separate spreadsheet exercise.

## What Happens When a Homeowner Refuses to Pay

Non-payment of a properly levied special assessment follows the same delinquency procedures as regular dues: late fees and interest accrue, the account moves to collections, and ultimately a lien is recorded against the property. Most states allow HOA lien foreclosure for unpaid assessments — the timeline and process varies, but the legal authority exists in California, Florida, Texas, Nevada, and Washington.

Homeowners who dispute the validity of an assessment (not the amount — the validity) need to pursue that dispute through a legal challenge, not non-payment. Courts have consistently held that a homeowner cannot withhold dues or assessments as a protest against the board's decisions, even when the underlying decision may be flawed. The remedy is legal challenge, not self-help.

The board's job when a legal challenge is filed is to ensure the documentation — the vote analysis, the notices, the resolution, the bids — is organized and available for counsel. That documentation is what converts a legal challenge from a problem into a manageable cost.
