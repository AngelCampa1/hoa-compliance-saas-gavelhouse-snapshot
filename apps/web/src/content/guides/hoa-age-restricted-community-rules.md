## What Makes a 55+ Community Legal Under Federal Law

Age discrimination in housing is illegal under the Fair Housing Act — with one specific statutory exception: communities that qualify under the Housing for Older Persons Act of 1995 (HOPA).

HOPA created two categories of legally permissible age-restricted communities. The first is "62 and over" communities, where every resident of every unit must be 62 years of age or older — no exceptions. The second, far more common, is "55 and over" communities that meet the HOPA three-part test.

Understanding this legal structure matters because the exemption is not self-executing. It doesn't exist simply because your CC&Rs say "55+" or because your community has operated as age-restricted for decades. The exemption must be actively maintained through ongoing compliance with each of HOPA's three requirements. Let any one of them lapse, and the exemption evaporates.

## The Three-Part HOPA Test

### Requirement 1: 80% Occupancy by Persons 55+

At least 80% of the community's occupied units must have at least one resident who is 55 years of age or older. The measurement is against occupied units only — vacant units drop out of the denominator.

The 80% threshold gives communities a 20% cushion. That cushion accommodates situations like:

- A 55+ owner who has a spouse or partner under 55 (the unit qualifies because the 55+ resident is there)
- Units that transitioned to owners under 55 before the community could intervene
- Units occupied by a resident who ages out of 55+ status through reverse migration (rare, but it happens when a resident occupies a unit before turning 55 and the community is managing the grandfathering)

The 80% threshold is a floor, not a target. Communities that operate at 82–85% have limited room for error. Communities at 90%+ have meaningful headroom to handle individual unit transitions without threatening the aggregate threshold.

### Requirement 2: Published Intent Policies

HOPA requires that the community publish and follow policies demonstrating its intent to be housing for older persons. The published intent requirement means:

- The age restriction must appear in the recorded CC&Rs
- The community must advertise itself as age-restricted (or at minimum, not represent itself as a general-occupancy community)
- The age restriction must be enforced consistently — selectively ignoring violations signals that the intent policy is not genuine

A community that has the words "55+" in its CC&Rs but has not enforced the restriction in years may have undermined its own published intent policies. Courts and HUD investigators look at the totality of how the community has operated, not just what the documents say.

### Requirement 3: Age Verification Through Biennial Surveys

The survey requirement is where most compliance failures occur. HOPA requires that the community maintain age verification procedures including a survey of all units conducted at least every two years.

The survey must:

- Contact every unit in the community
- Collect the name and age of the qualifying resident (the person 55 or older)
- Verify the stated age with a government-issued document — a driver's license, passport, birth certificate, or Medicare card
- Record the verification method used
- Retain documentation

"Maintain age verification procedures" means more than running the survey — it means keeping the records. A community that conducted a survey two years ago but cannot produce the documentation has the same compliance exposure as a community that never ran the survey.

## What Happens When HOPA Exemption Is Lost

Loss of HOPA exemption means the Fair Housing Act's familial status protections immediately apply. The community can no longer enforce age-based restrictions against households with children under 18.

If the board continues enforcing age restrictions after exemption loss — denying occupancy to families with minor children, issuing violations to units with children present — every enforcement action is a potential Fair Housing Act violation.

### HUD Complaint Process

An aggrieved family files a complaint with HUD or the applicable state fair housing agency. HUD investigates. If there is reasonable cause to believe a violation occurred, HUD issues a charge of discrimination. The case proceeds either before an Administrative Law Judge (where remedies include actual damages, injunctive relief, and civil penalties up to $21,663 for a first offense as of 2024) or in federal district court (where remedies can include unlimited compensatory and punitive damages).

Board members who participated in the discriminatory enforcement action can be named individually in the complaint. The "I didn't know the exemption had lapsed" defense is not a guaranteed protection if the lapse was caused by the board failing to maintain required compliance procedures.

### Requalification After Exemption Loss

A community can requalify for HOPA exemption after losing it. Requalification requires meeting all three requirements again: completing a new biennial survey that demonstrates 80% occupancy by 55+ residents, verifying published intent policies are in place, and conducting the full survey with proper documentation. During the gap period — from the time of exemption loss to the date of successful requalification — the community cannot lawfully enforce age restrictions against families with children.

## Enforcing Age Rules Within a Compliant Framework

Assuming HOPA compliance is maintained, how does the board actually enforce the 55+ requirement against non-compliant occupancy?

### At Purchase or Transfer

Most 55+ communities require that before any unit transfer, the buyer acknowledge in writing that at least one occupant will be 55 or older. This acknowledgment is typically collected at closing. Boards should also require prospective buyers to complete an age verification form before or contemporaneously with closing.

The board cannot block a sale to a buyer who has children, but it can condition occupancy on the presence of a qualifying 55+ resident. The distinction matters: the restriction is on occupancy, not on ownership.

### When Violations Are Discovered

If a unit is occupied by persons under 55 with no qualifying resident, the enforcement process follows your CC&Rs' standard violation procedure: written notice citing the specific CC&R provision, opportunity to cure (by either vacating the non-qualifying occupant or adding a qualifying 55+ resident), and fines for continued violation.

Courts have generally upheld enforcement of HOPA-compliant age restrictions when the community has maintained its survey records and applied the rules consistently. The most common enforcement failures are (1) inconsistent application — enforcing against some violators while ignoring others — and (2) lack of documentation showing the community was in compliance at the time of enforcement.

## Building HOPA Compliance Into Operations

HOPA compliance should not be a project that the board undertakes every two years in a panic. It should be a standing operational process:

- The biennial survey deadline is on the board's governance calendar, not just in someone's memory
- New unit sales trigger an age verification step in the resale process
- The survey records are retained in the association's permanent document archive, not in a board member's personal files
- Annual review of the 80% occupancy threshold happens alongside the annual budget process

The board's fiduciary duty to the community includes protecting the community's legal status. A 55+ community that loses HOPA exemption doesn't just lose the ability to restrict age — it loses a significant part of its market positioning and potentially the character the community was built around. Preventing that outcome is a fiduciary responsibility, not optional administrative work.
