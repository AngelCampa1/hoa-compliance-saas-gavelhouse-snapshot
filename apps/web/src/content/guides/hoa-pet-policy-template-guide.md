  - term: Service Animal
    definition: >-
      Under the Americans with Disabilities Act, a service animal is a dog (or in limited cases, a miniature horse) individually trained to perform tasks for a person with a disability. Service animals are not pets under the ADA — they are working animals. HOAs must permit service animals regardless of pet restrictions.
  - term: Emotional Support Animal (ESA)
    definition: >-
      An animal that provides emotional support or comfort to a person with a documented disability. Unlike service animals, ESAs are not trained to perform specific tasks.   - term: Reasonable Accommodation
    definition: >-
      A change in rules, policies, practices, or services that allows a person with a disability to have equal opportunity to use and enjoy housing. Under the FHA, HOAs must engage in an interactive process when a resident requests an accommodation — including for service and emotional support animals. Denial of a reasonable accommodation request is an FHA violation.
  - term: Selective Enforcement
    definition: >-
      Applying rules to some residents while ignoring violations by others. In the HOA context, selective enforcement of pet policies is both a Fair Housing Act risk (if the pattern tracks protected class membership) and a contract law defense (a homeowner charged with a violation can argue the board waived enforcement by ignoring the same violation elsewhere).

## The Federal Law Problem in HOA Pet Policies

Most HOA pet policies are written to address the operational concerns of running a community — noise, waste, liability from aggressive animals, insurance implications of certain breeds. These are legitimate concerns, and restricting pets based on them is generally legally permissible.

The enforcement problem — and the liability problem — arises at the intersection of those restrictions with federal law. The Fair Housing Act applies to every HOA with more than four units. It prohibits housing providers from refusing to make reasonable accommodations for persons with disabilities, including accommodations that would otherwise violate pet policies. No CC&R, however well-drafted, overrides the FHA.

What we found when building Gavelhouse's enforcement module is that most HOAs don't have a compliance gap in their pet restrictions — they have a compliance gap in their accommodation process. The restrictions are fine. The problem is there's no documented process for what happens when a resident with a disability asks for an exception.

## What the Fair Housing Act Actually Requires

HUD's 2020 guidance document on assistance animals (FHEO-2020-01) is the most current authoritative statement of FHA requirements for HOAs on this issue. The guidance distinguishes between service animals (which fall under both ADA and FHA) and emotional support animals (which fall under FHA only, not ADA).

For both categories, the board's role is to engage in what HUD calls an "interactive process" — essentially, a good-faith review of the resident's request. The board is evaluating:

1. Does the resident have a physical or mental disability that substantially limits one or more major life activities?
2. Does the animal provide disability-related benefit (for service animals, specific trained tasks; for ESAs, emotional support or comfort)?

The board is not the arbiter of whether the disability is "real" or "serious." A licensed healthcare provider's documentation creates a presumption that the board should not second-guess without specific reason. The 2020 HUD guidance specifically warns against policies that require particular forms or specific provider types — those requirements create barriers that can themselves constitute FHA violations.

### The Breed Restriction Problem

The most common enforcement mistake: a resident requests accommodation for a Pit Bull or German Shepherd that is also a certified service animal. The board, looking at its breed restriction list, denies the accommodation on breed grounds.

This is wrong, and courts have said so. Service animal breed restrictions require an individualized assessment of whether the specific animal poses a direct threat — not categorical exclusion based on breed. Unless the board can document specific dangerous behavior by that specific animal, categorical breed exclusion of service animals is FHA non-compliant.

### The Fee Problem

Charging a pet deposit or fee for a service animal or ESA is an FHA violation. The reasoning is straightforward: requiring a disabled person to pay more than a non-disabled person to live in the same unit on equivalent terms is prohibited discrimination. Registration fees that apply only to service animals and ESAs, or "damage deposits" that don't apply to regular pets, fall into this category.

Boards can require registration and documentation. They cannot require payment for the privilege of exercising an FHA accommodation right.

## Building a Legally Defensible Pet Policy

### Weight Limits

Weight limits are the most common pet restriction and among the most defensible — courts have consistently upheld them when they're in the governing documents and applied consistently. The word "consistently" matters. A board that routinely grants exceptions for long-term residents, board members' pets, or "well-behaved" dogs while denying exceptions to others is creating a selective enforcement record that will undermine the restriction entirely.

Set the weight limit with specificity (a number, not "small" or "medium") and define when it's measured (full adult weight, not puppy weight). Require weight verification at annual registration. Apply the rule to every unit, every year, without exception — except for the accommodation review process.

### Breed Restrictions

Breed restrictions are legally permissible in governing documents but require more care in enforcement than weight limits. Insurance underwriters for many HOAs exclude certain breeds — Pit Bull terriers, Rottweilers, Dobermans, Akitas, Chow Chows, and German Shepherds appear on most exclusion lists. This creates a legitimate financial rationale for breed restrictions that courts respect.

The policy should list specific breeds by AKC/UKC designation, not rely on vague terms like "aggressive breeds" or "dangerous dogs." Mixed-breed identification creates its own problems — DNA testing requirements are disproportionate and have been challenged as FHA barriers. Consider whether your restriction targets specific recognized breeds (defensible) or tries to catch any dog that "looks like" a restricted breed (enforceable nightmare).

### Registration and Documentation

Require registration within 30 days of move-in for existing pets and within 30 days of acquisition for new pets. The registration process creates the administrative record that enables consistent enforcement. Without it, boards are relying on visual identification and complaint reports — a system that inevitably produces selective enforcement claims.

Registration documentation should include current vaccination records. Most states require rabies vaccination for dogs regardless of HOA rules, but requiring proof at registration transfers some of the compliance burden from the board to the resident.

## Provisions Courts Have Struck Down

Beyond the service animal/ESA accommodation failures, courts have struck down pet policy provisions on these grounds:

**Board approval of individual animals without criteria.** Policies that say "all pets subject to board approval" without defining approval criteria give boards unchecked discretion that courts treat as arbitrary. The approval criteria must be stated in the policy — weight, breed, vaccination status, and documentation are appropriate criteria; "board discretion" alone is not.

**Retroactive restrictions on pre-existing pets.** Boards that amend CC&Rs to prohibit pets generally cannot apply the new restriction to residents who owned pets before the amendment. The legal theory is promissory estoppel — the resident relied on the prior rules when acquiring the pet and housing. Some jurisdictions have specific statutes protecting pre-existing pets; even where they don't, courts have often reached the same result on equitable grounds.

**Unlimited number restrictions applied to service animals.** A policy limiting units to one pet cannot be applied to deny a second service animal to a resident who needs two animals for disability-related purposes. HUD guidance specifically addresses multiple assistance animal requests — the board must evaluate each accommodation request individually.

**Lease restrictions that don't match CC&Rs.** When the CC&Rs permit pets but the lease form prohibits them, the CC&Rs govern for HOA enforcement purposes. Boards sometimes create this inconsistency by adopting lease addenda without updating the underlying governing documents. The result is an unenforceable restriction and, potentially, a claim by a tenant who was denied housing based on a pet that the HOA rules actually permitted.

## Enforcement Without Discrimination Claims

Consistent enforcement is the single best protection against discrimination claims in the pet context. A board that applies its rules identically to every resident — same notice procedure, same cure period, same fine schedule, same escalation path — is in a legally defensible position even if an aggrieved resident files a Fair Housing complaint.

The documentation standard is high. Every violation notice, every response, every fine, every hearing, every accommodation request, and every accommodation decision should be written and stored. When a Fair Housing investigation begins, the investigator will ask for the board's complete enforcement history for the last several years. A complete, consistent enforcement log is the evidence that demonstrates the board applied its rules evenhandedly.

Boards that rely on informal enforcement — "we mentioned it to them," "I sent a text," "everyone knows the rule" — have no evidentiary record when challenged. Document everything.
