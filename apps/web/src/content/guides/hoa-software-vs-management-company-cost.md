## The numbers most boards do not run

A typical HOA management contract for a 50-unit community runs \$15 to \$25 per door per month. That is \$9,000 to \$15,000 per year in base management fees. Add transaction fees on assessment payments, reporting fees, lockbox fees, and a markup on vendor invoices, and the all-in cost often pushes \$12,000 to \$20,000 per year for a community that size.

Self-managed software, by contrast, is flat-priced. Gavelhouse's tiers are \$20, \$49, \$99, and around \$199 per month depending on community size — \$240 to \$2,400 per year, regardless of how many transactions or owners. Other purpose-built tools price similarly. The software cost is rarely the deciding factor; it is in the noise compared to management fees.

The real question is what management does that software cannot. The honest answer is: a lot, but not as much as the management contract usually charges for.

## What software replaces

Software handles the financial and administrative core:

- Fund-separated accounting with reserve tracking.
- Owner ledgers and assessment billing.
- Payment processing for ACH and card.
- Bank reconciliation.
- Financial reports for board, audit, and owner disclosure.
- Document storage with retention.
- Audit log for compliance and litigation defense.
- Meeting agendas, minutes, and voting capture.
- Violation tracking with notice templates.
- Owner messaging and bulk communications.

For a self-managed board willing to do the data-entry and decision-making work, this list covers most of what a management company's accounting team produces — at a small fraction of the cost.

## What software does not replace

Software does not:

- **Pick up the phone** when an owner calls about a lockout or a leak.
- **Walk the property** to identify maintenance issues.
- **Coordinate vendors** for routine and emergency work.
- **Attend meetings** as a non-voting facilitator who knows Robert's Rules.
- **Serve as a buffer** between the board and angry owners.
- **Handle after-hours emergencies** like a roof leak at 2am.
- **File state and federal forms** the association is required to submit.
- **Manage insurance renewals** with brokers.
- **Run the annual budget process** as a coordinator.

These functions require human time. When a board switches from management to self-management, somebody — a board member, a volunteer, or a contracted specialist — has to absorb them. Underestimating that workload is the most common reason self-management fails.

## The break-even analysis

For most communities under 200 units, the financial case for self-management with software is overwhelming. The management fees dwarf the software cost, and as long as the board can find volunteers willing to do the operational work, the savings flow directly to reserves or to lower assessments.

The break-even shifts as communities scale up. At 300 to 500 units, the operational workload typically exceeds what volunteer time can sustainably cover. Amenities (pool, gym, gate, elevator) add maintenance complexity. Insurance renewals require expert input. State filings multiply. Boards in this range often run a hybrid: self-managed software for accounting and governance, with a management contract scoped to operational support only.

Above 500 units, full management is usually the right answer — but those boards should still demand fund-separated accounting from their management company, because compliance failures at scale create the same liability whether the books were kept by volunteers or professionals.

## The hybrid model

The most underutilized model for self-managed boards is hybrid. The board uses software for everything the software is good at, and contracts a management company on a scoped basis for the operational work the board does not want to absorb. Common hybrid scopes:

- **Vendor coordination** at \$200 to \$500 per month.
- **On-site supervision** for specific maintenance projects at hourly rates.
- **After-hours emergency response** through a 24/7 dispatch service at \$100 to \$300 per month.
- **Annual budget facilitation** at a flat project fee.

The hybrid model usually costs 20 to 40 percent of full management while preserving the board's control over financials and governance. For communities in the 100 to 300 unit range, hybrid is often the sweet spot.

## How to pilot the switch

If you are considering a switch from management to self-management, do not terminate the contract on day one. Run the software in parallel for sixty days, with the board picking up the operational work alongside management. Use that period to test whether the volunteer base can sustain the load. If the answer is yes, terminate at the contract renewal. If the answer is no, you have learned something cheap, and you can either recruit more volunteers or move to the hybrid model.

Boards that try to switch cold often discover halfway through that the operational workload is real, and they end up paying both the management company and the software vendor while they unwind the decision. The parallel run prevents that scenario.

## The bottom line

Software is cheaper, by a lot. Management is broader, by a lot. The right answer depends on what the board can sustain operationally, not on which option costs less on paper. For communities under 200 units with engaged volunteers, self-managed software is usually the right answer. For larger communities or boards with thin volunteer benches, management or hybrid is more realistic.

Whichever path the board chooses, the fiduciary duty does not change. The accounting must enforce fund separation. The records must be preserved. The disbursements must be controlled. Software gets a board there cheaply. Management gets a board there at a premium. Doing neither is not an option.
