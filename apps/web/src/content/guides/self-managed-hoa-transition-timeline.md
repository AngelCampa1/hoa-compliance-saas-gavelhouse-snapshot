## Why 90 days, not 30 or 180

Thirty days is too short. Document handoffs, bank signature changes, and vendor notifications each take 2 to 4 weeks individually, and they cannot run perfectly in parallel because some have hard dependencies on others. Boards that try to cut over in 30 days routinely lose vendor history, miss insurance certificate updates, and discover six months later that the management company never handed over the reserve study source files.

One hundred and eighty days is too long. The board loses momentum, the management company loses motivation to assist (since they are a lame duck for half a year), and owners get confused about who is in charge. Ninety days is the goldilocks window: long enough to do the work properly, short enough to maintain urgency.

## What changes for the board on day one of self-management

The board takes on five categories of responsibility that the management company previously absorbed:

1. **Financial operations.** Bookkeeping, reconciliation, owner billing, vendor payments, financial reporting.
2. **Compliance.** Reserve study cadence, audit prep, state-mandated disclosures, insurance renewals.
3. **Vendor management.** Contracts, performance, payments, dispute resolution.
4. **Owner communication.** Statements, notices, inquiry response, meeting management.
5. **Document custody.** Board records, financial files, insurance binders, legal documents.

Of these, financial operations is the most visible, but compliance is the highest-risk. Software that enforces fund accounting at the ledger level and keeps a full audit trail converts the highest-risk category into a routine one.

## The bridge bookkeeper question

Some boards hire a part-time bookkeeper for the first 90 to 180 days post-cutover. This is rarely a bad idea. A bookkeeper at $30–$50/hour for 5 to 10 hours/week costs $7,000–$25,000/year, which is dramatically less than a management contract while giving the volunteer treasurer breathing room to learn the workflows.

Boards that skip the bridge bookkeeper and rely on a heroic volunteer treasurer report two failure modes: the treasurer quits within a year, or financial errors compound until the first post-transition audit surfaces them. Both outcomes cost more than the bridge bookkeeper would have.

## Document custody is non-negotiable

State law usually requires HOAs to retain financial records for five to seven years. The management company holds those records during the contract. The handoff inventory at day 60 is the moment to confirm what they hold, what format it is in, and how they will deliver it. Common gaps:

- Reserve study source files (not just the PDF — the underlying spreadsheet with the model)
- Pre-2020 board meeting minutes (often in someone''s personal email)
- Insurance binder history (carriers change, but liability tail follows policy history)
- Vendor 1099 history (matters for the next tax season)
- Owner ledger transaction history beyond the past 12 months

Make the management company put the inventory in writing with delivery dates. A handshake handoff disappears when the management company''s next contract starts and their attention shifts.

## Owner communication: less is more

Owners care about three things during the transition: their dues stay the same, their payment method still works, and someone is reachable when something breaks. The owner communication should answer those three questions in two paragraphs, with contact information for the new board treasurer.

Long-form explanations of "why we''re going self-managed" are best saved for the next annual meeting. Owners who want detail will ask. Owners who don''t want detail will be confused by it.

## Insurance and the transition

The board''s D&O policy continues — but it should be reviewed at transition because the risk profile changes. A self-managed board carries different exposure than a management-company-supervised one, and some carriers price differently. Get a refreshed quote at day 30, bind the new policy at day 75, and confirm continuous coverage at cutover.

The general liability and property policies usually transfer cleanly. The carrier needs to know about the change of point-of-contact and the change of certificate-holder, but the underlying coverage continues.

## The post-90-day review

At day 120 (30 days after cutover), do a written review with the full board:

- Are bank balances reconciling?
- Is the reserve fund balance reconciling to the bank and are contributions posting to the correct fund account?
- Have owner inquiries been handled within reasonable response times?
- Are vendor payments going out on time?
- Has any document handoff item slipped?
- Is the treasurer''s actual workload sustainable?

Anything answered "no" or "partly" gets a tracked item with a new deadline. The transition is not done until the post-90-day review reads clean.

## When the transition does not work

Self-management is not for every association. Communities with absentee owners, complex litigation, ongoing construction defect claims, or treasurers without 5 to 8 hours/week to spare are honest candidates for staying with a management company. The transition exercise itself is a stress test — boards that lose two volunteers during the 90 days are getting a signal about future capacity.

The good news: a board that completes the 90-day transition successfully has the operational discipline to run self-managed indefinitely, because the same workflows that drove the transition keep the operation healthy.
