      Every HOA in a state with a solar access statute should have written
      architectural standards specifically addressing solar installations. These
      standards should be drafted with the help of a local HOA attorney and
      reviewed against current state law. At minimum, the standards should
      address: what application materials are required (installation plan, panel
      specifications, contractor license), placement preferences (rear-facing
      preferred, not visible from street where allowed by statute), hardware
      color and finish standards, insurance requirements, and the review
      timeline. Written standards protect the board from ad hoc decision-making
      and give homeowners a clear roadmap for getting approved. Do not rely on
      generic CC&R language written before solar access statutes were enacted —
      those provisions may be unenforceable and could expose the board to
      liability if relied upon to deny an application.
  - title: Create a solar application form and checklist
    content: >-
      A complete application is what triggers the review clock (45 days in
      California). Define "complete application" in your written standards so
      the clock starts only when all required materials are submitted. A typical
      solar application packet includes: site plan showing panel placement on
      the roof, panel manufacturer specifications and model numbers, mounting
      system specifications, contractor name and license number, proof of
      permit application or intent to permit, and a certificate of insurance.
      Having a checklist makes it easy for staff or a management company to
      confirm completeness before the board review begins. Never start the clock
      on an incomplete application — send a written deficiency notice within a
      few days of receipt so the applicant can complete the file.
  - title: Conduct the review and issue a written response
    content: >-
      When the application is complete, assign it to the architectural review
      committee (or the board if no committee exists) for review against the
      written standards. Review for compliance with placement and hardware
      standards only — do not evaluate whether solar is "appropriate" for the
      community or whether it will change the neighborhood's character. Those are
      not valid grounds for denial in states with solar access statutes. If the
      installation as proposed meets your standards, issue written approval with
      any conditions (such as "approved with the condition that mounting hardware
      be powder-coated to match roof color"). If the installation requires
      modification, issue a written conditional approval or denial with specific
      grounds and an invitation to resubmit a modified plan. Never simply fail
      to respond — that is a deemed approval in California and similar states,
      and it means the homeowner can proceed without the conditions you wanted
      to impose.
  - title: Maintain an approval record and inspect completed work
    content: >-
      After approval and installation, inspect the completed work against the
      approved plans. Document the inspection with photographs. If the
      installation materially deviates from the approved plans — different panel
      placement, different mounting hardware — issue a written notice requesting
      correction. Maintain a record of all solar applications, approvals,
      conditions, and inspection results. This documentation is critical if a
      dispute arises later about whether the board properly administered the
      approval process or whether a particular installation was approved with
      or without conditions.

## The Board's Job Has Changed

Twenty years ago, HOA boards had broad authority to block solar panel installations on aesthetic grounds. Today, that authority has been largely stripped away by statute in most states where solar adoption is highest.

California, Florida, Texas, Arizona, Colorado, Nevada, New Mexico, Hawaii, Maryland, and Virginia all have laws that limit HOA authority to restrict solar energy systems. In these states — which collectively represent a significant majority of all HOA-governed homes — the board's role is not to decide whether solar is acceptable but to administer a reasonable approval process for installations that meet aesthetic and placement standards.

Boards that still operate under the assumption that they can block solar installations are creating legal exposure. This guide explains what the law requires, what boards can still control, and how to run an approval process that holds up legally.

## What Solar Access Statutes Actually Say

The key phrase across most solar access statutes is that HOA restrictions on solar installations are void if they "effectively prohibit" or "unreasonably restrict" solar energy systems. What counts as unreasonable differs by state, but courts have been consistent: aesthetic objections alone are not sufficient grounds for denial.

### California: The Strongest Homeowner Protections

California Civil Code Section 714 is the model that many other states have copied. It voids any "deed restriction, covenant, condition, or any provision of a common interest development" that prohibits solar energy systems.

The statute allows HOAs to impose restrictions that are "reasonable," defined as restrictions that:

- Do not significantly increase the cost of the system (courts have interpreted this as no more than $1,000 in added cost)
- Do not significantly decrease the system's efficiency (no more than 10% reduction)
- Serve a legitimate aesthetic or safety purpose

The attorney fee provision in California's statute is the real enforcement mechanism. A homeowner who wins a solar access lawsuit recovers attorney fees from the HOA. This inverts the normal litigation calculus — the HOA, not the homeowner, faces the greater financial risk from a dispute.

California also requires HOAs to act on solar applications within 45 days. No response equals approval.

### Florida: Prohibition Extended to All Renewable Energy

Florida Statute 163.04 prohibits any deed restriction, covenant, or homeowners association rule from restricting solar panels or other renewable energy devices on properties. The Florida statute is broad and applies to all types of renewable energy — not just solar.

Florida courts have been willing to issue injunctions requiring HOAs to allow solar installations when boards have improperly denied applications.

### Texas: Roof Control Is the Key

Texas Property Code Chapter 202 takes a different approach. It prohibits HOA rules from restricting solar panels installed on a roof that the homeowner owns or controls. The "controls" language is important: in most townhome developments, owners control their roofs, which means the Texas statute applies. In condominiums where the roof is common property, the statute may not protect the individual unit owner.

Texas does not have the same 45-day response window as California, but the general prohibition on unreasonable restrictions applies.

## What HOAs Can Still Require

Even in states with the strongest solar access protections, boards retain meaningful authority to set reasonable installation standards.

### Placement Standards

The most defensible placement standard is a preference that panels not be visible from the street. California courts have upheld street-visibility standards when they are applied consistently and do not require placement that renders the system economically unviable. If the only viable roof surface faces the street, requiring rear-only placement is likely void — it would effectively prohibit the installation.

### Hardware and Appearance Standards

Boards can require that mounting hardware be colored to match the roof, that panel frames be standard industry finishes, and that conduit runs be concealed or minimized. These requirements are generally considered reasonable as long as they do not add significant cost. Requiring custom-colored mounting hardware that adds $3,000 to the installation cost would likely be unenforceable in California.

### Documentation and Insurance Requirements

Requiring a complete application with installation plans, permit information, and proof of insurance is a legitimate administrative requirement — not a restriction on solar access. Boards should define what constitutes a complete application in writing and stick to that definition.

### Post-Installation Inspection

Requiring that the completed installation match the approved plans is a legitimate condition. If the homeowner installs panels in a different location than approved or uses different mounting hardware, the board can require correction.

## Condo-Specific Considerations

Condominiums present a distinct legal issue because unit owners typically do not own the roof. The roof is a common element maintained by the HOA. California, Florida, and several other states have addressed this by:

- Allowing HOAs to establish shared solar programs that benefit the entire building
- Permitting unit owners to install solar on exclusive use common elements (balconies, patios)
- Authorizing HOAs to enter lease agreements with unit owners for roof space

For condo boards, the question is not just whether the owner can install solar — it is whether the owner owns the surface on which the installation would occur. This requires reviewing the CC&Rs and applicable state law together.

## How to Respond When a Request Comes In

When a homeowner submits a solar installation request, the board should:

1. **Acknowledge receipt in writing** within a few days, confirming receipt date and whether the application is complete
2. **Send a deficiency notice** if the application is incomplete, listing specifically what is missing
3. **Assign to the ARC or board** for review once the application is complete
4. **Issue written approval or denial** within the required statutory window (45 days in California)
5. **Document the decision and grounds** in the board's records

If the board approves the installation with conditions (such as specific placement or hardware requirements), state the conditions clearly in writing. If the board denies the request, the denial letter must state specific grounds and, in most states, invite the homeowner to submit a modified application.

## Boards Still Get to Set Standards — They Just Cannot Set Prohibitions

The core shift that solar access statutes created is this: boards moved from being gatekeepers who could say yes or no, to administrators who must say yes unless the installation violates specific, reasonable standards that do not effectively prevent the installation from happening.

That shift requires HOAs to do the work of creating written standards before applications arrive. A board that has never adopted written solar standards is in a worse position than one with clear written guidelines, because without standards, every application decision is ad hoc — and ad hoc denials are easier to challenge.

Investing the time to adopt reasonable, legally reviewed solar installation standards protects the board's ability to impose legitimate requirements while eliminating the exposure that comes from improper denials.
