## Why condo software is structurally different

Pure HOAs operate within state-level governance frameworks. Condos operate within the same state frameworks plus federal lender oversight via Fannie Mae, FHA, and VA project-eligibility review. The federal layer is the difference. A pure HOA can have weak financial reporting and still function; a condo with weak financial reporting starts losing sales because lenders refuse to fund mortgages.

After the 2021 Surfside collapse, the lender oversight regime tightened significantly. Fannie Mae issued temporary requirements that became permanent. Florida passed SB-4D requiring milestone inspections and SIRS for older buildings. Other states followed. The compliance load for condo boards increased materially in a short window, and software designed for the pre-Surfside world has been racing to catch up.

## What lenders ask for, in order

When a unit goes under contract, the buyer's lender requests a project-eligibility package. The typical list:

1. **Fannie Mae Form 1076** (short form) or the longer Form 1071, depending on the loan
2. **Most recent budget**
3. **Most recent reserve study** (and SIRS where applicable)
4. **Most recent audited financials**
5. **Master insurance policy declarations** (property, liability, fidelity, D&O)
6. **Pending litigation disclosure**
7. **Special assessment history and pending assessments**
8. **Owner-occupancy ratio**
9. **Delinquency ratio** (units 60+ days past due)
10. **Milestone inspection report** (where applicable)

Items 2, 4, 7, 8, and 9 draw directly from the association's financial records. Items 3 and 10 are professional work products from reserve specialists and engineers — the board commissions those, not the software. Item 1 is the synthesis the board or its managing agent completes using all of the above.

A board with clean, separated financial records and a full audit log in Gavelhouse can pull the financial pieces of this package fast. A board living in QuickBooks plus Google Drive plus the prior treasurer's laptop takes a week and frequently misses an item, blowing up the closing.

## The owner-equity stakes

When lenders refuse to fund a unit because the project documentation is weak, the unit can only sell to cash buyers, who pay 10% to 15% less than financed buyers. That discount comes out of the seller's equity. Multiply by 50 to 200 units in a building and the dollar value of strong project documentation becomes obvious. Condo boards that under-invest in financial record-keeping are quietly transferring owner equity to cash investors.

## SIRS in particular

Structural Integrity Reserve Studies are the most consequential compliance addition in Florida and similar states. They:

- Apply to specific building-age thresholds (30+ years statewide, 25+ in coastal counties in Florida)
- Cover only structural elements (roof, load-bearing walls, foundations, stairwells, balconies, plumbing, mechanical, electrical, fireproofing, waterproofing, windows, exterior doors, structural concrete)
- Require funding levels mandated by statute, not by board discretion
- Must be produced by qualified professionals on a fixed cadence

Gavelhouse's separate fund classifications matter because mixing SIRS reserves with general reserves invites the same commingling problem QuickBooks introduces between operating and reserve cash. The fund-accounting layer at the database level prevents the mixing. The reserve study itself — and tracking whether funding levels are on target — is the board's responsibility, supported by its reserve specialist.

## Milestone inspections and the cascading-failure risk

Florida buildings 30+ years old (25+ on the coast) require milestone inspections at age 30 and every 10 years thereafter. Missing a milestone inspection deadline triggers two cascading effects:

1. **Lender refusal on all sales** until the inspection is completed and reported.
2. **Insurance carrier reaction**, which can include premium increases or non-renewal.

The inspection itself takes weeks to schedule and complete with a qualified engineer. The board needs to manage that calendar — Gavelhouse does not track inspection due dates or send reminders. What Gavelhouse does is ensure that when the inspection is complete and a lender asks for the financial side of the package, the treasurer can produce accurate, separated fund balances and a clean audit log without a week of reconciliation.

## Fidelity insurance and project eligibility

Fannie Mae requires the association to carry fidelity coverage at a level tied to the operating budget plus reserves. Boards routinely under-insure here because the calculation requires up-to-date reserve and operating balances. Gavelhouse keeps those balances current and separated at the fund level, so the treasurer has accurate numbers for the fidelity coverage calculation when it comes up in a project review.

## Owner-occupancy ratio tracking

Fannie Mae has owner-occupancy thresholds for project eligibility. The ratio is calculated as owner-occupied units divided by total units. Boards that do not track owner-occupancy systematically end up estimating it on the spot when a lender asks, which produces inconsistent answers across multiple sales in the same month — a red flag for any underwriter. The board is responsible for tracking unit ownership status; Gavelhouse supports the financial side of each transaction in the ledger.

## What Gavelhouse does and does not do

Gavelhouse enforces operating-vs-reserve fund separation at the database layer, captures a full audit log of every transaction with user, timestamp, and before/after values, and produces clean financial statements and exports. Those are the financial records the board draws on when completing lender questionnaires, working with a reserve specialist, or preparing state disclosures.

Gavelhouse does not produce reserve studies, perform or track milestone inspections, auto-generate Fannie Mae forms, track percent-funded ratios against study targets, or provide an owner-facing portal. Those functions belong to the board, its engineers, and its reserve specialists — Gavelhouse handles the fund accounting that makes their work easier.

## When to migrate

Condo boards should migrate during a lull, never during a pending sale. The window between annual meetings is typically 2 to 3 months of low activity — perfect for the standard 30-day onboarding plan. Avoid starting onboarding within 60 days of a milestone inspection deadline or a lender disclosure spike. Coordinate with the property management company (if applicable) to align onboarding with their handoff timeline.
