## Why parking is the most-litigated area of HOA enforcement

We built Gavelhouse because volunteer boards routinely take actions that look administrative but are actually high-liability legal acts. Towing is the clearest example. The decision to hook a vehicle and move it off the property is a seizure of personal property. If the board did not have the authority, the signage, the notice, or the contract right, that seizure becomes conversion — and conversion claims survive against directors personally when the corporate veil is pierced for ultra vires acts.

Insurance carriers track this. Wrongful-towing claims are now one of the top three reported categories under HOA D&O policies, alongside discrimination and selective enforcement. The average claim runs $8,000 to $35,000 in damages, plus the homeowner''s attorney fees, which most state HOA acts shift to the prevailing homeowner.

## What the CC&Rs actually authorize

Before any enforcement step, the board must verify the authority chain. A common pattern is: the recorded CC&Rs say the board may "regulate parking on common areas." The board then adopts a rule banning commercial vehicles, then another rule banning overnight street parking, then another limiting each unit to two vehicles. Each new rule is one step further from the recorded grant. When a homeowner challenges, the court reads the CC&R grant strictly and may strike rules that exceed it.

### Common rules that exceed CC&R authority

- Limits on the number of vehicles per unit when CC&Rs only authorize regulation of "type" of vehicle.
- Bans on out-of-state plates.
- Requirements that vehicles be operable or registered when CC&Rs only address "abandoned" vehicles.
- Restrictions on company vehicles parked overnight when CC&Rs only address "commercial vehicles" (a marked panel van versus a salesperson''s sedan with a magnetic decal is a common dispute).

Run every parking rule through this question: does the recorded CC&R grant cover this exact restriction? If not, do not enforce it until you amend the CC&Rs.

## State notice requirements

### Texas

Texas Occupations Code Chapter 2308 governs non-consent tows. For most violations, the operator must place a 24-hour notice on the windshield before towing. The notice must include the date and time of placement, the violation, and contact information for the property owner. Exceptions include fire lanes, handicap spaces, blocking another vehicle, and blocking access.

Signage requirements: a 17-by-22-inch sign at each vehicle entrance, with white background and red lettering, listing the tow operator name and 24-hour phone, plus the words "Unauthorized Vehicles Will Be Towed at the Owner''s Expense." Missing or noncompliant signage makes the tow a wrongful tow as a matter of law.

### California

California Vehicle Code 22658 requires signage at each entrance and a written authorization on file from the property owner before each tow, except for tows from fire lanes or vehicles blocking driveways. The HOA can pre-authorize the tow company in writing for specific violation types, but the authorization must be specific. A blanket "tow anything you think is in violation" authorization will not meet the statute.

### Florida

Florida Statute 715.07 governs non-consent tows. The operator must give written notice to the local police before towing, and must accept payment by cash or major credit card. The HOA must post signage at every entrance.

## D&O coverage usually covers wrongful tows. Until it does not.

Most HOA D&O policies cover wrongful-towing claims under "wrongful acts of directors and officers." But two exclusions commonly apply:

1. **Bodily injury exclusion** — if the tow injures the homeowner (chasing the truck, confrontation), the bodily injury claim drops out of D&O and into general liability, which usually has lower limits and a per-claim retention.
2. **Intentional acts exclusion** — if the homeowner can show the board knew the rule was unenforceable or knew the signage was missing and towed anyway, the carrier will deny under the intentional-acts exclusion. This is why documenting the signage audit and the CC&R authority review matters: it converts the act from "intentional" to "negligent," and negligence is covered.

## Building an enforcement record that holds up

The right pattern is administrative and boring: written rule, written notice schedule, posted signs, photographed violations, dated windshield notices, photographed notices, documented authorization for each tow. Inconsistency is what breaks the defense. If your community has 14 violations a month and you only enforce on three, expect a selective-enforcement counterclaim. Track every violation, send every notice, and either enforce uniformly or stop enforcing.

Boards that move parking enforcement out of email threads and into a dated, photographed log inside their HOA management system see far fewer claims. The system does not have to be sophisticated. It has to be consistent and time-stamped.
