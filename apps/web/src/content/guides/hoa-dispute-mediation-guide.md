# HOA Dispute Mediation: When It Is Required and How It Works

We built Gavelhouse from the builder side of the table — we are not HOA attorneys. But after watching board after board stumble into expensive litigation that could have been resolved in a $400 mediation session, we want to make the procedural rules clear. Most HOA disputes that end up in court should never have gotten there.

This guide covers when mediation is mandatory, how it works, what it costs, and what happens if a party refuses.

## Why mediation matters more than most boards think

Litigation is the most expensive way to resolve an HOA dispute. Attorney fees alone routinely exceed $10,000 before either side files a motion for summary judgment. Mediation, by contrast, typically costs $200-$800 per session and resolves most disputes in one or two sessions.

But cost is not the only reason. In several states, mediation is procedurally required before a lawsuit can proceed. Boards that skip mediation get their cases dismissed or stayed, and they often pay the other side's attorney fees as a sanction.

## States that require pre-suit mediation

### Florida — the strictest framework

Florida Statute 720.311 requires pre-suit mediation for most covenant disputes between HOAs and members. The statute applies to disputes involving:

- Covenant interpretation and enforcement
- Amendments to governing documents
- Meetings of the membership
- Elections and voting
- Common area access

Either party can initiate by serving a statutory demand identifying the dispute and proposing certified mediators. The other party has 20 days to respond. If mediation fails or is refused, the requesting party can proceed to court and may recover attorney fees from a non-cooperating party.

Post-Surfside legislation tightened compliance expectations even further. Florida courts increasingly dismiss HOA suits that lack proper pre-suit mediation documentation.

### California — Davis-Stirling dual track

California's Davis-Stirling Act (Civil Code 5900-5965) creates two pre-litigation steps:

1. **Internal Dispute Resolution (IDR)** — an informal meet-and-confer process under Civil Code 5910. Either party can request it, and the association must offer a meeting within reasonable time.
2. **Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR)** — a formal mediation step under Civil Code 5925. Required before litigating most enforcement actions, with limited exceptions for emergency matters.

Failure to comply with ADR can result in dismissal or denial of attorney fees that the prevailing party would otherwise recover.

### Arizona, Nevada, and others

Arizona (ARS 33-1242 for condos), Nevada (NRS 38.310), and several other states have category-specific mediation or ADR requirements. Texas has a less formal framework but courts often expect good-faith resolution attempts before granting injunctive relief.

If your community is in a state not listed above, mediation may still be a smart first step even when it is not mandatory.

## What disputes are mediatable

Most HOA disputes are eligible for mediation:

- Covenant violation disputes (architectural, landscaping, parking, pets)
- Fine and assessment challenges
- Election and meeting disputes
- Document interpretation disagreements
- Common area access issues

What is not mediatable:

- Emergency matters requiring immediate injunction (active flooding, safety threats)
- Lien recording itself (though disputes about underlying assessments often are)
- Foreclosure in many states (though pre-foreclosure assessment disputes may qualify)
- Criminal conduct allegations

## The mediation process

### Step 1: Statutory demand

In states with pre-suit mediation, the process starts with a formal demand. This is not a casual letter. Florida requires specific statutory language; California IDR requires a written meeting offer. Defective demands can reset the timeline and hand the other side a procedural advantage.

### Step 2: Mediator selection

Both parties agree on a mediator from a court-approved list. Florida maintains a roster of certified HOA mediators. California associations often use members of local bar association ADR panels.

### Step 3: Pre-mediation preparation

Before the session, the board should compile:

- Relevant covenants and bylaws sections
- Board meeting minutes referencing the dispute
- Violation notices, fines, and correspondence
- Financial records if assessments are at issue
- A clear settlement range and authority

Bring a board member with authority to settle. A mediator cannot move a case if the only attendee has to phone the board for every decision.

### Step 4: The session

A certified mediator does not decide the case. They facilitate discussion, identify common ground, and propose settlement structures. Sessions typically run 2-4 hours.

Most disputes settle in one or two sessions. The settlement agreement is binding once signed and can be enforced in court.

## Costs

Typical fee ranges:

- **Florida pre-suit mediation:** $200-$400 per session, often split
- **California ADR:** $400-$800 per session, often split
- **Private mediation (non-statutory):** $300-$1,000 per hour for senior mediators

Compared to litigation, where pre-trial costs alone often exceed $10,000, mediation is dramatically cheaper.

## What happens if the other side refuses

In states where mediation is mandatory:

- The requesting party serves the demand and documents the refusal
- They can then file suit and seek recovery of attorney fees from the non-cooperating party
- Courts generally view refusal to mediate as bad faith, which influences fee-shifting and settlement leverage

A board that refuses mediation almost always regrets it. Even if you win on the merits, you may lose your fee recovery and signal bad faith to the judge.

## Documenting the process

Every mediation step belongs in the association's permanent records. Board minutes should reflect:

- The decision to send or respond to a mediation demand
- The selection of the mediator
- The date and outcome of each session
- The settlement terms (or the impasse)

Sloppy documentation creates appellate vulnerability. Tight documentation often deters appeals entirely.

## When to skip mediation

There are real situations where mediation is the wrong move:

- Active emergencies requiring immediate injunctive relief
- Cases where the homeowner has refused all prior communication
- Disputes involving criminal conduct or restraining orders
- Statutory deadlines that would lapse during mediation

Even in these situations, document why mediation was bypassed. Courts forgive procedural shortcuts when the record explains them.

## The compliance angle

Mediation is a compliance step, not a courtesy. Boards that treat it as optional run procedural risks that swamp the underlying dispute. Boards that treat it as a structured statutory process — demand letter, certified mediator, prepared session, documented outcome — resolve most disputes faster and cheaper than litigation, and protect their fiduciary record if litigation does become necessary.

Before any covenant enforcement escalates, check your state statute. Most of the time, mediation is the path your state has already chosen for you.
