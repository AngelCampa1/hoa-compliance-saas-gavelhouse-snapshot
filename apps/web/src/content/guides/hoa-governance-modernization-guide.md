## Why "modernization" is mostly about writing things down

Most HOA boards we talked to operate on informal consensus. The board has practiced architectural review the same way for ten years. There is a "policy" but it lives in the long-time treasurer's head. When the treasurer steps down, the policy goes with them, and the new board reinvents whatever it can remember from old emails.

This is the failure mode governance modernization solves. The fix is not technology. The fix is putting the practices in writing so they survive turnover, so D&O insurers can verify due process, and so owners and courts can hold the board to a documented standard rather than to whatever the prior officers say happened.

## What the written record actually does

Written policies serve four functions:

**1. Continuity across board turnover.** Volunteer boards rotate. Without written policy, every new officer learns from scratch what the prior officer did, and decisions inevitably drift. Written policy is the mechanism that lets a new treasurer pick up the books on day one and continue what the prior treasurer was doing.

**2. Defense against fiduciary duty claims.** When an owner sues the board over reserve fund management, or an unauthorized special assessment, or alleged self-dealing, the written record is what the defense relies on. "We followed the policy in place at the time" is a stronger position than "we used our best judgment."

**3. D&O coverage.** Insurers expect boards to have followed reasonable process. Policies document the process. Policy gaps are coverage gaps — claims arising from areas without written policy face higher reduction or denial risk.

**4. Audit credibility.** Auditors test internal controls by tracing samples of decisions back to documented policy. Boards without written policy have weak control environments by definition. The audit findings reflect that.

## The minimum written governance package

Beyond the recorded declaration, bylaws, and CC&Rs, every HOA should have:

**Committee charters.** One per standing committee. Contents: purpose, scope, authority, membership, term, meeting cadence, voting rules, reporting line, recordkeeping.

**Conflict-of-interest policy.** Disclosure requirements, recusal procedures, scope of conflicts (financial, personal, family, business), filing and review process.

**Executive session protocol.** Topics permitted under state law, notice procedure, attendance rules, recording requirements, ratification in open session.

**Document retention policy.** Categories of records, retention periods, storage location, destruction procedure when retention period expires.

**Electronic communication policy.** Official versus personal channels, when board action by electronic means is permitted, owner communication standards.

**Collection policy.** Late fee triggers, lien filing thresholds, payment plan terms, attorney engagement.

**Architectural review process.** Application requirements, review timeline, decision criteria, appeal process.

**Vendor approval policy.** Bid requirements, approval thresholds, conflict checks, contract review.

This is roughly 20–30 pages of policy total for a typical small HOA. Once drafted, the documents need light annual review and periodic updates. The drafting effort is one-time; the value compounds across years.

## Conflict-of-interest: the policy boards underestimate

Conflict-of-interest is the single most common source of HOA litigation we have seen referenced in trade press. The patterns:

- A board member's spouse owns the landscaping contractor; the board awards the contract without disclosure or competitive bid.
- A board member is the owner of a unit with extensive deferred maintenance; the board votes to spend reserve funds on common element work that primarily benefits that unit.
- A board member is a real estate agent listing units in the community; the board adopts marketing-friendly policies that reduce enforcement.
- A board member's family member is a contractor who completes work; the board approves payment without competitive comparison.

Each of these patterns may be entirely innocent. The problem is not the underlying transaction; the problem is the absence of disclosure, recusal, and documentation. With a written policy and proper compliance, the same transactions survive scrutiny. Without the policy, they look like self-dealing.

A workable policy requires:

- Annual written disclosure from every board member of all financial and personal interests potentially relevant to board business.
- Updated disclosure within 30 days of any new interest arising.
- Filing with the board secretary and review by the board chair.
- Recusal from votes and discussion on matters where the disclosed interest applies.
- Documentation of recusal in board minutes.

## Executive session: where audit trails break

State HOA open-meeting laws (California's Open Meeting Act under Civil Code 4900, Florida's Sunshine provisions, similar laws in most states) require board meetings to be held in the presence of members with limited exceptions for executive session. The exceptions cover: pending litigation, personnel matters, owner discipline, contract negotiation, attorney-client privileged communications.

Boards routinely abuse executive session by holding general business there to avoid owner observation. This is illegal and creates audit trail problems because actions taken in executive session must be ratified in open session — and the ratification often does not happen, or happens with insufficient detail to make the decision traceable.

A clean executive session protocol:

- Notice executive session in advance with the topic category (pending litigation, personnel, etc.) — not the specific matter, but enough that members know an executive session is occurring.
- Meet in private only on the noticed category.
- If a decision is made, ratify in open session at the same meeting or the next regular meeting, with sufficient detail in minutes to identify what was decided.
- Keep separate executive session minutes that capture the decision but not necessarily the deliberation.

Boards that follow this protocol have clean audit trails and survive owner records inspections. Boards that do not face open-meeting violations and the related fiduciary duty exposure.

## Document retention: more than a compliance line item

Most state statutes set a seven-year retention floor for financial records. Boards interpret this as "delete after seven years," which is wrong. Reserve fund claims can reach back further; construction defect claims can reach back to original developer turnover; tax disputes can reach back to the period at issue.

A retention policy that works:

- **Permanent retention:** Governing documents, board meeting minutes, reserve studies, audit reports, major capital project records, declarations of restrictions.
- **Long retention (10+ years):** Vendor contracts and major correspondence, architectural review decisions, tax returns and supporting documents.
- **Standard retention (7 years):** Routine financial records, general correspondence, owner ledger detail.
- **Short retention (1–2 years):** Routine vendor invoices below threshold, transient working papers.

Storage costs nothing meaningful for digital records. The defensive position is over-retention, with periodic purges of clearly transient material.

## Why this work is worth doing

Boards that complete the modernization package — somewhere between a weekend of focused drafting and a quarter of incremental work — find that meetings get shorter, decisions get clearer, owner trust improves, and D&O renewals get easier. The written record is doing the work that institutional memory used to do, and it survives turnover in a way that personal memory cannot.

Boards that skip modernization keep paying for it: in extra meeting time, in re-litigated past decisions, in claims that could have been defended cleanly with documentation, and in D&O premiums that creep up at every renewal. The math is not close.
