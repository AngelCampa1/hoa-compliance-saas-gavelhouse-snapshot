bluf: >-
  HOA board members have a fiduciary duty to the homeowners they serve. That
  duty includes maintaining adequate reserve funds, following your governing
  documents, and making decisions in good faith. Boards that ignore reserve
  requirements, commingle funds, or fail to document their decision-making
  process create personal liability risk for every board member who voted to let
  it happen.
faqs:
  - q: Can HOA board members be personally sued?
    a: >-
      Yes. Board members can be personally liable if they breach their fiduciary
      duty, for example by knowingly approving an operating budget that does not
      fund reserves adequately, by commingling reserve and operating funds in
      violation of state law, or by making decisions that benefit themselves at
      the expense of homeowners. D&O (Directors and Officers) insurance reduces
      but does not eliminate this risk.
  - q: What is D&O insurance for HOA boards?
    a: >-
      Directors and Officers liability insurance covers legal defense costs and
      damages for board members named in lawsuits related to their board
      decisions. Most HOA insurance advisors recommend it. D&O insurance does
      not cover intentional misconduct, fraud, or decisions made in bad faith.
      It covers good-faith mistakes. If your board made a documented, reasonable
      decision about reserve funding and a homeowner still sues, D&O covers your
      defense.
  - q: Does a special assessment mean the board did something wrong?
    a: >-
      Not necessarily. Special assessments happen when a capital expense comes
      due that reserves cannot fully cover. A board that maintained adequate
      reserves, tracked funding levels annually, and still needed a special
      assessment for an unexpected expense acted responsibly. A board that had
      no reserve tracking, no reserve study, and then hit homeowners with a
      large special assessment is in a much more vulnerable position if anyone
      challenges the decision.
definitions:
  - term: Fiduciary Duty
    definition: >-
      The legal obligation of HOA board members to act in the best interests of
      the association and its homeowners. For HOA boards, fiduciary duty
      encompasses the duty of care (make informed decisions), the duty of
      loyalty (act in the association's interest, not your own), and the duty to
      act within authority (follow governing documents and state law).
  - term: D&O Insurance
    definition: >-
      Directors and Officers liability insurance that covers legal defense costs
      and damages for board members named in lawsuits related to their board
      decisions. D&O insurance covers good-faith mistakes but does not cover
      intentional misconduct, fraud, or decisions made in bad faith.
  - term: Reserve Fund
    definition: >-
      A separate savings account maintained by an HOA to cover major repairs and
      replacements of common area components. Most state HOA statutes require
      reserve funds to be kept separate from operating funds and funded
      according to a reserve study schedule.

    answer: >-

      proper fund separation, board members who allow operating expenses to be

      reserve statutes require reserves to be kept in a separate account, not
      just tracked as a separate line in a general ledger.
  - question: What documentation protects board members from liability?
    answer: >-
      Board meeting minutes are the primary legal record. Every meeting where
      reserve fund status is reviewed, discussed, or voted on should include the
      reserve balance, the percent-funded figure, any reserve study update
      status, and the board's decision or deferral on reserve-related motions. A
      clear record of informed decision-making is the board's main defense.

steps:
  - title: Understand your fiduciary duty
    content: >-
      As an HOA board member, you owe homeowners three duties: the duty of care
      (make informed decisions), the duty of loyalty (act in the association's
      interest, not your own), and the duty to act within authority (follow your
      governing documents and state law). The duty of care is where reserve fund
      management creates risk. If you vote to approve an annual budget that does
      not fund reserves, and you knew or should have known your reserves were
      inadequate, that vote is evidence of a care failure. 'We did not know
      reserves were required' is not a strong defense when your state has a
      reserve statute on the books. 'We tracked our reserve balance, had an
      up-to-date reserve study, and made a documented decision about funding
      level' is.
  - title: Audit reserve fund adequacy
    content: >-
      Pull your current reserve fund balance and compare it to your most recent
      reserve study's recommended funding level. If you do not have a reserve
      study, that is your first action item. If your reserve balance is below
      70% funded, your board should formally discuss a funding plan at the next
      meeting and record that discussion in the minutes. The record of the
      discussion matters. A board that acknowledged underfunding and voted on a
      remediation plan is in a better position than one where underfunding was
      never discussed. In states with reserve mandate statutes, the underfunding
      threshold that triggers mandatory disclosure or funding milestones varies.
      Check your state's specific requirements.
  - title: Document your compliance process
    content: >-
      Board meeting minutes are your primary legal record. Every board meeting
      where reserve fund status is reviewed, discussed, or voted on should have
      a clear minute entry. Include the reserve balance, the percent-funded
      figure, any reserve study update status, and the board's decision or
      deferral on any reserve-related motion. Document does not mean 'write a
      lot.' It means create a clear record that a reasonable person reading
      those minutes in three years could understand what the board knew and what
      it decided. If you review reserve status and take no action because
      funding is adequate, record that too.
  - title: Fix accounting to eliminate commingling risk
    content: >-
      If your board keeps operating and reserve funds in the same bank account
      or the same QuickBooks ledger without fund separation, you have
      commingling risk. Most states with reserve statutes require that reserve
      funds be kept in a separate account, not just tracked as a separate line
      in a general ledger. Open a dedicated reserve savings account if you do
      not have one. Ensure that no operating expenses are paid from that
      account. Record every transfer between operating and reserve accounts as a
      formal resolution or at minimum a board-approved budget line item. If you
      use accounting software, confirm it tracks operating and reserve funds as
      separate pools with independent reports, not as line items in a shared
      ledger. That distinction is what separates fund accounting from general
      ledger accounting, and it is what state reserve auditors look for.
