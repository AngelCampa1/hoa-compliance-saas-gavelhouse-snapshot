bluf: >-
  The annual meeting is the treasurer's most visible compliance moment. Most
  state HOA statutes require specific financial disclosures to homeowners--the
  exact requirements vary, but at minimum you need a year-end financial report,
  a current-year budget summary, and a reserve fund status update. Getting these
  reports wrong--or incomplete--is both a legal exposure and a homeowner
  relations problem. This guide covers what to prepare and how to structure the
  presentation.
steps:
  - title: Review what your state legally requires
    content: >-
      Before preparing any reports, verify your state's specific annual
      disclosure requirements. Most states require HOA boards to distribute a
      financial report to homeowners within 60-120 days of the fiscal year end
      and to present or distribute a budget summary before the new fiscal year
      begins. California requires a detailed pro forma operating budget, a
      reserve fund summary including current balance and percent funded, and a
      disclosure of any unpaid assessments for the prior year. Nevada requires
      reserve fund disclosures including balance, percent funded, and whether a
      reserve study has been conducted. Florida's post-2022 condo requirements
      add structural inspection disclosures for applicable buildings. Your state
      HOA statute is the authoritative source. Search for your state name plus
      'HOA annual disclosure requirements' to find the relevant section. Confirm
      requirements with a licensed attorney if your state's rules are ambiguous.
  - title: Prepare the year-end financial statements
    content: >-
      Year-end financial statements for most HOAs consist of three reports: a
      balance sheet showing assets, liabilities, and fund balances as of
      December 31 (or your fiscal year end), an income and expense statement
      comparing actual versus budgeted amounts for the full year, and a
      statement of fund balances showing opening balance, contributions,
      expenditures, and ending balance for both operating and reserve funds. The
      fund balance statement is the most legally important for most states. It
      shows that your operating and reserve funds were maintained separately
      throughout the year and that the reserve balance is a real number--not a
      ledger artifact from a single account with category tracking. If your
      accounting has been in QuickBooks or a single-ledger system, producing a
      clean fund balance statement may require working with your CPA to verify
      the numbers before presenting them to homeowners.
  - title: Prepare the reserve fund status report
    content: >-
      The reserve fund status report is a separate document (or section of the
      financial package) that covers three things: your current reserve balance
      as of the reporting date, the fully funded amount recommended by your most
      recent reserve study for this point in the funding schedule, and the ratio
      of the two--your percent funded. Present this information clearly even if
      the numbers are uncomfortable. Homeowners who receive a reserve status
      report showing 45% funded are in a better position to make informed
      decisions (support a budget with higher reserve contributions, understand
      why a special assessment may be coming) than homeowners who receive no
      reserve information and are surprised by a major assessment. If your
      community has not conducted a reserve study in the past five years,
      include a statement that the board is in the process of commissioning one
      and provide your best estimate of the current reserve balance. The absence
      of a reserve study is itself a disclosure item in some states.
  - title: Present the proposed budget for the new year
    content: >-
      The budget presentation should cover three items in plain language: total
      assessment income projected for the year (number of units times annual
      assessment per unit), total operating expenses budget by major category
      (common area maintenance, insurance, utilities, management fees if
      applicable, administrative costs), and reserve contribution for the year
      (the amount being transferred to the reserve fund, expressed as a total
      dollar amount and as a percentage of the operating assessment). Separate
      the reserve contribution from operating expenses so homeowners can see
      clearly how much of their assessment is funding operations versus building
      reserves. If the assessment rate is changing from the prior year, explain
      the reason: which expense categories increased, what the reserve study
      recommends, or why the prior year's budget was not adequate.
  - title: Prepare a capital projects update if any were completed or planned
    content: >-
      If any significant capital projects were completed in the prior year--roof
      replacement, parking lot resurfacing, equipment replacement--include a
      summary with actual cost versus reserve study estimate and the current
      reserve balance after the expenditure. If capital projects are planned in
      the coming year, include them in the budget presentation with funding
      source identified: reserve fund (if it covers the cost), operating budget
      (for smaller items), or special assessment (if the reserve is
      insufficient). Homeowners who understand that a capital project will draw
      down reserves are less surprised when the reserve balance decreases. The
      treasurer's job at the annual meeting is to give homeowners the
      information they need to evaluate whether the board is managing the
      community's finances responsibly.
  - title: "Distribute reports in advance, not just at the meeting"
    content: >-
      Most state HOA statutes require financial reports to be distributed to
      homeowners before or at the annual meeting--not read aloud at the meeting
      for the first time. Distribute the financial package (year-end statements,
      reserve fund status, proposed budget) at least two weeks before the annual
      meeting so homeowners have time to review them and prepare questions. For
      communities with 50 or more units, paper distribution may not be required
      if your governing documents allow electronic delivery to homeowners who
      have consented. Maintain records showing when you sent the financial
      package and to whom. If a homeowner later claims they never received the
      materials, your distribution record is your defense.
  - title: Handle Q&A prepared with written answers to likely questions
    content: >-
      Prepare written answers to the questions you know will come: Why is the
      assessment increasing? What happened to the reserve fund balance? When was
      the last reserve study? What does a special assessment process look like?
      When are major capital projects scheduled? Having written answers prepared
      for predictable questions serves two purposes: it gives you accurate,
      consistent responses under pressure, and the written preparation itself
      documents that the board considered these questions in advance of the
      meeting. If a question arises that requires verification before answering,
      say so: 'Let me confirm the exact figure and get back to you in writing
      within five business days.' That response is far better than guessing at a
      compliance-sensitive number in front of a crowd.
definitions:
  - term: Pro Forma Operating Budget
    definition: >-
      A prospective budget showing projected income and expenses for the
      upcoming fiscal year. California requires HOA boards to distribute a pro
      forma operating budget at least 30 days before the new fiscal year, with a
      reserve fund summary attached.
  - term: Reserve Disclosure
    definition: >-
      A required communication to homeowners about the status of the HOA reserve
      fund, including current balance and percent funded. Required at different
      intervals and in different formats depending on state--annually in many
      states, at unit sale in California, Nevada, and others.
  - term: Fund Balance Statement
    definition: >-
      A financial report showing the beginning balance, contributions,
      expenditures, and ending balance of each fund (operating and reserve) for
      a reporting period. Documents that funds were maintained separately
      throughout the period.
  - term: Special Assessment
    definition: >-
      A one-time charge levied against homeowners for expenses not covered by
      the regular operating budget or reserve fund. Special assessments are
      often a sign of inadequate reserve funding and require specific notice and
      vote procedures under most state HOA statutes.

faqs:
  - q: What financial reports are required at an HOA annual meeting?
    a: >-
      At minimum: a year-end financial statement (income/expense and balance
      sheet), a reserve fund status update showing current balance and percent
      funded, and a proposed budget for the upcoming year. State-specific
      requirements add to this minimum--California's pro forma budget, Nevada's
      reserve disclosure, Florida's various condo requirements. Your state HOA
      statute governs. Consult a licensed attorney if you are uncertain what
      your state requires.
  - q: >-
      What happens if the HOA board presents inaccurate financial information at
      the annual meeting?
    a: >-
      Inaccurate financial presentations create personal liability exposure for
      board members, particularly the treasurer. Homeowners who make decisions
      based on inaccurate financial information--voting to approve a budget,
      deciding whether to sell their unit--have grounds for claims against the
      board. The risk is not theoretical: HOA litigation over financial
      misrepresentation is documented in multiple states. Verify every number in
      your financial package against bank statements and accounting records
      before distributing.
  - q: >-
      Can the annual meeting financial reports be digital, or do they need to be
      paper?
    a: >-
      Most states allow digital delivery to homeowners who have provided email
      addresses and consented to electronic communications. Your governing
      documents may specify the delivery method. Check both your state statute
      and your CC&Rs. For communities where some homeowners have not consented
      to digital delivery, paper distribution is still required for those
      homeowners.
  - q: >-
      How do I present a low reserve fund balance without causing a homeowner
      panic?
    a: >-
      Present the facts with context: the current balance, the reserve study
      target, the percent funded, and the board's plan for addressing the
      shortfall. A presentation that says 'we are at 52% funded, our target is
      70%, and the board has approved increasing the reserve contribution by X%
      over the next three budget cycles' is informative without being alarming.
      Homeowners who receive no reserve information and are then surprised by a
      large special assessment react much more adversely than homeowners who
      have been informed of the funding trajectory over time.

      balance and percent funded, a proposed budget for the upcoming year with

    answer: >-
      State statutes typically specify 30-60 days before the annual meeting for

      gives homeowners time to review them and prepare questions, which leads to
      more productive meetings.

## The treasurer's visibility problem

The annual meeting is the one moment per year when the treasurer's work is fully visible to every homeowner. Boards that have maintained solid financial records throughout the year find this straightforward. Boards that have been operating on informal processes, unclear reserve accounting, or delayed reporting find the annual meeting stressful.

The preparation steps in this guide are designed for both situations. If your records are clean, the annual meeting is a confirmation exercise--verifying the numbers against bank statements and presenting them clearly. If your records have gaps, identifying them now and addressing them before the meeting is the right approach.

## What homeowners actually care about

In our experience building this platform, homeowners at annual meetings ask three financial questions with regularity: Is the reserve fund adequately funded? Is the assessment rate changing and why? Are there major capital projects coming that will cost more money? Every other financial question is secondary to those three.

A treasurer who can answer those three questions with clear documentation--and demonstrate that the board reviewed the reserve status at every board meeting during the year--handles the annual meeting from a position of credibility. A treasurer who cannot answer them, or who is answering them for the first time at the meeting without prior board review documentation, is in a difficult position.

## Software that makes this easier

The reports this guide describes--fund balance statements, percent-funded calculations, budget-versus-actual comparisons--require fund-separated accounting to produce accurately. A single-ledger QuickBooks setup or a spreadsheet-based approach makes these reports difficult to generate cleanly.

Purpose-built HOA accounting software that separates operating and reserve funds from the start makes the annual meeting report package a matter of running standard reports rather than reconstructing accounting records. The treasury function runs more smoothly throughout the year, and the annual meeting preparation is less stressful for everyone.
