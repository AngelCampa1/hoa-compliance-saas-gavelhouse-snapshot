## The five-policy HOA insurance stack

Every functioning HOA insurance program has at least five components, each covering a distinct risk:

1. **Master property** — the buildings, common-area structures, and (depending on form) unit finishes.
2. **Master general liability** — accidents and injuries on the property, excluding directors'' wrongful acts.
3. **Directors and officers (D&O)** — wrongful acts of directors and officers, including breach of fiduciary duty.
4. **Fidelity bond / crime coverage** — theft of association funds, including by board members, employees, or vendors.
5. **Umbrella** — excess liability above the underlying general liability and auto limits.

A community with paid staff adds workers compensation. A community that handles significant homeowner data should add cyber liability. A community in a flood zone needs separate flood coverage; standard property policies exclude flood almost universally.

## Master property: bare walls versus all-in

The master property policy covers the buildings. The detail that drives every claim dispute is the form: bare walls or all-in (also called single-entity).

**Bare walls** covers the building''s structural elements — framing, exterior walls, roof, common-area finishes. It stops at the unfinished interior surfaces of individual units. Everything from the drywall in is the unit owner''s responsibility under their HO-6 policy.

**All-in** covers everything the bare walls policy covers plus the original finishes and fixtures inside the units — original cabinets, original flooring, original appliances. Improvements the owner installed (upgraded countertops, custom built-ins) remain the owner''s responsibility.

Most state condo statutes require the CC&Rs to specify which form applies. The board needs to confirm the policy in force matches the CC&Rs. A bare walls policy on a community whose CC&Rs require all-in produces a coverage gap on every claim.

## Master general liability

GL covers accidents and injuries on the property: a slip on a sidewalk, a pool incident, an elevator failure. Standard limits are $1 million per occurrence, $2 million aggregate. Boards should confirm the policy specifically extends to amenity liabilities — pool, fitness center, dog park, playground — and that there are no exclusions for diving boards, slides, or trampolines if those exist on the property.

GL does not cover wrongful governance acts. A board sued for a wrongful violation notice or a discriminatory enforcement pattern triggers D&O, not GL.

## Directors and officers

D&O is the only policy that protects directors personally. The most common pattern of personal liability for HOA board members is:

- A homeowner sues the association and names individual directors.
- The association''s GL policy does not cover the directors as named insureds for governance claims.
- The association''s D&O policy covers the claim, but only if the policy is in force, the wrongful act is covered, and no exclusions apply.

Common D&O exclusions to watch:

- Intentional wrongful acts.
- Criminal acts.
- Bodily injury (use GL).
- Property damage to association property.
- Prior acts before the policy retroactive date.
- Employment practices (problematic if the association has staff).
- Insured-versus-insured (one director suing another).
- Discrimination (varies — many policies cover defense but not damages).

A community without D&O should add it before the next renewal. Premiums for $1 million in coverage typically run $800-$2,500 annually depending on size.

## Fidelity bond / crime coverage

Fidelity covers theft of association funds. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac both require coverage equal to three months of assessments plus the operating and reserve account balance, with a $5,000 minimum, for HOAs whose units secure conforming mortgages. State HOA acts often impose similar requirements.

The coverage should extend to all persons handling association funds: directors, officers, the management company, and any volunteer collecting payments. Confirm the management company''s own fidelity coverage is on file and adequate; many state laws require this.

## Umbrella

Umbrella sits above the underlying general liability and auto policies. Typical HOA umbrella runs $1-5 million and costs $500-$2,500 per year. Larger communities with pools, multi-story buildings, or significant amenities should carry $5 million or more.

Umbrella does not extend over D&O unless the policy specifically says so. If the underlying D&O is $1 million and a claim runs $2 million, the second million comes out of the association''s own funds unless the umbrella is endorsed to extend over D&O.

## Workers compensation, cyber, flood

Workers compensation is required by state law any time the association has W-2 employees. Most states do not require it for independent contractors, but the line between employee and contractor is often unclear; carriers prefer over-coverage to under-coverage.

Cyber liability is increasingly relevant. HOAs that store homeowner contact information, payment data, or vehicle registration data have data breach exposure. A small cyber policy ($500K limit) typically runs $1,000-$2,500/yr.

Flood coverage is separate from property and almost always required if the buildings sit in a FEMA-designated Special Flood Hazard Area. Lender requirements often drive this — even one mortgaged unit can require the association to carry flood insurance on the entire building.

## The board''s annual review

Every board should review the full insurance program annually with the carrier or broker. The agenda: replacement cost adequacy, fidelity sized to current assessments and reserves, D&O extension language, named insureds, exclusions, and any new amenities or activities the existing policies may not cover. Fifteen minutes of review at one board meeting per year is the difference between a defensible insurance program and a coverage gap that surfaces during the largest claim the association has ever filed.
