## Why umbrella is the most misunderstood HOA policy

Most board members can describe what their property insurance covers. Many can describe D&O. Almost no one understands the umbrella. That gap matters because the umbrella is the layer that pays when a claim exceeds the primary policy — exactly when the association most needs the coverage.

The misunderstanding usually centers on three points: when umbrella triggers, what limits it requires underneath, and whether it covers governance (D&O) claims. Each of these is a board-decision point that shapes whether the association is genuinely protected against catastrophic losses.

## When umbrella actually pays

Umbrella pays only after the underlying primary policy is exhausted on a covered loss. If a slip-and-fall produces a $1.5 million verdict and the GL policy carries $1 million, the umbrella pays the second $500,000 — assuming the GL policy actually paid out its limit. If the GL policy denies coverage entirely (because the claim was excluded, or because of a coverage dispute), the umbrella may also deny. The umbrella follows form to the underlying for most claims.

The exception: true umbrella policies, as opposed to excess policies, can sometimes pay for claims the underlying excluded, subject to a self-insured retention (typically $10,000-$25,000) the association pays out of pocket first. The "drop-down" coverage is one of the main reasons to pay extra for true umbrella rather than excess.

## The underlying-limits trap

This is the largest avoidable error in HOA insurance programs. The umbrella declarations page includes a "Schedule of Underlying Insurance" listing the primary limits the umbrella requires. If those limits are not maintained — for instance, the GL is renewed at a lower limit, or the auto coverage is not added when a new association vehicle is purchased — the umbrella will not drop down. The gap between the actual underlying limit and the required underlying limit is uninsured.

Example: umbrella schedule requires $1 million GL. The association renews GL at $500,000 to save premium. A claim runs $1.5 million. The GL pays $500,000. The umbrella does not pay anything between $500,000 and $1 million because the umbrella attaches at $1 million per the schedule. The umbrella pays $500,000 for the layer above $1 million. The association is liable for the $500,000 gap.

The fix: at every renewal, compare the actual primary limits to the umbrella schedule. If they have drifted, either restore the primary limits or amend the umbrella schedule.

## Sizing umbrella by community and amenity profile

There is no single right number, but practical guidance:

- 50-unit HOA, no pool, no playground: $1-2 million umbrella over $1 million GL.
- 50-200 units with pool: $2-5 million umbrella, $1 million GL.
- 200-500 units with pool, fitness, playground: $5-10 million umbrella, $1-2 million GL.
- 500+ or master-planned with multiple pools, gates, paid staff: $10-25 million umbrella.

These are starting points. Communities with unusual exposures — water frontage, golf course, equestrian facilities, on-site daycare — need bespoke analysis. The premium difference between $2 million and $5 million is often only $300-$600 per year; sizing up is usually cheap insurance against tail-risk verdicts.

## Umbrella over D&O: read the policy

Standard HOA umbrella sits over GL and auto. D&O may or may not be in the schedule. The practical impact: a $2.5 million discrimination verdict against the association, with $1 million in D&O and no umbrella over D&O, leaves $1.5 million on the association''s books. A special assessment of that size to fund the verdict is the kind of event that ends board members'' tenures and triggers homeowner lawsuits against the directors personally.

If the community has any meaningful enforcement activity, any history of homeowner disputes, or any paid staff, the umbrella should extend over D&O. The extension typically adds 15-30% to the umbrella premium. The marginal cost is small relative to the catastrophic-claim protection.

## Exclusions worth checking before binding

Umbrella policies frequently exclude:

- **Pollution**, except sudden-and-accidental discharge.
- **Professional services**, including any board decision that could be reframed as professional advice.
- **Employment practices**, often by reference to whether EPLI is on the underlying.
- **Abuse and molestation**, increasingly excluded after large claims at communities with youth amenities.
- **Construction defect**, especially in newer communities still under developer warranty.

For each exclusion, the question is whether the community''s exposure profile creates a real risk. A community with a daycare partnership needs abuse coverage. A community in a watershed needs pollution coverage. A community with paid staff needs EPLI flow-through. The broker should produce a written exclusion comparison annually.

## Personal umbrella for individual directors

Beyond the association''s coverage, individual directors should carry a personal umbrella ($1-2 million) through their homeowner''s policy. The personal umbrella backs up the association''s D&O if the association''s policy denies, exhausts, or excludes a specific claim. Personal umbrella runs $200-$400 per year. Compared to the unbounded liability of an uncovered judgment, it is the cheapest line item in most household insurance programs.

The right approach is a layered defense: solid primary GL, D&O extended to all directors, association umbrella sized to community and amenity profile with a D&O extension, and personal umbrella as the last safety net. Boards that build this stack and review it annually with their broker rarely face uncovered claims. Boards that inherited a stack from previous boards and never review it are the ones that learn about coverage gaps mid-litigation.
