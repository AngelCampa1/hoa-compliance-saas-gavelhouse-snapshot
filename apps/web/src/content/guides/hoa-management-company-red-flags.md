## Why this matters more than any other vendor decision

The HOA management company sits between your board and your money. They write checks, hold reserves, manage vendors, and interpret governing documents. A bad management company does not just produce slow service — it produces fraud risk, compliance failures, and personal liability exposure for board members.

The pattern in nearly every HOA embezzlement case is the same: a management company with exclusive bank-account access, no dual-signature requirement, infrequent reporting, and a board that trusted the relationship instead of the controls. The losses run from tens of thousands to millions of dollars. They are usually unrecoverable because the money is gone, the bond is too small, and the manager has filed for bankruptcy.

We built Gavelhouse in part because the right answer for many small communities is not switching management companies — it is self-managing with software that enforces the controls a bad manager would skip. But whether you self-manage or stay with a manager, you have to know what the red flags look like.

## The five red flags that should end the relationship

### 1. Sole signing authority on association bank accounts

If your management company is the only signer on the operating account and the reserve account, you have a fraud risk that requires no further evidence. The treasurer must be a signer. Above a threshold — commonly $500 or $1,000 — two signatures should be required.

When a manager resists adding a board signer, the resistance is the diagnosis.

### 2. Commingled funds across multiple HOAs

Your association's operating funds belong in an account titled to your association. Same for reserves. If your manager holds your money in a single "trust account" alongside funds from other communities they manage, that is commingling.

State statutes generally prohibit commingling. Even where allowed in narrow forms, it creates traceability problems. If the management company is sued by another association, your money is in scope. If the management company fails, your money is in the bankruptcy estate.

Verify by requesting the actual account number. Compare it to account numbers shown for other communities the manager handles.

### 3. Missing or stale monthly reserve statements

You should receive a monthly statement showing the reserve account balance, the contributions made that month, any disbursements made that month, and the running funded percentage against the reserve study. If the manager produces a quarterly reserve summary instead — or worse, an annual one — you cannot detect a problem before it becomes catastrophic.

A manager that resists producing monthly reserve statements is signaling either incompetence or that the statements would reveal something unflattering.

### 4. No current COI and undersized fidelity bond

The management company should carry general liability insurance, errors and omissions insurance, and a fidelity or crime bond covering employee theft. The certificate of insurance should name your association as additionally insured.

Fannie Mae condo guidelines require fidelity coverage of at least three months of assessments plus reserve balances. Many state statutes set minimums as well. The trap is that bonds get sized at contract signing and never updated. A bond that was correct in 2019 is now badly undersized for a community whose reserves have grown.

Require an updated COI annually and a bond sized to current reserves.

### 5. Contract language that blocks audit access

Some management contracts include language requiring "30 days notice" for record requests, charging hourly fees for record production, or limiting which records the board can review. This language is generally unenforceable — state statutes give the association full access — but its presence in the contract is a red flag about how the manager views the relationship.

A legitimate manager produces records on request without friction.

## The supporting red flags

These are not termination triggers on their own, but they accumulate.

### Slow maintenance response

Track every ticket for 60 days. Industry norms are 24 hours for non-emergencies and 2-4 hours for emergencies. Patterns of consistent miss are evidence that the manager is over-capacity or under-staffed.

### Late or missing monthly financials

Monthly financial statements should arrive within 15-20 days of month-end. Statements that arrive 45+ days late or that skip months entirely make board oversight impossible. You cannot exercise fiduciary duty over financials you have not seen.

### No vendor list or vendor contacts

Ask for a list of every vendor providing services to your community, with contact information. A manager that cannot or will not produce this list is hiding something — usually preferred vendor relationships, kickbacks, or the simple fact that vendors only know the manager and would not respond to the board directly.

### Board portal that blocks document access

Some manager-controlled portals limit which documents board members can download. Governing documents, financials, contracts, and vendor agreements should all be downloadable by board members at will. A locked-down portal is a control mechanism, not a service feature.

### Refusal to participate in audit

The annual audit (or review or compilation, depending on size) requires the manager's cooperation — producing bank statements, reconciliations, vendor invoices, and supporting documentation. A manager that drags out audit cooperation is hoping the audit gets postponed or skipped. That is an enormous red flag.

## Contract terms to require

If you are negotiating a new management contract — or renegotiating at renewal — these clauses are non-negotiable.

### Banking controls

- Association is the named account holder on every account.
- Treasurer is a signer on every account.
- Dual signatures required above $500 or $1,000.
- Manager has view access and payment-initiation access only, not unilateral signing.
- Online banking credentials owned by the association, shared with the manager under written terms.

### Reporting cadence

- Monthly financial statements delivered within 20 days of month-end.
- Monthly reserve fund statement separate from operating statement.
- Annual budget draft delivered 90 days before fiscal year-end.
- Annual audit cooperation included in base fee, not billable.

### Insurance and bonding

- COI naming association as additionally insured, renewed annually.
- Fidelity or crime bond sized to three months of assessments plus current reserve balances, recalculated annually.
- E&O policy with minimum $1M coverage.

### Termination terms

- 60-90 days written notice for either party.
- No termination fee for cause (defined as material breach uncured after 30 days written notice).
- Specified handoff deliverables: complete records, vendor list with contacts, bank reconciliations through termination date, software access transition.
- Ownership clarification: all association records, regardless of where stored, are association property and must be transferred at termination.

### Records access

- Board has unrestricted access to all association records, financial and operational.
- Records produced within 5 business days of written request.
- No fee for normal records requests.

## How to exit a bad contract

Step 1: Read the termination clause. Note the notice period and any fees.

Step 2: Document the breach. If you are terminating for cause, you need a written record. Late reports, missed maintenance, refused records, missing COI — log everything in writing with dates.

Step 3: Send formal notice of breach with a cure period. Most contracts require 30 days written notice and an opportunity to cure before termination for cause is valid. Even if the contract does not require it, this protects you legally.

Step 4: Identify the replacement before sending termination notice. Either another management company or a self-managed setup. Have the new banking, software, and vendor relationships planned.

Step 5: On termination day, take possession of records, change bank account access, change vendor contact information, and verify the final reconciliation.
