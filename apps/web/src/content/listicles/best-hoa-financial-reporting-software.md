## Why HOA Financial Reporting Has a Single Hard Requirement

Most HOA financial reporting software claims fund-level reporting. The actual question is whether the software preserves fund separation from the source data through to the printed page, or whether it collapses funds at the report layer and lets the treasurer reformat manually.

The AICPA Audit Guide for Common Interest Realty Associations is unambiguous on this point: HOA financials should present each fund separately, with consolidated totals shown only as supplementary. State audits and CPA reviews apply the same standard. Software that produces a balance sheet collapsed across funds — with class tracking columns or sub-account groupings as a workaround — is software that requires manual reformatting before the report is usable.

For a self-managed volunteer board, that manual reformatting is the practical reason annual financial preparation takes weeks. For a CPA reviewing the books, it is the practical reason the engagement runs over budget. For a state auditor, it is the practical reason commingling claims surface even when the underlying fund management is correct.

The right software does this natively. Balance sheet by fund as the default, not a special export. Statement of revenues and expenses by fund. Owner statements that pull from the same fund-separated ledger. The board or a reserve specialist performs the reserve adequacy review using the accurate, separated records the software produces.

## What to Look For in HOA Financial Reporting Software

**Native balance sheet by fund.** Default presentation, not a workaround. Operating and reserve as separate columns, not separate filtered reports.

**Separated reserve fund records.** Operating and reserve funds separated at the ledger level with a full audit log. The board or a reserve specialist performs the annual adequacy review against study targets using the separated records the software supplies.

**Statement of revenues and expenses by fund.** Income statement preserving fund separation, with inter-fund transfers as explicit line items.

**Owner statements.** Per-owner transaction history and balance, drawn from the same ledger that produces the financial statements.

**Audit-supplementary information.** Notes on related-party transactions, inter-fund transfers, and reserve fund components.

The trade-off: Gavelhouse does not yet offer the report customization depth of Vantaca or CINC. For most self-managed boards, the standard reports cover what CPAs and state auditors expect. For management companies with bespoke reporting needs, the customization gap may matter.

### 2. MoneyMinder — Best for Treasurer-Only Fund Reporting

MoneyMinder is genuine fund accounting at ~$159/year. The balance sheet by fund is correct; budget vs. actual reporting is clean; the fund equity statement reconciles. For a treasurer who handles all financial reporting and accepts that homeowner statements need to come from somewhere else, MoneyMinder fits.

The gaps: limited audit-supplementary information depth, no owner-facing statements, no reserve adequacy reporting against external study data.

### 3. PayHOA — Best for Homeowner-Facing Reports

PayHOA''s strength is homeowner-facing reporting: per-owner balance, payment history, document access, statement download. For a board where owner-facing financial communication is the primary need, PayHOA covers the workflow.

The gap is the fund-level reporting depth. Operating and reserve funds share a single ledger, which means the standard balance sheet does not present funds separately. Boards using PayHOA typically produce annual fund reports through a separate accounting tool or CPA.
