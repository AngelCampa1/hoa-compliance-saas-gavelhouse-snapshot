## Why Reserve Fund Compliance Matters for Your Board

If you're a volunteer HOA board member - treasurer, president, or any role that votes on budgets - reserve fund compliance is the single highest-liability area you manage. It's also the one most likely to be handled poorly, because reserve accounting is genuinely confusing and the rules vary by state.

Here's the short version: your HOA collects money from homeowners. Some of that money is for daily operations (landscaping, insurance, management fees). The rest is supposed to go into a reserve fund for future capital expenditures - roof replacements, parking lot resurfacing, elevator modernization, pool replastering. State law in many jurisdictions requires that you have a professional reserve study to determine how much money should be in that fund, and some states mandate minimum funding levels.

When the reserve fund is underfunded and a major repair comes due, the board has to levy a special assessment. Homeowners get hit with unexpected bills of $5,000, $10,000, or more. That's when lawsuits happen. And in states with mandatory reserve study or funding laws, board members who failed to commission a required study can face personal liability.

This checklist covers the states with explicit mandatory reserve study or funding requirements, what compliance looks like in each, and the steps to get compliant if your board has fallen behind.

## States With Mandatory Reserve Study or Funding Laws

Not every state requires HOAs to conduct reserve studies. Many leave it to the discretion of the board or the governing documents (CC&Rs). But the following states have statutory requirements that apply to some or all common-interest communities.

**California** - Civil Code Section 5550 requires associations with major components to conduct a reserve study at least every three years. The study must include a physical analysis and a financial analysis. The board must distribute an annual reserve funding disclosure (Form Pro 100) to all members. California also requires a 30-year funding plan. Non-compliance can expose board members to breach of fiduciary duty claims.

**Florida** - The Surfside condo collapse in 2021 led to significant legislative changes. SB 4-D (2022) created Structural Integrity Reserve Studies (SIRS) for condominium and co-op associations in buildings of three or more habitable stories. SIRS must cover eight mandatory components (roof, structure, fireproofing, plumbing, electrical, waterproofing, windows, and items costing more than $25,000). For budgets adopted after December 31, 2024, associations subject to SIRS cannot vote to waive or reduce reserves for those structural components. HOAs governed by Chapter 720 are not subject to SIRS requirements - this distinction is critical.

**Nevada** - NRS 116.31152 requires associations to conduct a reserve study at least once every five years. The study must be performed by a reserve study specialist. The board must fund reserves based on the study's recommendations or explain in writing to homeowners why it's deviating.

**Virginia** - The Virginia Property Owners' Association Act (POAA) requires associations to maintain adequate reserves. While the statute doesn't specify a study frequency, the Virginia Common Interest Community Board provides guidance that a professional study should be updated every five years.

**Hawaii** - HRS 514B-148 requires condominium associations to prepare a reserve study and maintain a reserve fund. The study must be reviewed by the board annually and updated as needed. Associations must disclose reserve funding status to prospective buyers.

**Oregon** - ORS 94.595 requires planned community associations to conduct a reserve study. The initial study must be completed within the first year of the association's operation, with updates at least every three years for the physical analysis and annually for the financial analysis.

**Washington** - RCW 64.34.380 (condominiums) and RCW 64.90.545 (common interest communities under WUCIOA) require reserve studies. Washington specifies that the study must include a physical analysis, an inventory of components, and a financial analysis showing funding adequacy.

**Utah** - SB 57 (2023) introduced requirements for reserve studies in condominium and community associations. The law phases in requirements based on community size, with larger communities required to comply first.

**New Jersey** - S2760 (signed January 8, 2024) requires all condos, co-ops, and planned developments with $25,000 or more in common area capital assets to complete professional reserve studies every five years, prepared by a Reserve Specialist, licensed PE, or architect following CAI National Standards. S3992 (signed August 21, 2025) established baseline funding as the required standard - a 30-year plan that never projects a balance below $0. Existing associations may temporarily fund at 85% of baseline for up to five years with mandatory bold-font disclosure. After five years, full compliance is required. Applies to condos, co-ops, and large HOAs with 100 or more units.

**Delaware** - The Uniform Common Interest Ownership Act (UCIOA) adoption in Delaware requires associations to maintain reserve funds. Specific study requirements depend on the association's governing documents and the size of the community.

**Connecticut** - Common Interest Ownership Act (CIOA) requires associations to maintain adequate reserves. Connecticut is notable for requiring disclosure of reserve funding levels during resale.

**Illinois** - The Condominium Property Act requires condo associations to maintain reserve funds. Amendments in recent years have strengthened disclosure requirements and pushed boards toward professional reserve studies.

## How to Tell If Your Board Is Compliant

Compliance isn't just about having a reserve study on file. Run through this checklist for your state:

**Step 1: Locate your most recent reserve study.**
If you can't find one, or if the most recent study is older than your state's required update interval, you're non-compliant. This is the most common failure - the study expired and nobody scheduled a new one.

**Step 2: Check who conducted the study.**
Some states require a "reserve study specialist" or similar credentialed professional. In Nevada, for example, the study must be performed by a specialist registered with the state. In New Jersey, S2760 requires a Reserve Specialist, licensed PE, or architect following CAI National Standards. A study conducted by a board member or an unqualified person may not satisfy the legal requirement even if it's thorough.

**Step 3: Compare current reserve balance to the study's recommended funding.**
The study will project how much money should be in the reserve fund at any given year. Pull your current reserve fund balance from your bank statements (not your operating account - the reserve fund must be in a separate account). If the balance is below the study's recommendation, note the percentage funded. Below 70% funded is considered underfunded by most industry standards.

**Step 4: Verify fund separation.**
This is where many self-managed boards fail. Operating funds and reserve funds must be in separate bank accounts. Commingling these funds - even temporarily - is a violation in most states and can create personal liability for board members who approved the transfers. QuickBooks and similar general accounting tools don't enforce this separation, which is a major risk for boards using them.

**Step 5: Review your annual disclosure.**
California, Hawaii, Washington, Connecticut, and several other states require annual reserve fund disclosures to homeowners. Check whether your board has been distributing these. If not, start immediately - the disclosure obligation exists even if the underlying numbers aren't great.

**Step 6: Document board votes on reserve funding levels.**
When the board votes to accept the reserve study's recommendations (or deviate from them), that vote must be in the meeting minutes. If the board chose to fund at a lower level than recommended, the minutes should explain why. This documentation protects individual board members if a lawsuit arises later.

## Common Violations and How to Fix Them

**Expired reserve study.** This is the most common issue and the easiest to fix. Contact a reserve study firm and commission an update. Expect to pay $3,000-$8,000 depending on community size and complexity. The study typically takes 4-8 weeks to complete.

**Commingled funds.** If your operating and reserve funds are in the same bank account, open a new account immediately and transfer the reserve balance. Going forward, all reserve contributions should deposit directly into the separate account. Document the separation in board minutes.

**No annual disclosure.** Draft a reserve fund disclosure statement based on your state's requirements (or your most recent reserve study) and distribute it to all homeowners. Going forward, include this in your annual budget distribution.

**Below-minimum funding.** If your reserve fund is below the level recommended by the study, the board has a few options:

- Increase monthly reserve contributions gradually (most common approach)
- Levy a special assessment to bring the fund up to the recommended level (unpopular but sometimes necessary)
- Accept the underfunding and document the board's reasoning (legally permissible in some states, but increases liability exposure)

**No reserve study at all.** If your community has never had a reserve study and is in a state that requires one, this is a priority. You may also want to consult with an HOA attorney to understand your exposure.

## When to Hire a Reserve Study Firm vs. DIY

Reserve studies involve two components: a physical analysis (inspecting components and estimating remaining useful life) and a financial analysis (calculating required funding based on replacement costs and timelines).

**Hire a professional firm when:**

- Your state requires a credentialed reserve study specialist
- Your community has complex components (elevators, pools, large-scale HVAC systems, underground utilities)
- Your community has more than 50 units
- The study will be used for lending or insurance purposes
- You're facing a special assessment and need defensible numbers

**A board member can handle the financial update when:**

- Your state doesn't require a credentialed specialist
- You already have a recent professional physical analysis and just need to update the financial projections
- Your community is small (under 20 units) with simple components
- You have accounting or financial analysis experience

Even in cases where a DIY approach is permissible, the professional study provides legal cover. If a homeowner challenges the board's funding decisions, a study by an independent professional is much stronger evidence of due diligence than a spreadsheet built by the treasurer.

## Reserve Study Firm Selection

If you're hiring a firm, here's what to look for:

- **Credentials:** CAI (Community Associations Institute) has a Reserve Specialist (RS) designation. APRA (Association of Professional Reserve Analysts) certifies Professional Reserve Analysts (PRA). Not all states require these credentials, but they indicate the firm follows industry standards.
- **Experience in your state:** State-specific requirements matter. A firm that primarily works in California may not know Oregon's specific update cycle requirements.
- **Site visit included:** A reserve study without a physical inspection is just a financial projection. Make sure the proposal includes an on-site visit to assess component conditions.
- **Clear deliverables:** The final report should include a component inventory, condition assessment, remaining useful life estimates, replacement cost estimates, and a 30-year funding plan.
- **References from similar communities:** A firm that specializes in high-rise condos may not be the best fit for a 30-unit townhome community.

Expect to pay $3,000-$5,000 for communities under 100 units and $5,000-$10,000+ for larger or more complex communities. Updates to an existing study (no new physical analysis) typically cost less.

## Timeline for Getting Compliant

If your board is starting from scratch, here's a realistic timeline:

**Week 1-2:** Board meeting to acknowledge the compliance gap and vote to commission a reserve study. Document this in minutes.

**Week 2-4:** Solicit proposals from 2-3 reserve study firms. Review proposals and select a firm.

**Week 4-6:** Reserve study firm conducts site visit and begins analysis.

**Week 8-12:** Draft study delivered. Board reviews and asks questions.

**Week 12-14:** Final study delivered. Board votes to accept the study and adopt a funding plan.

**Week 14-16:** Update budget to reflect new reserve contribution levels. Distribute reserve fund disclosure to homeowners.

**Ongoing:** Track reserve fund balance monthly. Review the study annually (even if your state only requires updates every 3-5 years). Schedule the next professional update before the current study expires.

## Software Considerations for Reserve Fund Tracking

General accounting tools like QuickBooks are popular with self-managed HOAs, but they have a fundamental problem for reserve compliance: they don't enforce the separation between operating and reserve funds. You can create separate accounts in QuickBooks, but nothing stops someone from transferring between them or coding an expense to the wrong fund.

Purpose-built HOA software addresses this by treating operating and reserve funds as distinct ledgers with controlled transfers. When your accountant or auditor reviews the books, the separation is built into the system rather than relying on someone following a manual process.

Key software features for reserve compliance:

- Separate operating and reserve fund ledgers with audit trails
- Automated reserve contribution tracking from monthly dues
- Reserve study integration (import component data and funding projections)
- Disclosure report generation (formatted for your state's requirements)
- Board member access controls (treasurer can view/report, but transfers require board approval)

If your community is using spreadsheets or general accounting software for reserve tracking, the risk isn't that the math will be wrong. It's that the audit trail won't hold up when someone questions where the money went. A system designed for HOA reserve accounting generates that trail automatically.

## What Happens When You're Not Compliant

The consequences of reserve fund non-compliance depend on your state, but they generally fall into three categories:

**Regulatory penalties.** Some states (notably Florida, post-Surfside reforms) have established enforcement mechanisms with fines for associations that fail to meet reserve study and funding requirements. Florida's DBPR can impose fines up to $5,000 per violation, with website posting violations at $1,000 per day.

**Homeowner lawsuits.** When a special assessment hits because the reserve fund is empty, homeowners often sue the board. The first thing their attorney will check is whether the board followed state law regarding reserve studies. If the board didn't commission a required study, that's evidence of breach of fiduciary duty.

**Personal liability for board members.** In most states, volunteer board members are protected by some form of business judgment rule - you can't be sued for making a bad decision if you made it in good faith with reasonable information. But failing to follow a statutory requirement (like conducting a required reserve study) can pierce that protection. You made a bad decision by doing nothing.

The single best protection for a volunteer board member is documentation: a current reserve study, board minutes showing you discussed and voted on funding levels, and separate bank accounts for operating and reserve funds. Software that generates this documentation automatically is worth the cost for the liability protection alone.
