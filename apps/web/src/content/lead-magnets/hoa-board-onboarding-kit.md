## Why HOA board transitions are the highest-risk moment for your association

In any other governance context, a leadership transition involves a structured handoff - briefings, documentation, a transition period where outgoing and incoming leadership overlap. In most self-managed HOAs, the board transition happens at an annual meeting with a vote count, a round of handshakes, and an exchange of login credentials and file folders.

That gap between what transitions require and what they actually include is where associations get into trouble. A newly elected treasurer who cannot find the reserve study does not know their reserve compliance status. An incoming president who cannot locate the insurance policies does not know whether coverage has lapsed. A new secretary who inherits a shared Google Drive folder with no context for what is current versus outdated cannot maintain the documentation trail the board depends on.

The risks are not hypothetical. Board members in most states have a personal fiduciary duty to the association. That duty includes knowing the financial and compliance status of the association you are now governing. "I didn't know" is not a complete defense when the problem was knowable - when the information existed but was never transferred or reviewed.

This onboarding kit is designed to get new board members to situational awareness as quickly as possible. Not competency in every aspect of HOA governance - that takes time - but a clear picture of what the association has, what it owes, what it needs to do, and what is at risk.

## Document access: what to locate first

The first priority for any new board member is document access. You cannot assess the association's situation without the underlying records.

**Governing documents (immediate priority)**

- CC&Rs (Declaration of Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions) - the foundational document that establishes the association's authority, homeowner obligations, and board powers
- Bylaws - the procedural rules for how the board operates (quorum requirements, voting procedures, officer election process)
- Rules and regulations - the board-adopted operational rules (architectural standards, parking rules, pet policies, noise policies)
- Any recorded amendments to the above

These documents should be recorded in the county land records and should be obtainable from the county recorder if the board's copy is missing. If there have been amendments and you are not sure which version is current, a title search or an HOA attorney can clarify.

**Financial records (immediate priority)**

- Current year budget
- Year-to-date financial statements (balance sheet and income/expense statement)
- Bank statements for operating and reserve accounts (at least 12 months)
- The most recent reserve study
- Outstanding invoices and vendor contracts
- Delinquency report showing all homeowners with unpaid balances

**Compliance and legal records**

- Insurance policies - property, liability, directors and officers (D&O), fidelity/crime
- Any pending litigation or legal correspondence
- Recent correspondence with state regulatory agencies (if applicable)
- Meeting minutes from the past 12 months

**Vendor and contact records**

- List of all vendors and contractors with contact information and contract status
- HOA attorney contact information
- Accountant or CPA contact information
- Insurance broker contact information
- Reserve study firm contact information

## Financial review: what to assess in the first 30 days

Once you have access to the financial records, the first step is a structured review. You do not need to be a CPA to do this - you need to know what questions to ask and what answers should concern you.

**Bank accounts and fund separation**

Confirm that the association maintains separate bank accounts for the operating fund and the reserve fund. If both funds are in the same account, that is a compliance issue that needs to be addressed immediately in most states. The reserve balance should match roughly what the reserve study's funding plan projects for this point in the funding schedule.

If the operating and reserve accounts are combined, or if the reserve account balance is dramatically lower than the reserve study's recommended balance, document what you find and bring it to the full board at the next meeting. You are not responsible for what happened before your term, but you are responsible for what you do now that you know about it.

**Reserve fund percent-funded status**

Pull the most recent reserve study and find the recommended reserve balance for the current year. Divide the actual reserve balance by that recommended balance and multiply by 100. That is your percent-funded ratio.

- Above 70%: Generally adequate. Note it and move on.
- 30-70%: Moderate underfunding. The board should have a plan to address this over time. If there is no plan, create one.
- Below 30%: Significant underfunding. This is a high-priority issue. A major capital expenditure at this funding level almost certainly requires a special assessment. Board members should understand this risk and document that they are aware of it.

The [reserve compliance checklist](/free/reserve-compliance-checklist/) provides state-specific requirements for reserve studies and funding levels. The [HOA fund accounting guide](/resources/guides/hoa-fund-accounting-guide/) covers the mechanics of how reserve funds should be maintained.

**Delinquency review**

How many homeowners are delinquent? What is the total dollar amount outstanding? How long have those accounts been delinquent? Is there a documented collections policy, and has it been applied consistently?

High delinquency rates directly affect the association's ability to fund its budget. If significant amounts are outstanding, the board needs to understand whether collection efforts are underway and what the realistic recovery timeline looks like.

**Upcoming payables and commitments**

What vendor contracts are renewing soon? What insurance renewals are coming up? Are there any capital projects contracted that have not yet been billed? Surprises in the operating budget are much less painful when they are identified weeks in advance rather than the day an invoice arrives.

## Upcoming compliance deadlines

Compliance deadlines are where self-managed boards most commonly fall behind. Without a professional manager tracking state-specific requirements, deadlines get missed until someone asks about them.

Run through this checklist for your community:

**Reserve study currency.** When was the last reserve study completed? When is the next update required under your state's law? If the study is expired or expiring within 12 months, commission an update now.

**Annual meeting notice.** When is the next annual meeting? What is the required notice period? Has notice been sent, and was it sent within the required window?

**Budget distribution.** Has the proposed annual budget been distributed to homeowners within the required time frame before the board vote to adopt it?

**Insurance renewals.** When do property, liability, D&O, and fidelity policies renew? Are there any gaps in coverage the board should address?

**State-specific disclosures.** Some states require annual reserve fund disclosures to homeowners. Has your board been distributing these? If not, start immediately.

**Board election procedures.** Does your governing document require specific procedures for board elections? Are they being followed?

## Establishing your professional contacts

Every HOA board should have established relationships with at least three outside professionals:

**HOA attorney.** For legal questions about governing document interpretation, enforcement procedures, delinquency and lien filings, and disputes with homeowners. Not every question needs a lawyer, but having one you can call is essential.

**CPA or accountant with HOA experience.** For year-end financial review, tax filings (HOAs typically file Form 1120-H), and guidance on accounting questions that go beyond what a volunteer treasurer can resolve.

**Insurance broker specializing in community associations.** Community association insurance has specific coverage requirements (loss assessment coverage, directors and officers liability, fidelity/crime coverage) that general commercial brokers may not fully understand.

The contact sheet template in the downloadable kit includes fields for each of these professionals plus vendors (reserve study firm, management transition resources, and contractors).

## Your 30/60/90-day action plan

**Days 1-30: Situational awareness**

- Complete the document access checklist
- Complete the financial review checklist
- Identify any immediate compliance gaps
- Establish professional contacts
- Attend first board meeting with notes on findings

**Days 31-60: Address identified gaps**

- Commission any overdue reserve study updates
- Implement immediate correction for any fund separation issues
- Establish a collections policy if one does not exist or is inconsistently applied
- Review insurance coverage adequacy
- Set up compliance calendar for upcoming deadlines

**Days 61-90: System and process improvement**

- Confirm that all records are stored in a shared system accessible to all board members (not individual personal accounts)
- Review vendor contracts for renewal timing and pricing
- Prepare for budget process if budget year is approaching
- Confirm new board member access to all necessary accounts and records
