  - q: Can a board member conduct the reserve study instead of hiring a firm?
    a: >-
      In states without a credentialing requirement, a financially sophisticated
      board member can perform the financial analysis portion - updating
      contribution rates and projecting balances. However, the physical analysis
      (inspecting components, assessing condition, estimating remaining useful
      life) requires someone qualified to evaluate structural, mechanical, and
      civil systems. Using an unqualified person for the physical analysis
      undermines the study's defensibility and may not satisfy state law or
      lender requirements.
  - q: How much does a reserve study typically cost?
    a: >-
      For communities under 100 units with standard components, expect $3,000 to
      $5,000 for a full study including site visit. Communities with 100 to 300
      units typically range from $5,000 to $8,000. Larger or structurally
      complex communities - high-rises, communities with pools, elevators, or
      extensive underground utilities - can reach $10,000 to $15,000 or more.
      Update studies (financial analysis only, no new physical inspection) cost
      less, typically 30 to 50 percent of the full study price.
  - q: How often does a reserve study need to be updated?
    a: >-
      State law varies. California requires a physical analysis at least every
      three years. Nevada and New Jersey require updates every five years.
      Oregon requires a financial analysis update annually and a physical
      analysis update every three years. Even in states without a mandated
      interval, most industry guidance recommends a full update every three to
      five years and an annual financial review in between. The RFP should ask
      firms to recommend an update schedule as part of their proposal.
  - q: >-
      What happens if the reserve study reveals the fund is severely
      underfunded?
    a: >-
      The board has three paths - increase monthly reserve contributions
      gradually, levy a special assessment to close the gap immediately, or
      document a board decision to accept temporary underfunding with a written
      explanation in the meeting minutes. The third option is legally
      permissible in many states but increases personal liability exposure for
      board members. The reserve study itself is not the liability risk --
      operating without one, or ignoring its findings without documentation, is.

## Why a Formal RFP Process Protects Your Board

When we built Gavelhouse, one of the first things we learned from talking to self-managed HOA boards was that most reserve study procurement is informal. A board member calls two firms, gets verbal quotes, and picks the one that sounded most professional on the phone. No documentation. No comparable scope. No record of what the board considered.

That informality creates two problems. First, you can't compare bids that are priced on different scopes - one firm includes a site visit and 30-year funding plan, another quotes a desktop review with a 10-year projection. Second, if a homeowner later challenges the board's selection ("why did you hire this firm instead of the cheaper one?"), there's no documentation showing the board followed a reasonable process.

A formal RFP solves both problems. It forces all firms to respond to the same scope, the same deliverable requirements, and the same credential standards. The written proposals become part of your board records, and the board's written selection rationale demonstrates due diligence.

This template is designed for volunteer board members who may have never run a procurement process. Use it as-is or modify it for your community's specific circumstances.

---

## Section 1 - Community Description

Start the RFP with a factual description of your community so firms can scope the work accurately.

**Items to include:**

- Association name and legal structure (HOA, condo association, co-op)
- Total number of units
- Year the community was built (or phases, if built over multiple years)
- Rough number of major components you are aware of (roofs, parking lots, pool, elevators, HVAC systems, etc.)
- Date and provider of the most recent reserve study, if one exists
- Whether you need a full study (physical analysis + financial analysis) or an update to an existing study
- State of formation (so the firm knows which statutory requirements apply)

Be specific about community age and component complexity. A 1980s community with original roofs and aging pool equipment requires different expertise than a newly constructed community with a single reserve-study trigger from its developer-turnover budget.

---

## Section 2 - Required Qualifications

This is where most informal bids fall short. Firms know they can sell a cheaper study if no one specifies credentials. The RFP should state required credentials as non-negotiable.

**Minimum credential requirements to specify:**

- At least one lead analyst with a Reserve Specialist (RS) credential from CAI or a Professional Reserve Analyst (PRA) credential from APRA
- Demonstrated experience completing reserve studies in your state (state-specific statutory knowledge matters)
- Errors and omissions insurance with minimum coverage limits (ask your HOA attorney what limit is appropriate for your community size)
- References from at least two comparable communities (similar unit count, building type, or component complexity) completed within the past three years

**For communities in states with stricter requirements:**

Nevada's NRS 116.31152 requires the study be performed by a reserve study specialist. New Jersey's S2760 requires a Reserve Specialist (RS), licensed PE, or architect following CAI National Standards. Florida's SIRS requirements (post-Surfside) have specific component and engineering standards for buildings of three or more habitable stories.

If your state has a credentialing requirement, state it explicitly in the RFP and ask each firm to confirm in writing that their proposed analyst satisfies the statute.

---

## Section 3 - Scope of Work

Define exactly what you are buying. The deliverables section is where scope disagreements arise after award.

**Physical analysis (required for full studies):**

- On-site inspection of all common-area components by a credentialed analyst
- Condition assessment for each component (scale your state uses, or the CAI RS standard)
- Remaining useful life estimate for each component
- Replacement cost estimate for each component based on current market pricing

**Component inventory:**

- Complete list of all major common-area components subject to deterioration
- Categorization by type (structural, mechanical, civil, amenity)
- Indication of which components are included in reserve funding versus maintenance budget

**Financial analysis:**

- Current reserve fund balance input and projected balance under the recommended funding plan
- Annual contribution schedule showing required contributions for each year of the plan
- 30-year funding projection showing projected reserve fund balance at the end of each year
- Percent funded calculation at study date and at key milestones in the projection

**Report deliverables:**

- Draft report delivered for board review before finalization
- Final report in PDF format suitable for distribution to homeowners
- Electronic data file (Excel or CSV) with component inventory and financial model, so the board can import data into HOA management software
- Recommended update schedule (next full physical analysis date, next financial update date)

---

## Section 4 - Timeline and Process

Specify the process and ask firms to commit to a timeline.

**Typical timeline expectations:**

- Site visit scheduled within four weeks of contract execution
- Draft report delivered within six weeks of site visit
- Board review period of two weeks
- Final report delivered within two weeks of board comments

Ask each firm to propose a realistic timeline given their current workload. A firm that promises a two-week turnaround on a complex community may be cutting corners on the physical analysis.

---

## Section 5 - Pricing

Ask for itemized pricing so you can compare bids precisely.

**Price breakout to request:**

- Site visit and physical analysis fee
- Component inventory and condition assessment fee
- Financial analysis and 30-year funding plan fee
- Report preparation and draft/final delivery fee
- Optional: annual financial update pricing (for future years)
- Optional: update study pricing (next full physical analysis cycle)

Total project cost and payment terms (deposit on contract execution, balance on final delivery is standard).

## Red Flags to Watch for in Bids

After you receive proposals, evaluate each one for these warning signs.

**No site visit included.** A desktop reserve study using prior reports or county assessor data without a physical inspection is not adequate for most communities and will not satisfy credentialing standards in states that require physical analysis. If a proposal does not include an on-site visit, it is not a compliant full reserve study.

**Credentials are vague.** A proposal that says "our team has extensive experience" without naming the credentialed analyst and listing specific designations is hiding something. Ask for the resume and credential verification of the person who will conduct the physical analysis.

**The component count seems low.** If a firm quotes your 150-unit condo community with 12 components when a comparable community would typically have 30 to 50, they are likely planning a superficial analysis. Ask for a preliminary component list as part of the proposal.

**The price is significantly below other bids.** Reserve studies are not commoditized. A price that is 40 to 50 percent below other comparable proposals usually means a shorter site visit, fewer components analyzed, or an analyst who is not credentialed. The reserve study is your primary liability protection - it is not the place to optimize for the lowest bid.

**No references from comparable communities.** A firm that specializes in large commercial properties or high-rise condos may not have relevant experience with a 40-unit townhome association and vice versa. Ask for references and call them.
