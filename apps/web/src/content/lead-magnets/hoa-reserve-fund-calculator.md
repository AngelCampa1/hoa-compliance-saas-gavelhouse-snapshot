## Why most HOA boards do not know their percent-funded ratio

Ask most HOA treasurers what their reserve fund balance is and they can tell you within a few hundred dollars - it is on the bank statement. Ask them what their percent-funded ratio is and the answer is usually a pause, followed by "I think we're okay" or "the reserve study said we were at X% but that was a few years ago."

The gap between knowing the balance and knowing the percent-funded ratio is significant. A reserve balance of $200,000 sounds like a lot. Whether it is adequate depends entirely on what your community's reserve obligations look like - how many components you have, how old they are, how much they cost to replace, and how much you should have set aside by now given those factors.

A community with $200,000 in reserves and $300,000 in fully funded obligation is at 67% - underfunded but manageable. A community with $200,000 in reserves and $700,000 in fully funded obligation is at 29% - high-risk, with a high probability of special assessments for the next several years.

The percent-funded ratio is the number that makes the balance meaningful. Calculating it requires a reserve study. Most self-managed HOA boards have a reserve study somewhere - it just has not been translated into a current percent-funded ratio because nobody has done the calculation since the study was delivered.

This calculator walks you through that calculation step by step.

## What you need before you calculate

You need two things:

**Your current reserve fund balance.** This is the balance in your reserve account (not your operating account - these must be separate). Use the most recent bank statement balance, or the reconciled balance from your accounting system if it is more current.

**Your most recent reserve study.** Specifically, you need the fully funded balance projection for the current year. Reserve studies include a funding plan table that shows, year by year, what the fully funded balance should be. Find the row for the current year and note the fully funded balance figure.

If you do not have a reserve study, or if your most recent study is more than 3-5 years old (the required update frequency varies by state), the percent-funded calculation you perform will not be accurate. An outdated study may use replacement cost estimates that are significantly below current construction costs. Commissioning an updated reserve study is the prerequisite to meaningful percent-funded tracking.

The [reserve compliance checklist](/free/reserve-compliance-checklist/) covers state-specific reserve study requirements including update frequencies. If you are in California, Nevada, Florida, Washington, New Jersey, or another state with statutory reserve study requirements, the checklist covers what your specific state mandates.

## The percent-funded formula

The calculation:

**Percent-Funded = (Current Reserve Balance / Fully Funded Balance) x 100**

Example:

- Current reserve balance: $175,000
- Fully funded balance from reserve study (current year): $250,000
- Percent-Funded = ($175,000 / $250,000) x 100 = **70%**

This is the core calculation. The complexity comes from what goes into the fully funded balance - and understanding that is what makes this number useful rather than just a figure on a spreadsheet.

## How the fully funded balance is calculated

The fully funded balance is the theoretical ideal balance at any point in time. It represents what the reserve fund would contain if every component had been contributing to it proportionally since new.

The calculation for each component:

**Component annual contribution = Replacement cost / Useful life**

**Accumulated depreciation = Annual contribution x Current age**

For a roof with a $120,000 replacement cost, a 30-year useful life, and 20 years of current age:

- Annual contribution = $120,000 / 30 = $4,000/year
- Accumulated depreciation = $4,000 x 20 = $80,000

This $80,000 represents what the reserve fund should have accumulated for this one component. Sum the accumulated depreciation across all components and you have the fully funded balance.

This is the calculation reserve study firms perform professionally. The worksheet in the downloadable calculator walks you through this component-by-component for communities that want to understand the mechanics - or for small communities that are trying to estimate their funding status before commissioning a full reserve study.

## What different funding levels mean in practice

**Below 30% - High risk**

At below 30% funded, the reserve fund is insufficient to cover most significant capital expenditures without a special assessment. If a roof, elevator, or parking structure needs replacement, the board will almost certainly need to levy a special assessment to supplement the reserve balance.

Special assessments are unpopular. They can also be significant - $5,000 to $20,000+ per unit for major capital projects in underfunded communities. Homeowners who purchased in the community without disclosure of the reserve funding situation have legal grounds to challenge the assessment in some states.

Board members in a 30%-funded community should document their awareness of the underfunding and their plan to address it. That documentation matters if a homeowner later claims the board was negligent.

**30-70% - Moderate risk**

At 30-70% funded, the community can probably handle near-term capital expenditures from reserves, but has meaningful exposure to special assessments for larger or unexpected expenses. The appropriate response is a defined plan to increase contributions over time.

The increase does not have to be dramatic. Moving from 40% funded to 70% funded over 10 years requires incrementally higher contributions each year. The calculator shows the annual contribution required to reach a target funding level by a target year.

**Above 70% - Healthy**

At 70% or above, the reserve fund is considered adequately funded by most industry standards. Special assessment risk is significantly lower. Lenders and prospective buyers generally view this favorably. This does not mean the board can reduce contributions - it means the current contribution level is working and should be maintained.

The California Davis-Stirling Act requires boards to disclose the reserve fund percent-funded status annually. The disclosure creates an official record of the board's awareness of the funding level and their plan (or lack of plan) to address any underfunding.

## Fannie Mae's 10% assessment rule

For condominium associations, Fannie Mae requires that at least 10% of the gross budget be allocated to reserves for the project to be eligible as warrantable. This means lenders can originate conventional mortgages for unit purchases.

**The calculation:**

10% rule check = (Annual reserve contribution / Total annual assessment income) x 100

If this percentage is below 10%, the project may be flagged as non-warrantable. In practice, lenders check this when a unit owner applies for a conventional mortgage. If the association fails the check, the borrower may be unable to obtain conventional financing - which can derail a sale.

The calculator includes a worksheet for this check. Note that the 10% rule is a threshold for Fannie Mae warrantability, not a target for adequate reserve funding. Most reserve studies recommend contribution rates significantly above 10% for adequately funded communities.

## Three funding plan strategies compared

Reserve study professionals typically present funding plans in three strategies. The calculator includes a comparison worksheet for all three.

**Minimum funding:** Maintain the aggregate reserve balance above $0 in every year of the funding plan. This is the lowest annual contribution requirement and the highest risk - the fund will be near $0 in some years, meaning any expense in those years requires a special assessment.

**Threshold funding:** Maintain the reserve balance above a board-defined floor (often $0 per individual component, or a percentage of the fully funded balance). Higher contributions than minimum funding, but still carries meaningful special assessment risk.

**Full funding:** Target the fully funded balance in each year of the funding plan. Highest annual contributions, lowest special assessment risk. For communities that have been underfunded, reaching full funding typically requires a "catch-up" period with elevated contributions before leveling off.

The right strategy depends on your community's current funding level, homeowner appetite for assessment increases, and the board's risk tolerance. The [reserve fund compliance guide](/resources/guides/hoa-reserve-fund-compliance-guide/) covers the trade-offs in more detail.
